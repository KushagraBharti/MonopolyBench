Before the Menu Changes:
Prospective Statements and Applied Actions of
LLM Agents in Twenty Monopoly Games
Independent draft for coauthor review∗
12 November 2026
Abstract
An economic action often changes what can be done next. Selling a color group’s buildings
makes its deeds mortgageable, and selling a deed can let a rival build. We ask whether LLM agents
that state such consequences before acting then act accordingly, using twenty complete four-player
Monopoly games played by four configured systems: GPT-6.1 Sol, Gemini 4 Argon, Claude Opus
5.5 and Grok 4.7. In 16 of 188 debts, payment required mortgages that only building sales would
release. Agents paid 14. Before the first action, the recorded text named the released mortgages,
or a budget including them, in 13 of these payments and raised them as an open question in the
fourteenth. In both bankruptcies the text totaled only the listed items. Across 920 negotiation
responses, recipients often cited a rival’s next construction when refusing. Opening swaps that
would complete only the proposer’s group were rejected more often than swaps completing no
group (95/116 versus 37/72), while cash bids showed no corresponding increase (42/70 versus
67/103). The swap gap keeps its sign within every proposer large enough to compare, but falls from
30.5 to 16.7 points without Grok-authored offers, and recipients give similar reasons under both
forms. Eight incidents with verified quotations show a refusal, pricing, bluffing, a factual correction,
a selective claim, a coordination appeal, a withdrawn assurance and a ten-action payment. The
records show statements that match the state and actions that follow statements. They do not show
that any choice was good, that recorded text reveals how a system computed, or how the underlying
models rank.
1
Introduction
Many economic decisions change which decisions come next. In Monopoly, selling the houses on a
color group makes its deeds mortgageable. Selling the last deed of a group to a rival lets that rival
build. Lending to a nearly bankrupt opponent keeps a blocker in play, and refusing to lend can deliver
the same deed to whoever is owed money. An agent that plays well has to say, at least implicitly, what
an action will make possible for itself and for others, and then act on it.
Language-model agents can be asked to write that reasoning down. In MonopolyBench each
response carries a public message and, under the standard prompt, a private note attached to the
applied action, and most providers also return a reasoning text. These records let us ask a question
that outcomes alone cannot answer. When an agent states, before acting, how an action will change its
own or another player’s options, does what it then does agree with what it said? This is narrower than
asking whether the agent reasons well, and the records can answer it.

=== page 2
We study twenty complete four-player games played by GPT-6.1 Sol, Gemini 4 Argon, Claude
Opus 5.5 and Grok 4.7 through one gateway. The corpus contains 6,989 decisions, 920 responses in 526
negotiation threads, and 188 debts that required a liquidation menu. Every offer is linked to the state
its recipient saw and to its response, and every debt to its first menu, applied actions and outcome.
We grade each result against a five-step evidence ladder. A statement can mention a consequence
for some player (stated). The consequence can match the state and rules (accurate). The applied
action can agree with it (consistent). The action can change when the relevant opportunity changes
(sensitive). And the behavior can improve an outcome against a reference (effective). Observational
records can reach the third step. They cannot reach the fourth or fifth without a comparison that holds
other things fixed, and this study runs no new games, replays or model queries.
Own options.
The clearest evidence concerns the agent’s own changing menu. In sixteen debts, cash,
the salvage value of every building and every mortgage listed on the first menu together fall short.
Payment then requires mortgages that appear only after buildings are sold. Agents pay fourteen of
these debts and declare bankruptcy in two. Before the first action, the recorded text names the released
mortgages, or a budget that includes them, in thirteen of the fourteen payments and raises them as an
open question in the fourteenth. In both bankruptcies the text adds up only the listed items and the
agent declares at once. The worked example is a ten-action liquidation whose announced remainder
of $14 is exactly what remains.
Rivals’ options.
Bargaining evidence is broader and weaker. Recipients often refuse or reprice offers
that would complete another player’s color group, and their notes usually cite what the proposer could
build next. In opening offers, swaps that complete only the proposer’s group are rejected more often
than swaps that complete no group (95/116 versus 37/72). Cash bids show no corresponding increase
(42/70 versus 67/103). The swap gap keeps its sign in every proposer stratum with data, but its size
depends on who proposed the comparison offers. Without Grok-authored offers it falls from 30.5 to
16.7 percentage points, and a resampling interval that respects the seed design then includes zero.
Recipients’ stated reasons cite completion and building under both offer forms, so the reasons do not
explain why only swaps differ. We report this pattern as exploratory and do not interpret it as an
identified response to the rival’s next move.
Conduct in context.
Eight incidents show these statements in play: refusing and pricing against a
rival’s building cash, a factual correction, a settled bluff, a selective claim about financing, a public
appeal, a withdrawn assurance and the ten-action payment. Each comes with exact quotations, named
channels, checked delivery where claimed, and its recorded continuation.
The paper makes three contributions.
1. A complete debt census that separates easy from dependent liquidation and pairs each dependent
case with the prospective text that preceded it (Section 3).
2. A complete opening-offer census by form, completion, proposer, recipient and seed block, with the
composition and uncertainty a reader needs to judge the form pattern (Section 4).
3. Eight contextual incidents with verified quotations, channels and continuations, each assigned the
evidence level it reaches (Sections 3–5).
The study describes four configured systems under unequal conditions. Opus played most decisions
through a refusal-recovery path without private notes, and Grok ran with forced tool choice and
sometimes returned text naming a different action from the call the engine applied. The results are

Study design
2.1
Environment
Four agents start with $1,500 on the forty-space United States board. They buy and auction deeds, trade
through bilateral threads of up to twenty exchanges, and build evenly on complete color groups. The
bank holds 32 houses and twelve hotels. Developed groups cannot be traded, and a deed in a group
with buildings cannot be mortgaged until the group’s buildings are sold. A player who owes more
than its cash receives a liquidation menu of building sales and mortgages, refreshed after every action.
It cannot trade during liquidation, and it may declare bankruptcy even when payment is possible.
A player-creditor receives the debtor’s assets with buildings intact. Appendix E lists the remaining
conventions. Amounts are game currency unless marked USD.
The engine alone validates actions, changes state and emits events. At each decision one player
gets a menu of legal actions and returns one tool call with the action, a public message and, under
the standard prompt, a private note. The engine applies the received call; assistant content and
provider-returned reasoning are stored but never applied, even when they describe another action.
2.2
Cohort and configurations
The twenty games use five seeds, each played in four cyclic seat rotations of one fixed roster (Appendix
Table 6). Every system occupies every seat five times but always sits after the same opponent. Games
last 75 to 386 turns and all end by bankruptcy. The cohort has 50,918 events, 6,989 decisions and
8,679 response attempts. Gn dk denotes zero-based decision k in game n. Games 1–4 ran from source
commit 42f85183 and games 5–20 from d6808534.
Response conditions differ across systems (Table 1). An invalid response normally receives one
corrective request and then a deterministic fallback. When a provider refuses a first attempt, the second
attempt instead uses an action-only prompt with no private note. All 1,605 refusals are of Opus and
carry one identical provider message about reproducing model outputs; none of the recovery calls
is refused. Opus therefore plays 1,604 of its 1,696 decisions without a note and without a further
correction, and the text we read for Opus is its provider-returned reasoning. Grok runs with tool_choice
set to required; the others use auto. In 1,068 selected Grok responses the assistant content names an
action, and in 139 that action differs from the call the engine applied. We cannot attribute these conflicts
to the tool-choice setting, but other agents answered the offers they produced. Finally, past-trade

2.3
Information and channels
Prompts show every player’s cash, deeds, buildings and mortgages, the bank’s stock, a twenty-entry
public timeline and the agent’s own last ten notes. Trade responses include the thread history. Only
purchase prompts list a deed’s price and rents. The standard prompt permits public bluffing and
deception and asks that private notes reflect true reasoning; the recovery prompt drops the note and
that sentence but keeps the permission to deceive (Appendix B).
Every quotation names its channel. An applied private note is attached to the executed call and never
shown to opponents. Provider-returned reasoning is the reasoning field of the selected attempt. Assistant
content is free text that the engine does not apply. A public message is emitted with the action, and
we say it reached a recipient only after finding it in that recipient’s stored prompt. Recorded text is
evidence of what a system returned, not a view of its computation [12, 16].
2.4
Measures
Offers.
An offer is a set of terms awaiting a response; its author can differ from the thread’s initiator.
For each of the 918 offers the recipient could accept, we reconstruct both parties’ cash and holdings
after the proposed transfer and record which color groups it would newly complete: the proposer’s
only, the recipient’s only, both, or neither. A swap moves deeds in both directions and may add cash.
A cash bid offers only cash for deeds. Other forms, mostly deed-for-cash sales, form a third class. An
opening offer is the first offer of a thread.
Debts.
A liquidation episode starts at the first explicit debt menu and ends in payment or elimination;
debts covered by cash on hand never reach it. At the first menu we compute cash, the mortgage value of
deeds listed on the menu (L0), half the building cost of every building level (S, a hotel counting as five)
and the mortgage value of unmortgaged deeds absent from the menu (U). Their sum is the own-asset
bound. Payable debts fall into three tiers: tier A when cash and L0 cover the debt, tier B when sales
are also needed but U is not, and tier N, necessary released collateral, when only U can close the gap. A
bound above the debt is arithmetic, not a legal route. For the three unpaid debts within the bound,
earlier engine-executed checks supply payment sequences, which we inherit without rerunning.
2.5
Uncertainty
Counts are exact for this finite cohort. When we describe uncertainty, the target is the difference between
two pooled rejection shares for a cohort of the same design: the same roster and configurations, five
seed blocks of four rotations. Games within a block share a seed and are not independent draws. Our
primary summary therefore resamples seed blocks and then games within each drawn block (20,000
draws); Appendix C adds resampling by games alone and by whole blocks, and every block separately.
An earlier draft’s authors found the offer-form contrast after inspecting this corpus, so these intervals
describe its spread within the cohort and do not confirm it. We report no p-values.
The analysis reads stored records only and runs no game, engine branch, replay, baseline policy
or model query. Appendix A gives the repository, commits, seeds, game map, both stored replay
comparisons and the commands that regenerate every number. Appendix G separates what we checked
directly from what we inherit.

3
Anticipating one’s own changed options
A debt gives planning a known target: raise a stated sum from a menu that changes after each action,
or declare bankruptcy. We first ask how demanding the debts were, then examine those where the
first menu hides what payment requires.
3.1
Most debts were easy
Agents pay 128 of the 188 debts that reached a liquidation menu (Table 2). Fifty-six of the 60 eliminations
owe more than the debtor’s own assets could raise. One more, Opus in G7, is within the bound, but the
bank has no houses left and the menu offers no building sale; the funds it can reach are $440 against
$750. The remaining three were payable, as earlier engine-executed sequences showed. Payment
therefore succeeds in 128 of 131 debts known to be payable.
That rate says little about difficulty. In 62 payable debts the cash and listed mortgages suffice,
and all are paid. Sixty-seven payments take one action, and the median debt is 36% of the own-asset
bound. Because the menu refreshes after every action, an agent that keeps choosing listed financing
actions never has to look ahead to make progress. The decision that needs foresight is whether to stop:
bankruptcy is always on the menu, and an agent that judges payment impossible can end its game
while a route exists. That judgment matters most in tier N.
3.2
Sixteen debts that needed released collateral
In sixteen debts, cash plus every listed mortgage plus the salvage value of every building still falls short.
The difference can come only from mortgaging deeds whose buildings must first be sold. Figure 1
shows each case. Agents pay fourteen. Gemini pays five of six, Opus five of five, Sol two of three and
Grok two of two. The amount that only released collateral can supply ranges from $12 to $326.
We read the recorded text at each first decision, before any action in the episode was applied. In
thirteen of the fourteen payments the text names the mortgages that sales will release, or states a
budget that includes them. Nine of these are applied private notes from Sol, Gemini and Grok. Four
are Opus’s provider-returned reasoning; under recovery Opus wrote no note. Grok’s note at G1 d476
is typical: “Must sell before yellows can be mortgaged. Next prompt: mortgage one yellow (Atlantic/Ventnor
$130 or Marvin $140) to clear the debt.” In the fourteenth payment, Opus at G20 d291 correctly finds
that listed items leave it short and then writes, “I’m checking whether mortgaging the pink lots themselves
after clearing the houses could close the gap.” It goes on to pay.

3.3
What the sequences ask of the agent
The fourteen paid tier N sequences take two to ten actions. Each sells buildings before its first released
mortgage and needs one kind of prerequisite: clear a group’s buildings evenly, then mortgage the
group. Execution length therefore overstates dependency depth, and since refreshed menus show each
released mortgage once it exists, the actions could follow from step-by-step feedback. The first-decision
text adds a prospective statement of a resource the first menu does not show.
Released collateral is used more widely than it is needed. Nineteen payments use 33 mortgages
absent from the first menu. Fourteen are the necessary cases. In the other five, four by Gemini in G3
and one by Sol in G18, the agents could have paid from listed mortgages and salvage alone.
Incident 1. A ten-action liquidation reaches its stated remainder
In G20 only Gemini and Sol remain. Sol has $49, nine orange houses and two brown hotels. Before
raising Pennsylvania Avenue’s rent to $1,400, Gemini values its rival’s estate:
Gemini · applied private note · G20 d325 · attempt 0 · full field
p3's total liquidation value is $1,414 ($49 cash + $675 from selling 9 Orange houses and 2 Brown
hotels + $690 from mortgaging 8 unmortgaged properties). Building a hotel on Pennsylvania Avenue
for $200 raises its rent from $1,200 to $1,400 while leaving me $213 cash to easily cover Park Place
($35) and Luxury Tax ($100) before passing GO.
Sol lands there on the next turn. Its note at the first liquidation decision states an aggregate budget
and one ordering constraint:
Sol · applied private note · G20 d327 · attempt 0 · full field
Full liquidation should yield $1,414 including cash and all mortgages, enough for $1,400 rent by $14.
Must sell all development and mortgage everything; survive rather than concede. If hotel sales leave
four houses, sell those next evenly.
Sol sells its development in two actions and mortgages eight deeds, five of them released by the
sales. At the last mortgage it writes:
Sol · applied private note · G20 d336 · attempt 0 · full field
Final $30 mortgage covers $16 shortfall, leaving $14 after rent. Survive; rebuild cash through GO
and avoid greens.
The events record $700 from sales and $665 from mortgages, so $49 + $700 + $665 −$1,400 = $14.
Gemini’s total is also right, but its parts are not: it books $675 of salvage and $690 of mortgages, two
errors of $25 that cancel. Sol’s budget is the stronger evidence because the actions that follow confirm
it. The note specifies a total and a rule for hotel sales, not an ordered list of ten actions; the menus
supplied the order. This incident reaches accurate and consistent for both the debtor’s plan and the
creditor’s total, with the creditor’s components inaccurate.

A different kind of failure.
The third payable declaration has another cause. At G12 d249 Grok owes
$250 with $43 cash and seven pink houses. Its applied note first concludes that payment is impossible,

4
Bargaining over a rival’s changed options
A trade that completes another player’s color group lets that player build. We ask how recipients
respond to such offers, what they say, and how far the records connect the two.
4.1
The response census
The 920 responses comprise 113 acceptances, 394 counteroffers and 413 rejections. Sol and Opus
counter most (140 of 235 and 141 of 189), Gemini and Grok reject most (180 of 242 and 149 of 254),
and each accepts 26 to 30 offers. Opus answers 162 of its 189 offers under recovery.
4.2
Completion and offer form
Table 3 gives every opening offer. Swaps that would complete only the proposer’s group are rejected in
95 of 116 cases (81.9%), against 37 of 72 swaps that complete no group (51.4%). Swaps that complete
both parties’ groups are rejected in 34 of 48 (70.8%). Cash bids show no corresponding increase. Bids
that would complete the proposer’s group are rejected in 42 of 70 (60.0%), against 67 of 103 bids that
complete nothing (65.0%). Holders also reject 29 of 45 offers to sell them the deed that would complete
their own group, usually at a high price. Rejection falls in later offers, which are selected by what both
parties have already conceded: 8 of 39 versus 7 of 34 for swaps and 13 of 61 versus 13 of 73 for cash
bids.

4.3
Who proposed the comparison swaps
The comparison group is unusual (Table 4). Grok wrote 36 of the 72 opening swaps that complete
no group and 15 of the 17 that recipients accepted; thirteen of those fifteen came from calls whose
assistant content named a different action, nearly always ending the turn. Measured at board list
prices, the fifteen accepted Grok swaps transferred a net $4,020 to their recipients. List price ignores
mortgages, complementarity and blocking value, so this figure measures the bundles, not whether
they were giveaways.
The swap gap keeps its sign within every proposer whose cells are large enough to compare,
but its size varies: 6.2 points for Sol, 18.8 for Opus and 26.4 for Grok. It is also positive for every
recipient, and in seven of the nine proposer–recipient pairs that have both kinds of swap. Removing all
Grok-authored offers leaves 90 of 108 against 24 of 36, a gap of 16.7 points. Removing only the threads
opened by conflicting Grok calls leaves 93 of 112 against 26 of 43, a gap of 22.6 points. Standardizing
the within-proposer gaps to a common proposer mix gives 16.0 to 21.5 points, depending on which
cell supplies the weights. No version recovers the games that would have been played without Grok’s
offers. The cash bids have their own imbalance: Opus wrote 110 of the 173 in the two cells, and within
Sol, Opus and Grok the cash-bid gaps run from −5.4 to +2.1 points.
4.4
Spread across seed blocks
Figure 2 shows the same comparisons across the five seed blocks. With all offers, the swap gap is
positive in every block, from 19.4 to 40.5 points, and dropping any one block leaves 28.7 to 33.3. Without
Grok-authored offers it is positive in four blocks; in the fourth, all four remaining comparison swaps
are rejected and the gap is −23.5. The two-stage resampling interval is +12.3 to +48.8 points for all
swaps, +1.4 to +45.7 without conflicting openings and −10.0 to +40.9 without Grok-authored offers.
For cash bids it is −26.5 to +11.4. That interval includes no difference and moderate differences in
either direction, so the records do not show that completion is irrelevant to cash bids. Appendix C
gives every block and two other resampling units.
4.5
What recipients say
Two LLM-assisted readings of an earlier draft coded 74 direct rejections of offers that would complete
the recipient’s own group, 38 recipient-only and 36 completing both parties. They agree that 61 of the
74 cite a trade-linked consequence for the rival. Gemini and Grok supply 56 of the 61, and G1 and G15
supply 31. None of the 74 offers belongs to the proposer-only or neither cells of the form comparison.

only cash bids mention completion in all 42 and building in 35. The stated reasons are much the same
under both forms. What differs is the action. Recipients counter 26 of the 70 completing cash bids and
20 of the 116 completing swaps, and 15 of those 26 cash-bid counters keep the same deeds and ask for
more cash.
4.6
Two alternative explanations
A recipient might simply reject bundles that give it little. Yet at list prices proposer-only swaps offer
recipients more than comparison swaps (median net +$170 against +$80). Among swaps that favor
the recipient at list prices, 86 of 103 proposer-only swaps are rejected against 24 of 48 comparison swaps,
and 81 of 95 against 18 of 25 without Grok’s offers. List price omits mortgage status, complementarity
and the blocking value of the requested deed. Blocking value is the quantity at issue, so this check
narrows the immediate-benefit explanation without removing it.
A recipient might instead track how much the proposer could build next. Rejection of proposer-only
swaps does not rise with the proposer’s post-trade house budget: 26 of 33 when it could buy one or
two houses, 42 of 48 for three to five and 23 of 27 for six or more. Cash bids show no ordering either
(Appendix Table 12). The cells are small and cash omits mortgage capacity, but these slices give no
sign that refusals scale with the rival’s building power.
The bargaining evidence reaches stated for many refusals and accurate and consistent in individual

Discussion
6.1
What the records establish
Table 5 places each result on the evidence ladder. Two patterns stand out. First, in the debts and
incidents we examined, when an agent wrote down before acting how its action would change the
options, the applied action followed the statement. This held when the statement was right, in fourteen
payments, and when it was wrong, in two bankruptcies and in Sol’s nine-house overcount. Second, the
failures we can trace lie in the assessment rather than in carrying it out. The exception is Grok, whose
applied call contradicts its own assistant content in 139 decisions; at G12 d249 its note and content
both describe a payment and the call declares bankruptcy.

6.2
Own options and rivals’ options
The two halves of the paper ask the same question about different players. For its own collateral an
agent can apply a known rule, and the record shows whether it did. For a rival’s options it must judge
what another player will do, and we never see the game in which the refused trade was accepted. This
difference in what the records can show, not necessarily any difference in the agents’ reasoning, is why
the finance evidence is sharper. Recipients do state the rival’s next construction, and in G15 and G7
they compute it correctly. Holding the offered terms fixed while varying what the proposer could
build would test whether they respond to it.
6.3
Configured systems
The measured objects are four configured systems, not four models. In a multi-agent benchmark
their conditions spread: conflicting Grok calls supplied most accepted comparison swaps in Section 4,
and every player answered them. Appendix F answers each motivating question at the level these
conditions allow.

7
Related work
Negotiation with language models has been studied against explicit payoffs [8, 13] and under controlled
changes in tactics [3], and recent work separates modeling a counterpart from using that model well [5].
Our levels follow that distinction between knowing and acting, in a persistent setting without a utility
reference for each trade.
Strategic games give LLMs structured opponents. GTBench evaluates strategic reasoning across
ten board and card games [6]. Guhe and Lascarides compare persuasion and trade embargoes among
symbolic Settlers of Catan agents through game outcomes [7], so withholding trades from a leader is
an established tactic. A reinforcement-learning Monopoly agent with hybrid decision rules wins most
games against fixed-policy agents [4]; win rates across engines and opponents are not comparable
to ours. AgentMonopoly studies model and persona variation in Monopoly [18], FinBoardBench
financial management across board games [9], and Cattle Trade bluffing and bidding with code-agent
comparisons [15]. Playing Monopoly or making counteroffers is therefore not new; our contribution is
the census linking stated assessments to applied actions.
Long-horizon economic agents and communication have been studied in Vending-Bench [2] and
in a multi-agent commerce arena that links messages to later conduct with reference annotations [14].
PlanBench separates generating a plan from reasoning about how actions change state [17]; our debt
tiers make the same separation inside a game, and the prospective text plays the role of a stated plan.
Economic theory describes externalities that depend on who acquires an asset [10]; Monopoly makes
such externalities concrete through building and inheritance, and we use the idea only to interpret
stated stakes. Returned reasoning need not reflect the computation behind an answer [12, 16], and
agent evaluations should report the whole configured system [11].
8
Limitations
The cohort is twenty games from five seeds and one cyclic seat order of a fixed roster. Model, seat,
opponent and random exposure cannot be separated, and the 8/7/3/2 win split for Sol, Gemini, Opus
and Grok is not a ranking. The offer-form contrast and the sixteen necessary cases were both identified
retrospectively. Resampling describes spread within this cohort; it does not test a hypothesis chosen in
advance, and recomputing a contrast checks arithmetic, not replication.
Response conditions differ across systems and shape the offers others face; Opus’s text is provider-
returned reasoning, some of Grok’s applied offers contradict its own text, and removing either system’s
rows does not reconstruct a clean cohort.
We read the sixteen first-decision texts once, as one LLM reader, and the 61/74 codes come from
earlier LLM readings; no human annotated either. There is no baseline policy, no value reference for
trades and no counterfactual rollout, so nothing here shows that an action was good or that a reactive
policy would fail. Three payment sequences, the G7 menu search and the historical replay executions
are inherited from earlier engine-executed checks that we did not rerun. The task permits deception,
so bluffs and selective claims are permitted conduct, not instruction violations.
9
Conclusion
When these agents wrote down, before acting, how an action would change their own options, they
then did what they had written. In the sixteen debts whose payment depended on mortgages that
only building sales release, statements that counted those mortgages preceded fourteen payments, and
18

=== page 19
statements that missed them preceded two bankruptcies. For other players’ options the agents state
concern about rival construction often and compute it correctly in specific cases. A form-dependent
rejection pattern in opening offers holds in direction across proposers and seed blocks, but its size
depends on who proposed the comparison offers and its cause is not identified. The next step is to test
both findings prospectively on new games and matched states, with the definitions fixed here.

Two stored replay comparisons.
Every run stores two replay reports, written by the run pipeline on
25–26 October 2026. We read them; we did not rerun them. The artifact comparison (artifact_replay_
report.json) replays the applied actions and compares the complete event stream, each event serialized
by canonical_event_lines as JSON with sorted keys and compact separators. It excludes no event type
and removes no field; it covers all 50,918 events. Identifiers and timestamps can match because the
runs use a synthetic clock (start 0 ms, step 250 ms). The state comparison (state_replay_report.json)
drops the four LLM observation event types (decision requested, decision response, private thought,
public message) and renumbers event identifiers, sequence numbers and timestamps to a state index; it
covers 24,506 events. Both pass in all twenty games. An earlier executable audit in the research record
repeated complete-event replay; we inherit its result. Replay equality shows reproduction under the
frozen implementation, not correctness against an independent rules specification.

Entry point.
From the draft folder, bashbuild.sh runs analysis/run_all.sh, which executes offers_ana
lysis.py, finance_analysis.py, provenance_checks.py, quotes.py, tables.py and figures.py in order and
then builds this PDF with LuaLaTeX. Each script reads stored records, writes JSON to data/ with the
SHA-256 of every input file, and stops on a failed assertion. Resampling uses a fixed generator seed
(20261112). No script imports the engine or arena, replays a game or contacts a provider.
B
Configuration, prompts and response conditions
Response policy.
At both run commits the arena requests one tool call per decision. An invalid
response receives one corrective request; a second failure triggers the deterministic finance_before_bankr
uptcy_v2 fallback. An explicit provider refusal instead triggers the versioned claude_refusal_recovery_v1
policy: the next attempt uses an action-only prompt without the private-note field. The policy also
names anthropic/claude-opus-4.8 as a fallback model, but no decision in the cohort reached that stage.
Provider routing pins each model to its own provider with fallbacks disabled.
Refusals.
All 1,605 refused attempts carry one identical message: “This request was blocked as it
seems to violate Anthropic’s Terms of Service restrictions on reverse engineering or duplicating model
outputs.” Each has finish reason content_filter and native finish reason refusal. The gateway response
exposes no structured refusal category, so the upstream trigger is not identified [1]. First-attempt
refusals cover 1,604 of Opus’s 1,696 decisions; the recovery prompt keeps permission to deceive, and
none of its calls is refused.
Prompts.
The two system prompts below match the system messages stored in G1 records word for
word; line breaks were added for width. The standard prompt asks for private notes and permits public
deception. The recovery prompt removes the note and the sentence about honest private reasoning.
Standard prompt
You are an autonomous player in a game of Monopoly competing against other AI players.
Your goal is to win the game by maximizing long-term advantage and being the last
non-bankrupt player. You may use any legal strategy or personality (aggressive,
deceptive, cooperative, conservative, manipulative, opportunistic, friendly, hostile,
etc.) and may adapt dynamically as the game evolves.
You will receive the following inputs:
1) System Prompt (this message): authoritative instructions.
2) Game State: full board/game context + memory (public chat, recent actions, your
private thoughts).
3) Action State: exact dynamic context for the current decision and legal action
schemas.
### Action Rules
- Make exactly one tool call per decision.
- Use only tools listed as legal for that decision.
- Never invent tools, arguments, or targets.
- Obey the provided argument schema exactly.
- If a tool requires no arguments, pass none.
### Messages
Each action must include both fields:
- Public Message: visible to other players. Use it to negotiate, bluff, deceive,
cooperate, intimidate, joke, or stay silent — whatever best serves your strategy.

E
Implementation conventions
Construction is validated entry by entry, so ordering matters even when the final allocation is even. A
hotel downgrade requires four houses in the bank; in G7 the bank had none and the menu offered
no building sale. Mortgage redemption and transferred-deed interest use ceiling expressions; for five
board principals redemption exceeds the exact ten-percent charge by one dollar. Buildings pass intact
to a player-creditor, inherited mortgaged deeds incur no interest at transfer, developed groups cannot
trade, and assets returned to the bank are not auctioned at once. The system prompt does not list these
house rules.
