What a Move Unlocks:
Stated Consequences and Actions of LLM Agents
in Twenty Monopoly Games
MonopolyBench project∗
12 November 2026
Abstract
Some moves matter for what they make possible next. We ask whether LLM agents state such
consequences before they act, whether the statements are accurate, and whether their actions
agree with them, using twenty complete four-player Monopoly games with free-form negotiation
among four configured systems (GPT-6.1 Sol, Gemini 4 Argon, Claude Opus 5.5, Grok 4.7). For
the agent’s own options we examine all 188 debts that required liquidation. Agents declared
bankruptcy at the first menu in 50 of 56 debts that exceeded everything they owned and in 3 of
131 payable debts. Sixteen payable debts could be met only with mortgages that the menu lists
after buildings are sold, a rule and values the prompt does not supply. Agents paid fourteen. The
text stored before the first action counts the unlisted mortgages in thirteen of those, raises them
in one, and omits them in both failures. For a rival’s options we examine all 920 responses to
trade offers. Recipients accepted 3 of 186 opening offers that would complete only the proposer’s
color group. They rejected 81.9% of such swaps against 51.4% of swaps completing no group, a
gap of 16.7 to 30.5 points depending on which proposers are counted, and they countered cash
bids more often than swaps. Refusals mention the rival’s set under both forms, yet rejection
does not rise with the building the rival could then afford. Eight sourced incidents place these
statements in context, including a deed sold as insurance against a third player’s inheritance.
Stated foresight about the agent’s own financing is accurate and matches action. Stated concern
about rivals matches action and attaches to the event of completion. The study is observational,
covers one cohort under unequal response conditions, and measures neither effectiveness nor
model rank.
1
Introduction
Some moves matter for what they make possible next. A Monopoly player in debt who sells the
houses on a color group receives the sale price and also gains something the menu does not show,
the right to mortgage that group’s deeds. A player who sells the last deed in a rival’s color group
receives cash and gives the rival the right to build. In both cases the terms on the table understate the
move. An agent that reads only those terms will quit a debt it could have paid, or sell a monopoly
at the price of a single deed.

1

Language-model agents now play games of this kind with free-form negotiation, and they write
down reasons as they act. That makes the problem observable. When an agent states what a move
will make possible, we can ask four questions in order. Is the statement recorded before the action?
Is it accurate against the game state and the rules? Does the action agree with it? Does the behavior
change when the size of the consequence changes? Work on LLM negotiation separates knowing a
counterpart’s position from using that knowledge [3], and work on planning tests reasoning about
how actions change the world [13]. We ask the same kind of question about two consequences in
one environment: what a move opens for the agent itself, and what it opens for a rival.
The evidence is twenty complete four-player games of MonopolyBench played by one roster of
four configured systems: GPT-6.1 Sol, Gemini 4 Argon, Claude Opus 5.5 and Grok 4.7. The records
hold 6,989 decisions, 920 responses to trade offers and 188 debts that required a liquidation menu.
Each offer is tied to the state its recipient saw, and each debt to its first menu, the actions taken and
the outcome.
The agent’s own options.
At the first menu of a debt, agents quit in 50 of the 56 debts that exceeded
everything they owned and in 3 of the 131 debts that could be paid. Sixteen of the payable debts
could be met only with mortgages that the menu would not list until buildings were sold. Agents
paid fourteen. In thirteen of the fourteen, the text stored at the first decision counts those unlisted
mortgages before any building is sold, and in the other it raises them as an open question. In both
failures the text adds up the listed items only, and the agent declares bankruptcy. The prompt states
neither the rule that a sale releases mortgages nor any mortgage value. The figures the agents state
match the records in 14 of 15 checks.
A rival’s options.
Recipients accepted 3 of 186 opening offers that would complete only the
proposer’s color group. They rejected 81.9% of such swaps, against 51.4% of swaps that completed
nobody’s group. The gap has the same direction for every recipient, proposer and seed block, and
its size depends on which proposers are counted. Cash bids for a completing deed were rejected
less often (60.0%) and countered about twice as often. Refusal texts mention the completed set
in nearly every case. Gemini states a reciprocity rule, Grok states never-sell commitments with
estimates of the rival’s building cash, and Sol and Opus mostly name a price. Rejection does not
rise with the number of houses the proposer could then afford.
Third parties and the uses of talk.
Eight sourced incidents show these statements in context.
They include a refusal of one’s own monopoly to deny a rival’s, a deed sold as insurance against a
third player’s inheritance, a ten-action payment that ends on the announced $14, a bluff, a corrected
false claim, a public call to withhold cash and a withdrawn assurance.
The two halves reach different levels of evidence. For the agent’s own financing, the statements
are prospective, mostly accurate and followed by matching actions, in cases fixed by rule arithmetic.
For rivals, stated concern and matching refusals attach to the event of completion, with no measured
response to the size of the rival’s opportunity. Neither result measures whether the behavior was
effective. All of it describes four configured systems in one cohort, two of which ran under altered
response conditions (Section 2).
The paper contributes a census of all 188 debt episodes partitioned by what the first menu
could raise, a complete table of the 918 acceptance-legal offers by form, completion and stage with a
stated sensitivity analysis, and eight incidents with exact quotations, channels, receipt checks and
continuations.

Setting and method
2.1
The game and the interface
Four agents start with $1,500 on the United States Monopoly board. They buy or auction properties,
trade bilaterally in threads of up to twenty exchanges, build on complete color groups and mortgage
deeds. All amounts are game dollars.
A player who cannot pay a debt receives a liquidation menu listing the deeds it may mortgage
now and the buildings it may sell now. The engine applies the chosen action and asks again until the
debt is paid. A deed in a group that still carries buildings cannot be mortgaged and is absent from
the menu. Selling the group’s buildings puts its deeds on the next menu. The player may declare
bankruptcy at any decision, including when payment is possible. The liquidation prompt gives the
amount owed, the shortfall, the two lists and the player’s holdings. It gives no price, mortgage value
or sale value, and it does not say that selling buildings releases mortgages. A bankrupt player’s
deeds pass to a player-creditor with their buildings intact.
The engine issues the legal options and validates the returned action. The agent returns one tool
call with a public message and, under the standard prompt, a private note. Later prompts carry a
twenty-entry public timeline and up to ten of the agent’s own notes. The system prompt permits
bluffing, deception, intimidation and cooperation in public messages and asks that private notes
reflect true reasoning. Appendix E prints it.
2.2
Cohort and response conditions
Five seeds were each played in four cyclic seat rotations of one roster, giving twenty games. We call
the four games that share a seed a seed block. Games have no turn cap, last 75 to 386 turns and end
by bankruptcy. Table 1 lists the four configurations. Every request asks for medium reasoning effort
and sets no temperature or token limit. Appendix D gives the seeds, game map, source commits
and analysis entry point.
Two response conditions differ by system and reach every player through the interaction.
Opus. The provider refused 1,604 of Opus’s 1,696 first attempts with one message, which cites
terms-of-service restrictions on reverse engineering or duplicating model outputs. The share is 214
of 306 in G1 to G4 and all 1,390 in G5 to G20. The interface then resends the request without the
private-note field and without the sentence asking for true private reasoning. Permission to deceive
stays. None of the 1,604 recovery requests was refused. Under recovery Opus writes no private
note and gets no corrective retry. The provider still returns explanation text, which we treat as a
separate channel.
3

Grok. Requests to Grok were sent with tool choice “required”. The other three used “auto”. In
139 Grok responses the text outside the tool call names a different action from the call that was
received, including 64 of its 136 trade openings. No other system has such a response. The received
call governs play. These are real offers that other agents answered, so we keep them and report the
bargaining results with and without them. Whether the setting causes the disagreement is untested.
2.3
Channels and receipt
We keep four recorded channels apart. A public message is delivered to the other players. An
applied private note returns only to its author. Assistant content is text outside the tool call, and a
provider-returned explanation is reasoning text the provider attaches to a response. The interface
neither applies nor delivers those two. We say a player received a message only when it appears in
that player’s stored prompt. Stated text is what a system returned, not a view of the computation
behind the action [8, 12].
2.4
Measures
Offers.
An offer is a set of terms awaiting a response, either a thread’s opening proposal or a later
counter. The games contain 920 offers in 526 threads, and acceptance was legal for 918. For each
offer we rebuild ownership after the proposed transfer and record who would newly complete a
color group: the proposer only, the recipient only, both or neither. We also record the form. A swap
moves deeds in both directions. A cash bid offers only cash for deeds. A sale offer offers only deeds
for cash.
Debts.
A debt episode runs from the first liquidation menu to payment or elimination. At the
first menu we compute four sums from the delivered state: cash C, the mortgage value M0 of the
deeds the menu lists, the salvage S from selling every building at half cost, and the mortgage value
M1 of unmortgaged deeds the menu does not list. A debt above C + M0 + S + M1 exceeds every
asset the player owns. A debt above C + M0 + S and within the full sum cannot be paid without
mortgages that appear only after buildings are sold. These sums bound what each resource can
raise. A sufficient sum is not a payment plan. We count a debt as payable when the agent paid it,
or when an earlier audit of this project executed a legal payment sequence on the engine. Three
eliminations are payable in that second sense, and we inherit those sequences without rerunning
them.
Levels of evidence.
A record can show five things about a stated consequence. It can be (1) stated
before the action, (2) accurate against the state and rules, (3) consistent with the action taken, (4)
sensitive, meaning behavior changes when the consequence changes, and (5) effective, meaning the
behavior improves an outcome against a reference. Each result is tagged once with the highest level
it reaches. None reaches level five, because the games contain no reference policy or value estimate.
Sensitivity of the reported contrasts.

of these records, and the sixteen debt cases follow a referee’s suggestion. For each contrast we
therefore report how it moves when the set of games changes: its value in each seed block, its range
when one block is left out, and its distribution over all 3,125 ordered resamples of the five blocks and

3
The agent’s own options: paying a debt
3.1
Quitting and continuing across all 188 debts
A debt gives the agent a local goal with a known endpoint: raise a stated amount or leave the game.
The one decision that needs foresight is whether to continue. Once an agent continues, the menu
refreshes after each action and shows what can be done next. An agent that quits has judged the
whole route from the first menu.
Table 2 and Figure 1A partition the 188 episodes by what payment required at the first menu.
In 56 the debt exceeded everything the player owned. The agent declared bankruptcy at the first
menu in 50 of them and sold or mortgaged first in 6. In the 131 payable debts the agent continued in
128 and quit at the first menu in 3. Agents paid 128 of the 131, with 180 mortgages and 89 building
sales and no engine fallback. The remaining elimination, Opus in G7, had assets above the debt on
paper, but its menu offered no building sale. The bank had no houses left, so its one hotel could not
be broken down, and that blocked the rest of the group.
Most payable debts were easy. In 62 of them, cash and the mortgages already on the menu
covered the debt, and 67 of the 128 payments took one action. The median paid debt was 36% of the
debtor’s asset bound. The overall payment rate of 97.7% therefore says little about difficulty. The
table does show discrimination at the first menu. The agents quit almost always when no payment
existed and almost never when one did. This is level three: the choice agrees with the arithmetic of
the state.

A rival’s options: the completing deed
4.1
What happens to an offer that completes the proposer’s group
The agents answered 920 offers with 113 acceptances, 394 counters and 413 rejections. Each system
accepted between 26 and 30 offers. They differ in how they disagree: Sol and Opus countered 140
and 141 times, and Gemini and Grok rejected 180 and 149.
A deed that would complete the proposer’s color group is the clearest case of a move that opens
something for a rival, because only a complete group can be built on. Table 4 gives every opening
offer by form and by who would complete a group. We compare opening offers because later offers
are shaped by what both sides have already refused (Appendix B).
An opening swap or cash bid that would complete only the proposer’s group was accepted 3
times in 186. Recipients rejected 137 and countered 46. The same two forms, when they completed
nobody’s group, were accepted 21 times in 175.

4.4
Does refusal follow the size of the rival’s opportunity?
Grok’s note in G15 reasons about amounts: how much the rival would hold and how many houses
that buys. If refusals in general followed that reasoning, they would be more common when the
proposer would keep more building cash. They are not. Among completing swaps, recipients
rejected 26 of 33 when the proposer could afford one or two houses after the trade, 42 of 48 for three
to five, and 23 of 27 for six or more. When the proposer could afford none, they rejected 4 of 8, too
few to read. Completing cash bids show no order either (Appendix B). The measure ignores what
the proposer could still mortgage, so it is coarse.
A crude check speaks against the simplest alternative, that completing swaps are refused because
they pay badly. At board list prices the completing swaps offered the recipient more, a median of
$170 against $80. Among swaps that favored the recipient at list price, 86 of 103 completing ones
were rejected and 24 of 48 of the others. List price leaves out mortgages, group value and the value
of blocking, so this narrows the alternative without removing it. The stated amounts are also not
always right.

5
Third parties, and what the agents use talk for
A trade between two players can change what a third player gets. Because a bankrupt player’s deeds
pass to its creditor, keeping a weak player solvent can keep a monopoly away from someone else,
and letting it fail can deliver one. Of the 40 eliminations that left at least two players in the game, 35
sent the estate to the eventual winner. The agents bargain over this prospect in so many words.

A bluff with a recorded ceiling. In G7 Grok bids $600 for a deed and calls the offer final, while its

6
Discussion
What the two results share, and where they differ.
In both settings the agents wrote down a
consequence that the terms in front of them did not show, and then acted in line with it. The
debt result can be checked in a way the bargaining result cannot. Whether a debt needs unlisted
mortgages follows from four sums and the rules, so the hard cases are defined without reference to
what any agent did or said. In those cases the stated arithmetic was right in all but one instance,
and the action matched the statement every time. The systems that left out released collateral once
counted it in other games, so the two failures are lapses and do not mark a limit of any system.
In bargaining, the consequence is a judgment about value: how much a rival’s monopoly will
cost the holder later. The agents stated that judgment and refused accordingly, but their refusals
tracked the event of completion and the form of the offer. They did not track the amount of building
the rival could afford, and some stated amounts were wrong. Two systems declined almost every
15

such opening under an announced rule or commitment. A rule of that kind may be a sound response
to an opportunity that is hard to price. It is also what an agent would do if the word “monopoly”
alone drove the refusal. The records cannot separate the two.
Why form might matter.
A counter to a cash bid changes one number, while a swap requires
valuing two bundles and the rival’s gain together. The swaps on offer may also have been worse in
ways list prices miss, and proposer mix differs across the cells. A test would hold the recipient’s
side of the offer fixed and vary only whether the proposer completes a group, and separately vary
how much the proposer could then build.
The systems are configured systems.
The response conditions are part of what was measured.
Opus played without private notes after the first games, and Grok’s offers include many that its
own text disowned and that other agents accepted. With several agents in one game, a change to
one participant’s interface changes the offers every other participant sees.
7
Related work
Negotiation with language models.
Early neural negotiation agents were trained on dialogues
with hidden rewards [9]. NegotiationArena evaluates LLM bargaining in defined scenarios and
reports that tactics such as feigned desperation change payoffs [2]. Cosentino et al. find that agents
can model a counterpart’s preferences accurately and still fail to turn that into better outcomes [3].
Our levels of evidence follow that line. Those studies have an explicit payoff for each agreement
and controlled variation, which ours lacks. Ours has persistent consequences: a trade changes who
can build, who goes bankrupt and who inherits.
Externalities and trade restrictions.
Economic theory treats sales in which the identity of the
buyer changes the position of others. Jehiel and Moldovanu show that such externalities can delay
agreement [6], and Jehiel, Moldovanu and Stacchetti study sales that affect the later interaction
among potential buyers [7]. A completing deed is an object of this kind, and the G13 sale is priced on
a harm to a third party. We use the idea descriptively and test no equilibrium prediction. Refusing
trades that help a rival is also not new to LLM agents. Guhe and Lascarides study persuasion and
trade embargoes among symbolic agents in Settlers of Catan and measure their effect on winning
in controlled simulations [4]. Our records show free-form versions of such refusals without the
comparison that would say whether they help.
Monopoly and economic agent benchmarks.
AgentMonopoly varies model family, size and
persona in four-player Monopoly and reports trade frequency and acceptance [14]. FinBoardBench
covers financial management across board games and finds that models buy assets while short of
cash [5]. Cattle Trade pits LLMs against scripted agents and relates resource discipline to rank [11].
Li et al. check inter-agent messages in long commerce simulations against simulator state, with a
reference set and several judge models [10]. Playing Monopoly, countering offers and managing
cash are therefore not our contribution. We add a complete census of offers and debts paired with
the text recorded before each action, and the split of debts by what the first menu could raise.
16

Planning and stated reasons.
PlanBench evaluates plan generation and reasoning about change
in planning domains and reports that LLMs fall well short on plan generation [13]. Debt payment
here is a different task. Execution has feedback because the menu refreshes after each action. The
decision to continue has none, and the sixteen cases place the test there. Explanations produced by
language models need not reflect the process that produced the answer [8, 12], so we compare each
note with the state and the action and do not treat it as evidence of how the action was chosen.
8
Limitations
The cohort is twenty games from five seeds, one roster and one cyclic seat order. It cannot separate
a system from its seat, its opponents or its luck, and it supports no ranking. Wins were 8, 7, 3 and 2
for Sol, Gemini, Opus and Grok, and 7, 7, 3 and 3 by seat.
Every contrast was selected after the games were inspected, and no new games were run to
confirm any of them. The sixteen hard debts include only two failures. The offer comparisons differ
in terms, counterparties and timing as well as in completion and form. No reference policy, value
estimate or human annotation is available, so nothing here says whether a refusal, a price or a
liquidation was good play. Whether a rule that takes any listed financing action would pay the same
debts is untested. Such a rule would never face the decision to quit, where both failures occurred.
The readings of text come from language-model assistants, some from the same model families
as two participants, and exact spans are printed so that a reader can check them. The three payment
sequences behind the payable bankruptcies and the replay comparisons are inherited from earlier
work in this project. The prompt invites public deception, so the incidents show permitted conduct.
The response conditions for Opus and Grok shaped what every player faced.
9
Conclusion
Across twenty complete games, LLM agents stated what a move would make possible before they
made it, and their actions agreed with those statements. For their own debts the statements were
accurate. Agents almost always quit when no payment existed and continued when one did. They
paid fourteen of the sixteen debts that could be met only with mortgages absent from the menu,
and in thirteen of those they had counted that collateral before acting. For rivals the statements
were as frequent and less exact. Agents almost never accepted an opening that completed only the
proposer’s group, they refused swaps and put prices on cash bids, and their refusals followed the
event of completion without following how much the rival could then build. Whether these choices
were good ones, and whether they hold in a new cohort, are questions for controlled tests.

Interface configuration.
Every request went through OpenRouter with medium reasoning effort,
parallel tool calls off, and no temperature, nucleus or token setting. Tool choice was “required”
for Grok and “auto” for the other three. We read this from the 8,687 saved request records and
from the source at both run commits, where the function that sets it carries the comment that the
setting should make Grok invoke a tool and not describe one. An invalid first response receives
one corrective request, and a second invalid response falls back to a deterministic engine policy,
recorded in each run configuration as finance_before_bankruptcy_v2. Engine fallback resolved 61
Opus decisions, 4 Sol decisions, 1 Gemini decision and no Grok decision. A provider refusal on
the first attempt uses the second attempt for the action-only request described in Appendix E. The
27

run configuration names a second model as a further fallback for Opus. No attempt in these games
used it.
Replay comparisons.
Each run directory stores two replay comparisons made when the run was
finalized on 25 and 26 October 2026. The engine was re-executed from the seed and the applied
actions, and its event stream was compared with the stored one.
• artifact_replay_report.json compares the full event stream. Each event is serialized with sorted
keys, no event type is excluded and no field is dropped. All twenty runs passed, covering 50,918
events. Equality of full events is possible because the runs use a synthetic clock that starts at
zero and advances 250 ms per event.
• state_replay_report.json compares state-relevant events only. It drops the four event types that
record LLM requests, responses, private thoughts and public messages, and it renumbers event
identifiers, sequence numbers and timestamps. All twenty runs passed, covering 24,506 events.
Both comparisons show that the frozen engine reproduces the stored events. Neither checks the
rules against an independent specification. We read the stored reports and did not rerun either
comparison.

Standard system prompt.
The same system prompt opens all 6,989 decisions. Its exact text follows.
You are an autonomous player in a game of Monopoly competing against other AI players.
Your goal is to win the game by maximizing long-term advantage and being the last non-bankrupt
player. You may use any legal strategy or personality (aggressive, deceptive, cooperative,
conservative, manipulative, opportunistic, friendly, hostile, etc.) and may adapt dynamically as
the game evolves.
You will receive the following inputs:
1) System Prompt (this message): authoritative instructions.
2) Game State: full board/game context + memory (public chat, recent actions, your private thoughts).
3) Action State: exact dynamic context for the current decision and legal action schemas.
### Action Rules
- Make exactly one tool call per decision.
- Use only tools listed as legal for that decision.
- Never invent tools, arguments, or targets.
- Obey the provided argument schema exactly.
- If a tool requires no arguments, pass none.

Each action must include both fields:
- Public Message: visible to other players. Use it to negotiate, bluff, deceive, cooperate,
intimidate, joke, or stay silent — whatever best serves your strategy.
- Private Thoughts: visible only to you. Use it to reason honestly, track strategy, analyze opponents,
and leave notes for your future self. Be concise but clear.
- Keep both messages token-efficient and concise.
### Strategy Guidance
- Play strategically with long-term outcomes in mind.
- Observe opponents and adapt.

Your role is not to explain rules or debug the system.

When a decision is presented, think carefully and make one legal tool call that best advances your

Research assistance and provenance
The games were designed, run and archived by the project owner. This manuscript is one of four
complete drafts written independently by LLM research assistants under the owner’s direction:
Astra, Sol, Opus and Fable 5.2. The present draft was written by Fable 5.2. Two of the assistants
share a model family or identifier with a participant in the games. They worked under different
prompts and tasks. Assistants are not authors.
The analysis reads the saved records directly. The offer ledger and debt episodes were rebuilt
from decisions.jsonl and events.jsonl and agree with the ledgers of the previous manuscript on
every compared field. Quotations are generated from the stored fields, and the build fails if a span
no longer matches. Three kinds of evidence are inherited from earlier stages of the project and were
not redone: the engine-executed payment sequences for three eliminations and the menu search for
a fourth, the two stored replay comparisons, and the model-assisted reason codes for 74 rejections.
No game, engine run, replay, baseline, synthetic state or model query was made for this paper.
