Planning and Bargaining in a Persistent Economy:
An Observational Study of LLM Agents
Abstract
An economic action changes what its author and com-
petitors can do next. We examine how LLM agents de-
scribe those changes and act on them in twenty com-
plete four-player Monopoly games. The corpus connects
920 negotiation responses and 188 financing episodes
to delivered information, recorded statements and ex-
ecuted actions. Sixteen debts require mortgages made
available by earlier building sales. Fourteen are paid; thir-
teen successful responses state the additional financing
or a budget including it before acting, and one consid-
ers it tentatively. The two failures omit that collateral
from their stated calculations and declare bankruptcy
immediately. A detailed sequence realizes an announced
liquidation budget across ten actions and leaves the pre-
dicted $14. Bargaining presents a less direct relationship
between statements and outcomes. Agents explicitly dis-
cuss rival construction and creditor inheritance, but a ret-
rospective rejection contrast changes substantially with
proposer composition. Eight contextual incidents doc-
ument refusal, pricing, bluffing, factual correction, se-
lective presentation, financial-denial appeals, assurance
withdrawal and successful payment. We distinguish ex-
pressed anticipation, factual assessment and subsequent
execution from bargaining effectiveness. The findings
describe four interacting configurations under unequal
response conditions and supply evidence of local finan-
cial planning alongside a complete account of their ne-
gotiation behavior.
1
Introduction
A player owes $1,400 and holds $49 in cash. Its prop-
erties can cover the obligation, but some mortgages are
unavailable until buildings have been sold. The player
announces a liquidation budget, sells its development,
mortgages eight deeds and pays, leaving the predicted
$14. Another player facing a smaller debt counts only
the mortgages currently offered, overlooks collateral re-
leased by sales, and declares bankruptcy. These episodes
pose a concrete question about LLM planning: does an
agent anticipate how its actions change the resources
available to it, and does its subsequent behavior realize
that assessment?
The same question becomes strategic when the re-
sources belong to someone else. Selling a deed can fund
the seller’s development while completing the buyer’s
monopoly. Withholding it preserves a blocker but for-
goes cash. Financing a distressed rival can postpone a
transfer to a stronger creditor. In these decisions, the
relevance of an asset depends on ownership, available
finance and what each participant can do next. A plau-
sible account of those consequences is evidence of an
expressed assessment. Its accuracy, realization and value
require further evidence.
We study twenty complete MonopolyBench games
played by four configured LLM systems. The records
connect 6,989 decisions to delivered information, sub-
mitted actions and state changes. Our analysis covers
all 920 negotiation responses and all 188 debt episodes
requiring explicit financing. We use the complete popu-
lations to situate selected trajectories, and the trajectories
to examine prospective statements alongside their out-
comes. The scientific object is economic behavior in an
interacting system: how agents describe opportunities,
negotiate over them and execute local financial plans.
The clearest statement–action correspondence occurs
when payment requires a change in the available collat-
eral. Sixteen debts cannot be covered by current cash, all
building salvage and the mortgages listed initially. Four-
teen are paid after sales release additional mortgages.
The initial text in thirteen successful cases states the ad-
ditional finance or a budget including it; one considers it
tentatively. Both failed cases omit released collateral in
their calculations and immediately declare bankruptcy.
This is a localized observation of prospective assessment
followed by dependent actions. It gives more informa-
tion about planning than a payment count alone, while
leaving the internal computation and the quality of asset
selection unresolved.
Bargaining has a different evidential structure. Its im-
mediate choices are observable, but the value of accept-
ing or rejecting a trade depends on future play. Opening
swaps completing only their proposer receive 95/116
rejections, compared with 37/72 completing neither par-
ticipant. The difference falls substantially when the pro-
poser population changes, and the corresponding cash-
bid comparison points in the other direction. Separately,
agents explicitly invoke rival construction, financing and
creditor succession in their reasons and messages. We
1

report these as complementary observations rather than
treating the reasons as an explanation of the aggregate
contrast.
Eight contextual incidents show the range of conduct
behind the counts. They include refusing one’s own
monopoly because of the rival’s stated opportunity, pric-
ing a completing deed, bluffing about a limit, correcting
a false pitch, selectively describing financing, seeking col-
lective financial denial, withdrawing an assurance and
carrying out a payment plan. Quotations identify their
channels and are connected to delivered messages and
subsequent events. The environment expressly permits
public deception; these observations characterize tactics
under that instruction rather than estimating a rate of
instruction violations.
Our contributions are a complete account of the en-
countered negotiation and financing decisions; an analy-
sis of financing dependencies linking prospective state-
ments to executed payment; and contextual evidence of
how strategic communication operates over persistent
economic stakes. The design supports those observa-
tional claims. It does not provide an independent value
reference for trades or a comparison of underlying model
intelligence. The five seed blocks, response conditions
and retrospective analysis choices are made explicit so
that the scope of each finding is visible.
The paper first defines the observed systems and mea-
surement populations, then presents financial planning,
bargaining and conduct. Its discussion connects these
findings to the motivating questions about strategic rea-
soning, opponent modeling, negotiation, memory, adap-
tation, economic judgment, collective behavior and eval-
uation validity. The appendix retains the full question
map, episode and offer tables, prompts and artifact entry
points.

2.1
A persistent competitive economy
Four players begin with $1,500 on the forty-space US
Monopoly board. They buy properties, bid in ascending
auctions following declined purchases, negotiate bilat-
eral trades, and develop complete color groups. Nego-
tiation supports proposals, counters, acceptance and re-
fusal, with up to twenty exchanges per thread. Deeds,
cash and jail cards can change hands. A developed group
cannot be traded. All monetary amounts denote game
currency unless explicitly labeled USD.
Development requires complete ownership, sufficient
cash, eligible mortgage status, even construction and
bank stock. A player unable to pay an obligation en-
ters a financing phase. The engine lists available sales,

2.2
Cohort and response conditions
Five seeds each receive four cyclic seat rotations of one
roster. Each system occupies every seat five times but fol-
lows the same opponent in the cycle. Games last 75–386
turns and end in bankruptcy without a turn cap. The
corpus contains 50,918 events, 6,989 decisions and 8,679
attempts. Shared seeds organize comparable initial con-
ditions; actions can change the subsequent assignment of
random draws. Appendix F provides seeds, source com-
mits and the G1–G20 artifact map. Decision identifiers
are zero-based.
Players receive current holdings, cash, mortgages,
buildings, bank stock, model names, a twenty-entry pub-
lic timeline and up to ten own notes. Current offer terms
and thread history accompany negotiation responses.
Purchase prompts contain the landed deed’s price, group,
construction cost and rents. Other phases supply less
board-value information, and the system prompt does
not enumerate the house variants. An agent’s board-
value calculation may therefore use recalled rather than
currently supplied information.
An ordinary invalid first attempt receives one correc-
2

tive request before deterministic fallback. A first-attempt
refusal instead consumes the second attempt in action-
only recovery. Opus has 1,604 first-attempt refusals and
one later refusal, all with the same provider message
about reverse engineering or duplicating outputs. No
recovery response is refused. Recovery removes the
private-note request while retaining permission to de-
ceive. First attempts are universally refused in G5–G20,
with the transition already occurring during G1–G4 by
wall clock. Opus then has public state and history but
usually no authored private note or further corrective
attempt. The historical gateway records expose no struc-
tured refusal category.
Grok’s recorded source configuration requires a tool
call, whereas the other systems use automatic tool choice.
Its received call conflicts with the action named in assis-
tant content in 139 selected responses, including 64 of its
136 thread openings. This is a plausible setting to investi-
gate, not an established cause of those conflicts. A histori-
cal formatter also misattributes some trade summaries in
2,054 initial prompts. Current holdings and all 920 deliv-
ered offer terms agree with the acceptance menus. These
conditions are part of the interacting systems studied,
since one participant’s offers become another’s inputs.
2.3
Observation and analysis
An offer is one set of terms awaiting a response; a counter
changes its author without changing the thread’s initia-
tor. We evaluate newly completed color groups after
the proposed transfer. Completion can include mort-
gaged deeds. Opening and later responses are separated.
Swaps transfer deeds in both directions; cash bids request
deeds for cash; cash asks offer deeds for cash; other forms
retain their own category. Every completion category is
reported, including zero cells.
A financing episode starts at an explicit debt menu and
ends at payment or elimination. Debts paid directly from
adequate cash are outside this population. At entry, let D
be the debt, C cash, S the salvage value of all buildings,
M0 the sum of initially offered mortgages and M∗the
sum for all unmortgaged owned deeds. We use
U0 = C + S + M0,
U∗= C + S + M∗.
(1)
If U∗< D, own assets cannot pay even under the gener-
ous bound. If U0 < D ≤U∗, payment requires mortgages
unavailable initially. A sufficient bound alone does not
prove a legal sequence. Actual payments establish feasi-
bility for successful episodes; previously executed legal
sequences establish three additional payable declarations.
A separate historical search resolves one menu-restricted
case.
Counts describe this cohort. The bargaining contrasts
and incidents were selected retrospectively. We report
proposer and seed-block composition and enumerate
all 55 = 3, 125 draws of five whole seed blocks with re-
placement. The central 95% of this empirical distribution
measures sensitivity to reweighting the observed blocks;
it is not a selection-adjusted confidence interval. We also
leave out each block in turn. No independent-trial model
is applied to individual offers.
Two earlier LLM-assisted readings classified seventy-
four refusals involving recipient completion; the second
had partial prior exposure. Their agreement is consis-
tency of reading rather than human validation. Initial
texts in the sixteen necessary-collateral episodes receive
source-linked model-assisted reading as well. Returned
explanations are evidence of expressed considerations,
not privileged access to internal computation [6, 9]. Each
incident distinguishes its quoted channel, verified receipt
when applicable, and the executed continuation.
3
Anticipating and executing finan-
cial dependencies
3.1
A complete account of encountered debts
Of 188 episodes requiring explicit financing, 128 end in
payment and sixty in elimination. Payment entails 180
mortgages and 89 building-sale actions to meet $51,997
in obligations. Sixty-seven payments require one action
and sixty-one require two to ten. No successful payment
uses an engine fallback. All four configurations complete
multi-action sequences, although their encountered obli-
gations differ.
Historical feasibility checks identify three further
payable debts that end in declaration. Thus 128 of 131
demonstrated-payable debts are paid. Fifty-six other
eliminations exceed the generous own-asset bound. The
remaining case, G7 Opus, has $1,310 in nominal finance
against a $750 debt but only $440 reachable under the
supplied menu. The empty bank house stock leaves no
offered hotel sale. This distinguishes asset accounting
from the available sequence, rather than asserting impos-
sibility under every Monopoly implementation.
The payment fraction provides the population context.
Its interpretation changes when we ask which resources
the debts require. Sixty-two payable episodes can be
covered with current cash and initial mortgages. Fifty-
three additionally need sales but do not require newly
released mortgages; fifty-two are paid. The remaining
sixteen need mortgages unlocked by sales; fourteen are
paid. Figure 1 displays this partition. These categories
characterize financing requirements, not calibrated levels
of task difficulty.

3.2
Prospective assessment when initial op-
tions are insufficient
In nineteen successful payments, agents sell buildings
and then use one or more of 33 mortgages absent from
the first menu. Fourteen of these payments meet the ne-
cessity condition U0 < D ≤U∗. Five use newly released
collateral despite having another arithmetic source of
sufficient finance. Necessity and use answer different
questions: the former identifies dependence on changed
options, while the latter describes the observed route.
The sixteen necessity episodes comprise nine private-
note successes, five Opus successes with provider-
returned explanations, and two private-note failures.
Thirteen successful initial responses state the subsequent
mortgages or an aggregate budget including them. In
G20 d291, Opus instead asks whether clearing the pink
houses and mortgaging their deeds could close the gap.
It then carries out payment. These prospective state-
ments add evidence beyond the fact that an agent even-
tually follows refreshed menus. They remain recorded
statements, not a measurement of the computation pro-
ducing the action.
Both failures declare at the first menu. In G12 d190,
Sol totals $53 cash, $225 salvage and $130 in listed mort-
gages, obtaining $408 against $500 owed. Selling the red
buildings also releases $340 in mortgages, which the sum
omits. In G17 d234, Gemini totals $500 salvage and a $30
Baltic mortgage against $550 owed, omitting $375 in re-
leased dark-blue collateral. The same systems succeed in
other necessity episodes: Sol pays two of three and Gem-
ini five of six; Opus pays five of five and Grok two of two.
These are two omissions within a population contain-
ing successful dependency assessment, not a universal
boundary of either model.

3.3
Investment and subsequent ownership
Finance also determines what an agreement becomes.
Sol and Gemini build in the first management turn after
trade completion on 16/17 and 17/18 groups; Opus and
Grok do so on 7/15 and 3/13. The twenty other windows
include nine groups with no build option for that group,
four engine defaults, four calls conflicting with content
and three other valid ends. A missing current build op-
tion can coexist with a possible redemption-and-build
sequence. The categories therefore distinguish offered
actions, stated financing choices and execution rather
than labeling every non-build a preference to wait.
These ownership histories provide the stakes of bar-
gaining. Trade-completed groups collect $57,378 before
loss or game end, with $53,810 collected by groups built
in the first window. Receipts reflect development, sub-
sequent landings, holding time and selection; they are
not incremental investment returns. Thirty-eight of forty
nonterminal eliminations follow rent on the creditor’s de-
veloped color property. Estates then move 227 deeds and
complete 52 groups during play. A further 69 comple-
tions occur only at final elimination. The later incidents
use these pathways to explain what participants describe
as worth financing or preventing.
4
Bargaining about another player’s
opportunities
4.1
Responses and the composition of offers
Across 526 negotiation threads, players accept 113 offers,
counter 394 and reject 413. Acceptance is legal in 918 of
the 920 response opportunities. Sol and Opus counter
most frequently, whereas Gemini and Grok reject most
frequently. Each nevertheless accepts between 26 and
30 offers. Those similar settlement totals emerge from
different ways of responding to disagreement.
Table 2 reports every opening-offer form and comple-
tion category. Among swaps, offers completing only
the proposer receive 95/116 rejections, compared with
37/72 completing neither participant. Both-completing
swaps receive 34/48 rejections. Pure cash bids give a
different estimate: 42/70 proposer-only bids and 67/103
neither-completing bids are rejected. The latter differ-
ence is negative in this cohort, but its uncertainty does
not establish a zero or negative population effect. Later
swaps show much smaller differences, 8/39 versus 7/34.
They are selected by the preceding negotiation.
Proposer composition matters to the opening-swap
difference. Grok writes 36 of the 72 neither-completing
swaps and fifteen of their seventeen acceptances. Thir-
teen of those accepted offers originate in calls whose
assistant content names a different action. Table 3 shows
that each observed proposer stratum has a positive rejec-
tion difference, but the comparison cells vary greatly in
size. Removing Grok-authored offers changes the pooled
difference from 30.5 to 16.7 percentage points. Removing
only threads opened by conflicting calls instead gives
93/112 versus 26/43, a 22.6-point difference. These ex-
clusions describe different populations; neither recreates
play under a corrected interface.
Across the five seed blocks, the unfiltered opening-
swap difference stays positive. The whole-block resam-
pling range is 24.2–35.5 points, but removing Grok au-
thors changes it to −3.8–31.9. Leaving one block out gives
28.7–33.3 and 9.4–22.1 respectively. Figure 2 shows the
observations underlying these descriptions. The smaller
filtered estimate is sensitive to which observed blocks
receive weight. Neither range adjusts for selecting the
comparison after inspecting the corpus.
These comparisons leave the mechanism open. Cash
and property compensation, the proposer’s identity and
the recipient’s portfolio differ across offers. Board list
prices cannot resolve that problem because they omit
complementarity and blocking value. We therefore use
the record to establish the pattern of responses and ex-
amine strategic considerations directly in their own pop-
ulations.
4.2
Explicit consideration of rival develop-
ment
The separate semantic sample consists of seventy-four
rejections of offers completing the recipient’s group. Two
LLM-assisted readings agree that 61 name a trade-linked
consequence for the rival: 27/38 recipient-only and 34/36
both-completing offers. These populations are disjoint
from the proposer-only/neither comparison above. They
document expressed consideration of the rival, not an
explanation for the swap/cash difference. Gemini and
Grok contribute 56 of the agreed cases; G1 and G15 con-
tribute 31. The concentration and reader identities matter
when interpreting how representative those statements
are.

5
Communication over persistent fi-
nancial stakes
5.1
Bargaining limits and changing cash
needs
Cash affects both the willingness to transact and what a
transaction enables. Among proposer-only completion
requests, holders below $100 reject 17/69; holders at $300
or more reject 120/175. Most low-cash responses are
counters, 44/69. The pooled difference includes later
negotiations and Opus’s response style. Of the 69 low-
cash offers, 43 are later offers and 21 face Opus. Gemini
remains resistant even at low cash, rejecting ten of eleven
such requests. There is no uniform threshold at which a
holder releases a rival’s completing deed.
Longer histories show positions and terms changing
together. Fourteen completing transfers follow an ear-
lier refusal or counter by the same holder to the same
prospective completer. Pairing settlement with the first
earlier resistance on a previous turn, cash falls in thir-
teen, from a median $571.50 to $82. But the net cash
offered rises in eight and only five pairs preserve the
same reciprocal deed bundle. These histories document
the course of negotiation, not an effect attributable solely
to liquidity.

Funding a rival to preserve the competi-

Ownership makes a rival’s survival relevant to other play-

holder to seeing it inherited by a stronger creditor. Eco-
nomic theories of sales with identity-dependent external-

nonbuyers [5]. The following incident supplies explicit
statements about that consequence, without establishing
equilibrium behavior.

nicative acts with different evidence. G7 juxtaposes a
public limit and a private contingency; G13 explicitly
describes an omission; G19 corrects a false premise while
accepting the terms; G20 seeks collective financial denial;
G18 changes an assurance. Their effects on the other
players’ beliefs or on winning are not interchangeable
with the acts themselves. No population deception rate
is estimated from this selected set.
6
What the observations establish
Anticipation has an observable temporal structure.
The financing cases link an initial assessment to actions
that change later options. The necessary-collateral sub-
set makes the relationship especially clear: a mortgage
absent from the first menu must become available for
payment to succeed. Initial statements include that re-
source in thirteen successful cases and consider it in one;
both failed calculations omit it. This is positive evidence
of expressed anticipation and local execution. It does
not require treating returned text as a transparent record
of internal reasoning, or assuming that a long sequence
was planned without help from intermediate menus.
Local goals and strategic value are different evalua-
tion problems.
Paying a specified debt supplies a di-
rectly checkable endpoint. Bargaining asks whether an
exchange is worth making over uncertain future play.
The latter depends on rivals’ holdings, development,
survival and subsequent behavior. G15 records a rival-
development objection; G2 combines the same concern
with a quantitative error; G19 corrects a pitch and accepts
the terms. These observations distinguish expressed con-
sideration, factual accuracy and action consistency. An
outcome such as settlement or victory cannot replace a
reference value for whether the decision was effective.
The social behavior is economically situated.
The con-
duct spans bluffing, omission, assurance withdrawal and
financial-denial appeals. In each case, a statement con-
cerns a concrete resource or opportunity. G13’s appeal
depends on a third party inheriting the yellows. G18
instead concerns prolonging the last opponent’s survival.
G7 links a sale to two competing construction programs.
Reading the continuation makes those acts interpretable
without converting selected cases into a population mis-
conduct rate. The prompt permits deception, so an align-
9

ment judgment would additionally require a norm the
behavior violates.
The interacting system is the unit of observation.
A
provider refusal changes the participant’s note and retry
path. A conflicting call introduces an offer that another
agent may accept. An inaccurate trade summary be-
comes context for later choices. These conditions propa-
gate through the game, so filtering one participant’s rows
does not produce an unaffected control group. They are
necessary conditions on the interpretation of the behav-
ior, while the executed transactions remain real observa-
tions of this cohort.
The broader research questions receive explicit an-
swers in Appendix C. The records demonstrate exam-
ples of prospective financial assessment, rival-focused
bargaining language and conditional communication.
They contain some persistent targets and revised offers,
but do not measure memory benefit, durable learning,
calibrated theory of mind or transfer to unfamiliar oppo-
nents. There is no independent general-capability axis
for relating general intelligence to multi-agent perfor-
mance. These questions remain open under the present
design.

Related work
Negotiation agents have long been evaluated through
both language and outcomes. Lewis et al. [7] study multi-
issue bargaining with private rewards and human ne-
gotiation data. He et al. [3] separate strategic decisions
from natural-language generation. Those settings offer
explicit utility measures, whereas an offer’s value in a
complete economic game depends on future interaction.
Our distinction between stated assessment and effective-
ness follows that established separation.
Guhe and Lascarides [2] use controlled Catan simu-
lations to study when persuasion and trade restrictions
improve game outcomes. The strategic idea of withhold-
ing trades is therefore not new. Economic models of
identity-dependent externalities likewise explain why an
asset’s buyer can matter to other players [5]. We use this
perspective to organize the observed motives, without
claiming to estimate an equilibrium strategy or a causal
benefit of withholding.
AgentMonopoly compares LLM models and persona
instructions through complete games [10]. FinBoard-
Bench studies dynamic financial decisions across Cash-
flow, Acquire and Monopoly, including liquidity man-
agement and negotiation [4]. Our contribution is not a
new claim that LLMs can play Monopoly. It is the connec-
tion between complete negotiation and debt populations,
recorded prospective assessments and the particular eco-
nomic actions they precede.
The Vending-Bench Arena communication study clas-
sifies inter-agent messages using simulator state and
logged text, with replication across judge families [8].
Its population-level communication analysis differs from
our purposively selected incidents. We retain contex-
tual transcripts because they make the public claim, pri-
vate or provider text, recipient response and continua-
tion inspectable. Our explicit permission to deceive also
makes the normative comparison task-dependent. Work
on explanation faithfulness further motivates separating
recorded explanations from internal computation [6, 9].
8
Limitations
Twenty games span five seeds, one cyclic opponent order
and no repeated seed/rotation cells. They cannot sep-
arate system, seating, random exposure and opponent
effects. Wins are 8/7/3/2 for Sol/Gemini/Opus/Grok
and 7/7/3/3 by seat. One seed block is won by Sol from
every seat; another is won from seat two by every system.
Neither observation establishes a causal decomposition
of skill and luck.
Contrasts, cash thresholds and incidents were selected
retrospectively. Empirical block reweighting does not cor-
rect selection or validate generalization from five blocks.
The sixteen necessary-collateral cases also form a small
retrospective subset with only two failures. There is no
evaluated reference policy, counterfactual trade value, in-
dependent human annotation or intervention on prompt,
memory, communication or response handling. Con-
sequently the paper makes no intrinsic model ranking,
effectiveness claim for a tactic or inference about internal
reasoning. The shared source trail supports the historical
observations; it does not remove these design limits.
9
Conclusion
In twenty complete games, LLM agents make prospective
statements about finance and rival opportunities, and
those statements sometimes align closely with later ac-
tion. Fourteen of sixteen debts requiring newly released
collateral are paid, with initial texts anticipating the re-
source in thirteen cases and considering it in one. Bar-
gaining shows explicit rival concern alongside a rejection
pattern sensitive to offer form and proposer composition.
The contextual incidents demonstrate how bluffing, selec-
tive presentation, correction and financial denial operate
over persistent economic stakes. Together, the records
support an observational account of local planning and
strategic interaction, with effectiveness and generaliza-
tion left to designs that can measure them.

Rules and historical validation
Bank stock is 32 houses and twelve hotels. Construction is checked entry by entry. Mortgages on a developed group
become available only after development is removed. The sale menu identifies highest-built deeds, and a hotel
downgrade requires four bank houses. In G7, the supplied menu therefore contains no building sale. Reading the
separate plan validator shows it can accept a broader bundled group sale if offered. The historical result concerns the
actual action menu.
Mortgage redemption and transferred-deed interest use floating-point ceilings. For five mortgage principals,
redemption exceeds the exact ten-percent charge by one dollar; transfer interest is exact across board principals. No
management prompt directly supplies the redemption cost. An agent quoting the exact ten-percent figure need not
have made an arithmetic error.
The earlier executable audit covers sixty eliminations: fifty-six insufficient generous bounds, three positive payment
sequences and one restricted menu search. It also covers sixty-six construction defaults, including sixty-one Opus
allocations executable after legal reordering without changing purchases. These are prior engine-executed results;
the present descriptive scripts read their retained reports.
All twenty stored replay reports pass two comparisons. Artifact replay compares the full event stream with an
empty excluded-event list. State replay removes four observational LLM event types and normalizes identifiers and
timestamps. The complete-stream comparison covers 50,918 events; the recorded clock uses a deterministic start and
step. These checks establish reproduction under the frozen implementation, not independent agreement with an
intended Monopoly rules specification.

Prompts, response handling and cost
The prompts below are verbatim stored examples from a G1 refusal/recovery chain. Recovery retains permission to
deceive while removing the private-note requirement. Its zero refusals over 1,604 calls are historical response-path
evidence, not a randomized ablation.
Standard system prompt
You are an autonomous player in a game of Monopoly competing against other AI players.
Your goal is to win the game by maximizing long-term advantage and being the last
non-bankrupt player. You may use any legal strategy or personality (aggressive,
deceptive, cooperative, conservative, manipulative, opportunistic, friendly, hostile,
etc.) and may adapt dynamically as the game evolves.
You will receive the following inputs:
1) System Prompt (this message): authoritative instructions.
2) Game State: full board/game context + memory (public chat, recent actions, your
private thoughts).
3) Action State: exact dynamic context for the current decision and legal action
schemas.
### Action Rules
- Make exactly one tool call per decision.
- Use only tools listed as legal for that decision.
- Never invent tools, arguments, or targets.
- Obey the provided argument schema exactly.
- If a tool requires no arguments, pass none.
### Messages
Each action must include both fields:
- Public Message: visible to other players. Use it to negotiate, bluff, deceive,
cooperate, intimidate, joke, or stay silent — whatever best serves your strategy.
- Private Thoughts: visible only to you. Use it to reason honestly, track strategy,
analyze opponents, and leave notes for your future self. Be concise but clear.
- Keep both messages token-efficient and concise.
### Strategy Guidance
- Play strategically with long-term outcomes in mind.
- Observe opponents and adapt.
- Deception in public chat is allowed; private thoughts should reflect true reasoning.
- Be consistent unless there is a reason to change.
- Prefer concise reasoning and communication.
Your role is not to explain rules or debug the system.
Your role is to **play Monopoly and win**.
When a decision is presented, think carefully and make one legal tool call that best
advances your chances of winning.
