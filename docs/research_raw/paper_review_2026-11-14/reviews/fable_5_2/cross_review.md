Agreement among three language models that read the same records is not independent confirmation in the statistical sense. It does mean the arithmetic and code reading were done three times with the same result.

## 2. Where my opinion has changed

Each item states what I wrote, what I now hold, and why.

**2.1 The scope and form of the sufficiency claim.** I wrote that the statement is "a property of the rules" for 108 hotel-free payable debts and that I made no claim about the other 23. Sol's cross-review sets the argument out as a conditional proof with explicit premises and an invariant, which is better than my version. Astra and Sol both limit it to the sixteen and say a claim about all payable states still needs a proof.

I now hold a wider and more exact statement. I checked the premises at entry for all 131 payable debts. Every developed group is completely owned and even in all 131, and U* covers the debt in all 131. Three sufficient conditions then cover 129 of them.

| Condition | Why no continuing sequence can end unpaid | Payable debts |
|---|---|---|
| No hotel at entry | Some building is always sellable until a group is clear, and stock only rises | 108 |
| Cash plus initially listed mortgages cover the debt | A listed mortgage stays listed until used. A state with nothing offered has cash of at least C + M0, so the engine has already paid | 12 more |
| Bank houses at entry are at least four per hotel held | Every downgrade is affordable in the worst order | 9 more |

The two not covered are G3 d456 (two hotels, no bank houses) and G3 d461 (two hotels, six bank houses). Gemini paid both. I did not analyze them further. All sixteen necessity debts are covered.

So Astra's sentence that a claim about all 131 "needs either a proof covering those states ... or an executed reference policy" can be answered for 129 by proof. This is still an argument from source and recorded holdings. It is not a measured baseline.

**2.2 The order of note and action.** I wrote that within a response "the note is written after the action is chosen", because the action is the function name and the note is usually the last argument. Sol's point is that field order does not establish cognitive order. I accept a narrower correction. The stored record gives the order of keys inside the argument string. It does not record whether the function name was generated before the arguments. I now hold only this. The note and the first action come from one response, the record cannot show the note came first, and so the note is not a separately elicited forecast. I withdraw the claim that it was written after the choice.

**2.3 The middle row of Table 2.** I recommended relabeling the row or adding a sentence. With 47 observed routes, one stored witness and five routes derived three times independently, the label "released mortgages not necessary" is supportable for this corpus. I now recommend keeping the label and adding the justification. This matches Astra's and Sol's revised position.

**2.4 How to describe the carried figures.** I called them "partial inventories". Astra and Sol call them conditional inventories and note that each figure was correct for the condition under which it was written. That is the more accurate description and I adopt it. My counts stand.

**2.5 The restoration-cost metric.** I called it a contestable objective that would "probably be a null". Sol's accounting shows it can be defined coherently as a net penalty of half the replacement cost of buildings sold plus a tenth of new mortgage principal, with gross restoration spending a different quantity. I accept that it is definable. I also accept Sol's caution that a median near zero would not show saturation. My remaining view is narrower. The metric's relation to winning is unestablished, and the records suggest the bulk of natural states would score near zero. See 3.5.

**2.6 Two details I had not connected.** Sol notes that Opus at G17 d254 says five houses cannot be sold in one call because of evenness, when the validator checks only the final state, and that Gemini at G18 d309 sold a deed in a bundle that the menu did not list. I had read both texts and missed what they show about the gap between the menu, the validator and the agents' beliefs. I adopt both observations. The first also fits section 3.3 below.

**2.7 Things I could not confirm that a peer did.** Sol read the FinBoardBench thinking table as 4 of 36 against 10 of 36, which I could not read cleanly. Sol read the Catan paper and found that recipients were forced to comply with embargo requests. I have not verified either and take them as Sol's.

## 3. Where I challenge the shared judgment

Astra and Sol converge on one formulation. The paper's best contribution is the separation among available resources, expressed assessment, applied action and eventual payment, and the best addition from existing data is a first-response table for all 188 entries with a fixed codebook. I agree with much of this and disagree on five points.

**3.1 Payment is not a separate accomplishment.** Both reviews accept the sixteen-state proof and then keep "final payment" or "eventual payment" as a dimension to be scored beside recognition and arithmetic. With the proof covering 129 of 131 payable debts, payment is implied by never declaring. All three payable declarations happened at the first menu. A matrix column for payment repeats the column for the first action.

This matters for how the paper is built, and neither review draws the consequence. The finance section should be organized around the one choice that varies, which is the first-menu choice, and around the text that accompanies it. "Separable accomplishments" is a framework. The finding should be a statement over the complete population. Mine is that the applied first action agrees with the asset bound in 179 of 188 episodes, and that six of the nine exceptions are one system's calls contradicting the action named in the same response.

**3.2 The statement and action divergences are almost all one system's response inconsistency.** Astra and Sol describe the six above-bound cases well and say they belong in a paper about statements and actions. Neither gives the population count. Across all 338 liquidation decisions there are nine named-action conflicts and all nine are Grok. Only Grok's assistant content names an action, so the check cannot see such a conflict in another system.

The consequence is uncomfortable for the title. In liquidation, where the paper has ground truth, the cases in which statement and action come apart are a property of one configuration's output. That is a result about an instrument condition and should be reported as one. It does not support a general account of plans and actions. I accept Sol's wording that this is a response inconsistency or channel conflict and not a resolver failure.

**3.3 The fee belief is the published rule.** Astra calls Gemini's reasoning at G18 d344 "a mistaken consequence of liquidation". Sol says the charge "is absent from the engine's bankruptcy routine" and that some stated motives "are false under the frozen implementation". Both are true of the engine. Neither says that the official rules impose exactly that charge. I read the Hasbro text. The new owner of a mortgaged deed "must at once pay the Bank the amount of interest on the loan". Gemini applied the rulebook.

This changes the interpretation in three ways.

- It is the same behavior as the successes. The release rule is also a rulebook rule, and there the engine agrees.
- It gives the corpus a natural instance of the "unstated rule" condition that one referee proposes to build. A keyword screen finds the inherited-interest belief in eight liquidation decisions, seven of them Grok.
- It exposes a gap in the manuscript. The paper cites the rulebook for the release rule and lists the engine's variants as plain rules. At least four of them depart from that rulebook in liquidation. One is the hotel sale. The rulebook says hotels "may be sold at once". The engine offers a hotel sale only when the bank holds four houses. G7's obstruction, which the paper presents as the difference between asset accounting and reachable finance, exists only under the engine's variant.

**3.4 The conditional-inventory hypothesis should not be ranked first.** Astra's recommendations call it "the strongest candidate from the current corpus". Sol's cross-review already objects to privileging it, and I side with Sol. I add the count that neither review gives. Among the eleven necessity episodes with an applied note, a carried conditional inventory precedes payment four times (G5, G13, G18 d313, G20 d327) and declaration twice. Astra and Sol name three of the four. The fourth is Grok at G18 d313, whose turn-90 note says a $700 rent is "unpaid even after selling all 5 houses ($500)".

Both failed sums equal U0, and U0 can be computed from the menu alone. A memory term adds nothing that "counted what the menu shows" does not already predict for these two cases. A discovery paper should state the equality and list the candidate explanations without an order.

**3.5 The 188-entry semantic table is not the highest-value addition.** Both reviews put it first. I would put three things ahead of it because they need no semantic judgment.

1. The sufficiency paragraph with its premises and coverage.
2. The named-action conflict count.
3. A numeric check on first texts. Does the text state a total, and does that total equal U0, U*, or neither? This can be verified against the record by arithmetic. In the sixteen it already separates the cases. Six totals equal U* and two equal U0.

A model-coded table of 188 first responses would be the paper's third unvalidated semantic count, after 61 of 74 and thirteen plus one. If it is built, the codebook should prefer criteria of the third kind.

On the metric, I add evidence neither review has. Under a mortgage-first cost ordering, buildings were sold in 2 of the 62 paid episodes where listed mortgages alone sufficed, and no paid episode that needed a sale sold $100 or more beyond the minimum left after listed mortgages. The comparison is to a no-release benchmark and not to a searched optimum, so it does not bound regret. It does suggest that an oracle built first would mostly report zeros on natural states.

## 5. The new financial-memory precedents

Astra's cross-review adds three sources and Sol checked them. I read one myself.

- **MemTrial**, arXiv 2610.11732v2 of 11 October 2026. An experience-learning agent for portfolio allocation. PlantedMem keeps real drafts and imposes a synthetic shift on their weights for useful, neutral and misleading experiences. Nothing plants a number in an agent's notes and nothing involves a decision to stop. My reading agrees with Astra's and Sol's. I covered the first 100,000 characters.
- **The anchoring paper** in the Journal of Behavioral and Experimental Finance. The DOI redirected and returned no content to me. I have not read it.
- **"Memory and Generative AI"**. Not opened by me.

I agree with the conclusion both draw. These narrow any broad claim that financial memory effects are unstudied, and none tests the specific idea of a carried conditional inventory leading to a wrong terminal choice. A targeted search is not a survey.

## 6. Position after cross-review

1. In this engine, for 129 of 131 payable debts, any player who keeps choosing an offered financing action pays. This follows from source and recorded holdings and was not run.
2. The decision that varies is the first-menu choice. By applied action it agrees with the asset bound in 179 of 188 episodes.
3. Six of the nine exceptions are Grok calls that contradict the action named in the same response. The other three are two false infeasibility declarations and one deliberate mortgage that applies a rulebook rule the engine lacks.
4. Both false declarations state a total equal to U0. Thirteen of the fourteen successful first texts include the released resource, six state a total equal to U*, and three state a remainder the route then meets to the dollar. One response produces both text and action.
5. Agents act on the published rules. The engine agrees with those rules on the release of mortgages and departs from them on bankruptcy, inherited mortgages and hotel sales.
6. The bargaining contrast does not survive proposer composition and call conflicts.
7. No mechanism is identified. Menu-visible inventory, a carried conditional inventory, rule application and response variability all fit the two failures.

I hold items 1, 2, 3 and 5 more firmly than before. Item 4 is weaker in one respect, since I no longer claim the note followed the choice.
