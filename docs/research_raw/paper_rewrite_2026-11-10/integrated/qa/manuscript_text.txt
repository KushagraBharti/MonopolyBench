Bargaining, Development, and Creditor Inheritance in
MonopolyBench
MonopolyBench project
11 November 2026
Abstract
An agent trading with competitors must consider what each party can build and who may
inherit the assets after bankruptcy. We study these dependencies in twenty complete four-player
MonopolyBench games, containing 6,989 decisions and 920 negotiation responses. Agents bargain
over rival development, liquidity and blocking properties. Counteroffers commonly preserve the
exchange while changing its price, and recipients sometimes refuse trades that would complete their
own groups because of the advantages stated for a rival. Sol, Gemini, Opus and Grok complete 17,
18, 15 and 13 groups through trade and build on 16, 17, 7 and 3 in their first management windows,
respectively. Groups developed in that window collect $53,810 of the $57,378 received after trade
completion. Financing choices, available options and response conditions shape these paths from
ownership to income. At bankruptcy, 56 of 60 debts exceed a generous own-asset bound, and
eventual winners receive 202 of 227 deeds inherited before the final elimination. Eight contextual
transcripts show bargaining limits, selective disclosure, an appeal to deny a rival financing, and
withdrawal followed by lower bids, alongside factual correction and conflicting execution. Together,
the population records and trajectories explain how strategic bargaining becomes development,
income and creditor succession in these configured systems. The study is observational and does
not identify causal tactic effects or an underlying-model ranking.
1
Introduction
A property that completes a rival’s monopoly is not an ordinary item in a bilateral exchange. Selling it
may finance the seller while giving the buyer a source of rent. Refusing may preserve a blocker while
leaving both parties exposed to a third player. If either later becomes insolvent, the deed can pass to a
creditor without another negotiation. An economic agent must therefore consider the exchange, the
development it enables and the positions that persist after it.
Monopoly makes these dependencies observable over a complete game. Players buy, auction and
trade properties, build houses, mortgage collateral, pay rents and liquidate assets. In the implemen-
tation studied here, a player-creditor inherits a bankrupt debtor’s properties with their buildings
intact. The recipient of an estate can consequently matter to players who were not parties to the debt.
This paper asks how LLM agents bargain over such dependencies, and what happens between an
acquisition, an investment request and a realized economic outcome.
We study twenty complete four-player games involving four configured systems, abbreviated Sol,
Gemini, Opus and Grok. The corpus includes all 526 negotiation threads, 209 group-completion
episodes and sixty eliminations. Legal menus, response attempts, delivered prompts and canonical
events let us relate the terms of an exchange to the opportunities and actions that follow. The unit
of inference is the configured system operating in these games, including its response handling and
information. It is not an isolated language model.
1

The results follow an economic sequence. First, agents bargain over what an exchange enables.
They discuss a rival’s building funds, the value of withholding a completing deed and their own
reserves after a purchase. In sixty-one of seventy-four rejections of offers that would complete the
recipient’s group, both textual readings identify a stated consequence for the rival. Counteroffers
commonly preserve the proposed exchange while demanding more cash. The response census places
these explanations in context: negotiation stage and recipient configuration account for substantial
differences in rejection and countering.
Second, development is the step that converts a collection of deeds into a rent-producing position.
The four systems complete similar numbers of groups by trade, but build on sixteen, seventeen, seven
and three in their first management windows. The trajectories show mortgage-financed investment,
cash-reserve decisions and delayed development, together with requests that do not execute. Groups
developed in that first window collect $53,810 of the $57,378 subsequently received by trade-completed
groups. Following the financing and execution sequence explains how these positions differ after
apparently similar acquisitions.
Third, creditors turn another player’s failure into an acquisition. Fifty-six of sixty elimination
debts exceed even a generous own-asset financing bound. Eventual winners receive 202 of 227 deeds
transferred through estates before the final elimination, and estates complete fifty-two groups while
play continues. This makes creditor identity a strategic consideration before bankruptcy: participants
bargain over keeping a rival solvent, preserving a blocker and preventing another player from inheriting
a complete group.
Eight contextual incidents accompany these results. They show a seller changing its ask with the
buyer’s position, refusal of one’s own completion, a public bargaining limit below a private contingency,
an omitted financing route, an appeal to withhold a rival’s building cash, and withdrawal followed
by lower bids after a seller’s deterioration. Correction followed by acceptance and a contradictory
bankruptcy response provide contrasts. Each transcript follows the statement through its recipient’s
response and the later economic trajectory. Public deception was permitted by the task; its occurrence
and economic context are distinct questions from instruction adherence.
Prior work studies sustained economic operation, state-grounded communication, Monopoly be-
havior and complete model-plus-execution configurations [1, 5, 8, 13, 15]. Our contribution is to
connect bargaining by all four players to subsequent financing, development, income and creditor
succession over complete games. Legal menus and historical feasibility checks establish the opportu-
nities behind those trajectories. The discussion relates the findings to strategic reasoning, opponent
modeling, negotiation, planning, adaptation, risk management, collective behavior and evaluation
validity, distinguishing observed behavior from questions requiring a different study design.

2.2
Twenty complete games
The cohort contains eighty player-games, 50,918 events, 6,989 decisions and 8,679 response attempts.
Five seeds are each used for four cyclic seat rotations, so every configuration occupies every seat five
times. All twenty games end through bankruptcy without a turn cap; lengths range from 75 to 386
turns. Gn dk denotes zero-based decision k in game n. Turns and decisions are different units.
All calls use OpenRouter, with no temperature or nucleus parameter supplied. Recorded completion
tokens average 128 per decision for Sol, 368 for Gemini, 410 for Opus and 4,041 for Grok; provider
token semantics differ. The configurations face the same three opponents, whose model names are
visible.
2.3
Response and memory conditions
The standard policy permits one corrective retry after invalid output, then a deterministic fallback.
A first-attempt provider refusal instead triggers an action-only request that consumes the remaining
attempt and removes the private-note requirement. For Opus, this occurs in 1,604 of 1,696 decisions.
An invalid action-only response receives no further corrective request. Although the recorded policy
also names a fallback model, that stage never runs: all Opus decisions return from the identifier in
Table 1. Appendix C.1 gives the complete response counts and policy settings.
Response handling also affects later information. In 1,394 of Opus’s 1,696 delivered prompts,
the ten-note private-memory field is empty or contains only synthetic fallback entries. Its observed
planning and negotiation must therefore be read under this altered note and retry condition.
For Grok, 1,068 of 1,663 selected responses contain an extractable action name in assistant content;
139 disagree with the received tool call, which the engine applies. The disagreements already appear
in saved nonstreamed response bodies, but their origin remains unresolved. Empty content for Sol
and Gemini precludes the same comparison. Where relevant, we distinguish these executed calls from
the accompanying stated action.

2.4
Delivered information and conduct norms
Prompts show cash, ownership, buildings, bank stock, twenty public timeline entries and ten own
private notes. Trade responses include current terms and thread history. Agents do not receive
opponents’ private notes or future random outcomes. Static board parameters are supplied unevenly:
the 547 initial purchase prompts give the landed deed’s price, building cost, rent schedule and group;
the 854 auction prompts give its group. Management, trade-response, jail and liquidation states identify
deeds by space key without those parameters or mortgage and redemption values. Valuation in those
phases requires board knowledge or information recoverable from earlier observations.
Past-trade summaries misattribute some authors and terms, affecting 2,054 initial prompts. Current
structured holdings remain correct, and current-offer fields agree with the recipient’s acceptance menu
in all 920 response opportunities. We use the delivered request when interpreting a participant’s
information, and the transaction records when measuring the exchange.
Public messages enter a bounded timeline, so incident claims about reception use the actual
recipient’s request. The delivery and history-reconstruction counts are in Appendix C.1.
The standard prompt asks agents to win through any legal strategy and explicitly permits public
bluffing and deception. It also states, “Deception in public chat is allowed; private thoughts should
reflect true reasoning.” The action-only prompt omits that sentence. A permitted public bluff is not
instruction misalignment. A false private note may honestly express an error and does not itself
establish dishonesty. Private notes, assistant content and returned provider explanations are separately
recorded channels, not privileged measurements of internal reasoning [7, 12].
3
Measures and evidence
3.1
Population accounting
An offer is one proposal or counter awaiting a response. Its author is the writer of the current terms,
not necessarily the thread initiator. We reconstruct both parties’ cash after mandatory charges, trans-
ferred deeds and newly completed color groups. Completion means ownership of every deed in a
group, including mortgaged deeds; it is not buildability or economic value. Settlements are evaluated
atomically. In forty-four of 113 settlements, the final author differs from the initiator.
A completion episode runs from acquisition of the completing deed until loss of the group or game
end. For every trade-completed group, the first management window comprises the owner’s actual
management decisions in its first such turn after settlement. We record whether construction on that
group is offered on entry, offered later in the window, and applied. An option to build on a different
group does not count as access to the newly completed one.
Cash flows, deed flows and construction expenditure remain separate. Paid rent after group
completion measures realized receipts until loss or game end. A spending unit is one game/player/color
triple with own construction expenditure. Its developed-rent receipts differ from all rent received on
the group, and inherited buildings can earn income before the player’s own spending. Neither ratio
supplies incremental return on investment. Acquisition, interest, salvage, unpaid rent demands and
estate value are separate quantities.
Counts cover the declared finite populations. Offers in a thread share terms and players; decisions
in a game share a board; rotations within a seed block share random sequences that later actions may
consume differently. We report configuration, game and thread-position breakdowns without treating
decision counts as independent capability trials.

3.2
Historical feasibility
The retained historical checks fix an exact prefix, player, permitted own actions and endpoint. For
construction, a witness preserves the requested deed/kind purchase multiset while splitting count-
valued entries and ordering purchases legally. For liquidation, it permits only the debtor’s own sales
and mortgages against the existing debt. No resource, cooperating player or later phase is supplied.
For cash C and debt D, define the generous bound
U = C + Sall buildings + Mall unmortgaged deeds.
(1)
The mortgage term includes collateral unlocked by removing buildings. Ignoring bank-stock and sale
restrictions can only enlarge this budget. If U < D, those own-asset actions cannot pay. Otherwise,
a legal witness or a sound restricted search is needed. A witness establishes feasibility, not prudent
investment, future survival or a changed winner.
The historical checks cover all sixty eliminations and sixty-six construction defaults. Appendix C
specifies their arithmetic bounds, positive witnesses and restricted search, including the distinction
between available actions and bundled-sale validation.
3.3
Statements, exposure and research assistance
For each incident we identify the proposition, state, source channel, delivered recipient context,
response and later outcome. Delivery establishes exposure, not belief. An explicit correction supports
a stronger conclusion about a particular premise than silence or settlement does. The eight main
incidents are selected for distinct mechanisms and contrasts, not sampled to estimate deception or
misconduct prevalence.
Semantic judgments are LLM-assisted. Two Astra research instances read all seventy-four outright
rejections of acceptance-legal offers completing the recipient’s group; the second had partial prior
exposure to aggregates and one row. We report labels on which they agree and preserve both row-level
codings. A single instance coded prospective statements in 240 decisions, three per player-game,
selected as described in Section 7. These are model-assisted readings, not independently validated
human annotations.
Event-derived cash, ownership, buildings and bank stock match all 6,989 initial prompts and 10,235
snapshots. All 113 reconstructed settlements match subsequent state, and all twenty applied-action
streams reproduce the recorded events under documented canonicalization. Exact quoted fields and
focal recipient exposure are checked against original records. These checks establish correspondence
to the preserved implementation; Appendix C gives their scope, and Appendix E records research-
assistant roles and provenance.

4.2
Counters reprice exchanges with and without completion
Counter contents show how disagreement changes a transaction. Of 111 counters to author-only
completion offers, ninety-two retain every requested deed and preserve the original author’s completion.
Those two predicates select the same records here. Eighty-eight still leave only that party completing.
Of those eighty-eight, eighty-five improve the counterer’s net cash; the median cash change across the
eighty-eight is an increase of $125. Seventy-nine preserve the entire noncash bundle, including deeds
and jail cards, and all seventy-nine increase the counterer’s cash. These counters negotiate a price for
the same exchange rather than removing the completing deed.
This preservation and repricing is also common without completion (Table 4). Among 110 counters
to non-completing offers whose authors request at least one deed, eighty-seven retain every requested
deed and eighty-one preserve the entire noncash bundle while increasing the counterer’s cash. Sol and
Opus write ninety-two of the 111 counters and eighty of the 110, consistent with their greater use of
counteroffers. Repricing the unchanged bundle occurs in a majority of each configuration’s counters
in both populations. It is an observed bargaining practice in these games, not evidence of a special
completion premium.

4.3
Cash at settlement and bargaining limits
An exploratory cut describes the holders who release completing deeds. Among forty-eight cash
settlements completing the paying party’s group, median seller cash is $194, compared with $571 in
fifty-seven cash settlements completing neither party’s group. Twenty-one of those forty-eight sellers
hold less than $100. Conversely, among thirty cash-receiving settlements with a seller below $100,
twenty-one complete the payer’s group. The latter denominator conditions on low cash rather than on
completion.
The association appears across the reported $50, $100, $150 and $200 thresholds (Appendix A.1).
Opus supplies thirteen of the twenty-one low-cash sellers of completing deeds, and six of the twenty-
one eventually win. These settlements illustrate a recurring tradeoff: releasing a rival’s completing
deed supplies immediately usable cash. Their selected prices and circumstances do not identify the
effect of cash scarcity on agreement.

5
From ownership to executed development
5.1
Construction access and response resolution
All sixty-three trade-completed groups reach a first management window, and forty-three receive
construction. Sol builds on sixteen of seventeen, Gemini seventeen of eighteen, Opus seven of fifteen
and Grok three of thirteen. The twenty unbuilt groups comprise nine for which no recorded menu
offers construction on that group, four defaults, four received-call conflicts and three other valid ends
(Figure 1). Grok’s ten comprise four group-specific no-option windows with stated financing deferrals,
four conflicts and two stated holds. Opus’s eight comprise five group-specific no-option windows
under action-only recovery and three defaults. Sol’s single unbuilt group ends in a default; Gemini’s
ends with a valid turn-ending action.
Seven of the nine no-option groups receive a mortgaged completing deed. Another, Opus’s G20
dark blues, already contains a mortgaged Boardwalk; only Opus’s G15 dark blues have no mortgage
but insufficient cash for a house. Static budgets at the first post-completion management decision
show that two owners can cover redemption and a house with cash alone: Grok has $602 against $193

6.2
Estates redistribute groups while the game continues
During continuing play, estates complete fifty-two groups, forty-five owned by eventual winners;
eighteen of sixty-three trade-completed groups belong to eventual winners. The contrast describes
concentration conditional on the same games’ outcomes. Across all stable acquisition boundaries there
are 209 episodes: sixteen through direct purchase, nine through auction, sixty-three through trade and
121 through inheritance. Sixty-nine inherited completions occur at the final elimination and provide
no further management opportunity (Table 5).
Trade transfers 207 deeds. Estates transfer 397, including 227 before the final elimination. Eventual
winners receive 202 of those 227 nonterminal deeds and are creditors in 35 of forty nonterminal
eliminations, compared with 54 of sixty overall. The eventual winner receives the first estate in eighteen
games and all three estates in sixteen. In 57 of sixty eliminations, including 38 of forty nonterminal
ones, the debt is rent on the creditor’s developed color deed. An established rent-producing position
can therefore be followed by further asset concentration through inheritance. These counts describe
that sequence, not inheritance’s separate contribution to victory.
Routes also differ in income and timing. Fifty-one of sixty-three trade-completed groups collect rent,
totaling $57,378 after completion; directly purchased completions collect $23,070. Estate-completed
groups collect $11,472, with fourteen of fifty-two nonterminal episodes receiving any rent. Later
acquisition leaves less exposure, and rent on inherited buildings need not involve new spending. Of
fifty-eight winner spending units, thirty-four contain an inherited deed at first own expenditure, while
only thirty first become complete through an estate. Four mixed-route units close through a later
purchase or trade.
Two winners, Sol in G14 and Gemini in G15, settle no trade. Gemini’s G15 orange group completes

rent income, creditor acquisitions and the final elimination.

Planning across turns and responding to feedback
The temporal sample contains three decisions from each of eighty player-games, selected by a salted
hash from an earlier pool of public-message-unflagged records. One LLM-assisted reader examined
the complete applied public and private fields, excluding provider explanations. Of the 240 decisions,
fifty-nine contain forward-looking own-action statements, thirty-six contain prospective opponent-
capacity or outcome statements, and twenty-five refer to correction or feedback, with overlap. Grok
18

accounts for thirty-nine of the fifty-nine own-action records and twenty-two of the thirty-six opponent
records. Fifty-three private fields are absent, all for Opus. These counts characterize the selected
records rather than estimate a corpus-wide planning rate.
Most statements are contingent or open-ended rather than scoreable commitments. Eight specify
buying a named property on landing there. Six never meet a later matching purchase opportunity.
One is carried out. The other, Grok’s G16 Virginia plan, encounters an affordable purchase menu
at d159 with $371 against a $160 price. Its note still favors purchase and its public message claims
the pink set is closed, but the received call starts an auction. Gemini buys the deed for $380. This is
an observed failure of follow-through with conflicting channels, not an identified change of mind or
memory failure.
A positive longer target appears in G9. Sol’s turn-29 note plans three houses on each dark-blue
deed as funds arrive. Construction and financing over subsequent turns reach that target on turn 60.
The sequence demonstrates an expressed target and its later realization; it does not show that retaining
the note caused the outcome. The G15 offers likewise respond to an opponent’s stated objection, while
other notes make checkable deductions about publicly visible cash and collateral. These are concrete
forms of opponent attention, rather than calibrated predictions of hidden beliefs.
The complete uneven-build recurrence ledger follows 114 error decisions to 109 later build submis-
sions. Of fifty successors after a decision containing a corrective request with the validator message,
nine repeat the error. Of fifty-nine successors without that feedback, fifty-four repeat it; all fifty-nine
belong to Opus, whose refusal path consumed the corrective opportunity. The contrast documents re-
pair and recurrence under different response conditions. Because feedback and configuration coincide,
it does not identify feedback-caused learning.

Discussion
8.1
What the participants negotiate
Participants bargain over positions extending beyond the exchanged deed. They discuss a rival’s
construction reserves, the value of withholding a completing property and whether funding a distressed
seller benefits a third-party creditor. The offer census places those considerations in context: outright
refusal is more common when only the offer author completes a group, even within opening and
later offers, while counters commonly preserve the exchange and ask for better terms. The transcripts
show what that bargaining involves. In G15, a recipient refuses its own completion after the proposer
addresses a liquidity objection. In G2, Sol quotes different prices to different buyers, while Opus
advertises a lower ceiling than its private contingency permits. These are observable responses to
interdependent economic stakes.
The conduct spans successful exchange, refusal, factual correction and changed assurances. G7’s
bluff settles at the advertised price. G19’s recipient corrects a false claim and still accepts the exchange.
G13’s seller selectively presents its financing options while the buyer discusses the estate at risk. G18’s
buyer withdraws a repeated price and resumes bidding after the seller deteriorates. Public deception is
permitted in this task, so these incidents describe strategic conduct under that contract. Their context
shows both what the tactic sought and what happened next, without attributing the resulting price or
victory to the tactic alone.
19

8.2
From ownership to income and control
The next question is whether a negotiated position becomes productive. The systems complete similar
numbers of groups by trade—seventeen, eighteen, fifteen and thirteen—but build on sixteen, seventeen,
seven and three in the first management window. Those early-built groups collect $53,810 of $57,378 in
post-completion rent. Owners sometimes preserve cash or defer redemption; elsewhere the response
path prevents a requested build. Following acquisition into financing and construction thus connects
bargaining with the income the position actually produces.
Financial access also determines what can happen at a debt endpoint. Sol’s G20 sequence realizes
$1,365 from sales and mortgages to meet a $1,400 bill, whereas Gemini’s G16 calculation correctly
recognizes that all available own resources fall short. Across the sixty eliminations, fifty-six debts
exceed even the generous asset bound. These endpoints establish the immediate financing position,
leaving the quality of earlier spending as a separate question.
Inheritance then extends the economic consequences beyond the eliminated player. Eventual
winners receive thirty-five of forty nonterminal estates, and thirty-eight of those forty eliminations
follow rent on developed property. A productive group can therefore be followed by acquisition of
the debtor’s remaining assets. This pathway explains why participants discuss who would inherit a
blocker or a complete group. G13’s insurance pitch and G18’s withdrawn offer make that concern
explicit; G15’s bank-purchased oranges and subsequent estates show the corresponding sequence in
play. Bargaining, development and creditor succession belong in the same account because the assets
and incentives carry through all three stages.

Related work
Negotiation and economic agency.
NegotiationArena studies bargaining through controlled tactic
changes [2]. Recent work distinguishes expressed counterparty modeling from better agreements,
including experiments that supply coarse ranked preference information [3]. MERIT studies feed-
back for bargaining under specified buyer and seller utilities [9]. Those designs provide criteria for
agreement quality that our multi-player game does not. Our response and cash tables describe the
exchanges proposed, while exact counter transitions show how their terms change; neither supplies an
optimal-utility reference.
Vending-Bench studies sustained operation of a simulated business [1]. Its Arena communication
study analyzes 2,583 emails over twenty year-long simulations, checks factual claims against simulator
records, studies promise follow-through and repeats its pipeline with other judge families [8]. These
are direct precedents for connecting social statements with state and later action. Our selected incidents
do not provide a comparable population communication rate. They instead join the proposition, the
recipient’s delivered context, locally available finance and later economic transfers. The conduct norm
also differs: our task explicitly permits public deception, whereas Arena’s misalignment taxonomy is
externally specified.
Games and configured-system evaluation.
AgentMonopoly examines model families, sizes and
personas through Monopoly actions and language [13]. Its paper’s prompt encourages bluffing while
prohibiting public messages contradicting the action [13, §4, App. A.2]. The repository implementation
inspected at commit 2434f427 accepts or rejects single offers and eliminates at negative cash without
the debt-liquidation step studied here; it also transfers buildings intact to a player-creditor [11]. The
counteroffers and liquidation sequences in our corpus extend the economic trajectory available for
study under these different rules.
FinBoardBench evaluates financial decisions across board games, including Monopoly counterof-
fers [5, §3.2]. Its main decision phase permits up to five execution failures before a recorded system-
forced fallback [5, App. E]. Its Monopoly bankruptcy treatment sells buildings to the bank [5, App. C.3],
21

so its return summaries concern a different accounting setting from receipts on inherited development
here. DECE studies complete model-and-execution configurations across ten Eurogames and runs
matched execution-framework comparisons [15]. HAL likewise motivates evaluating complete agent
systems [6]. Our observational study follows those configurations through bargaining, construction
and creditor transfers.
Interpreting agent behavior.
Work on explanation faithfulness distinguishes recorded reasons from
internal computation [7, 12]; MASK distinguishes honesty from factual accuracy [10]. Agent-evaluation
reliability and measurement-focused auditing likewise motivate explicit units and evidence require-
ments [4, 14]. We combine that discipline with complete economic trajectories: the state establishes the
stakes, the delivered messages establish exposure, and later actions establish the observed outcome.

Limitations
The cohort has five seeds, four cyclic rotations per seed and a fixed opponent roster. States and
responses are dependent, opponent identities are visible, and response conditions are confounded
with configuration. Opus’s refusal recovery changes its prompt, retry opportunities and memory;
Grok has unresolved disagreements between response channels; some delivered histories misattribute
past trades. These conditions are reported before the results and attached to affected analyses. They
preclude intrinsic model rankings and claims about corrected interfaces or unfamiliar opponents.
The ruleset and observational design limit economic conclusions. Intact creditor inheritance and
restricted building stock shape the available strategies. The bargaining categories differ in assets,
prices and stages, cash thresholds are exploratory, and eventual-winner splits condition on the same
trajectories. We measure realized flows and local financing possibilities, without identifying optimal
prices, causal persuasion or the separate contributions of skill and chance. Appendix C gives the
implementation and historical-witness scope.
Semantic evidence is retrospective and LLM-assisted. Research assistants selected incidents and
developed coding categories after inspecting the records; Sol and Opus assistants share model identi-
fiers with two evaluated participants under different tasks and settings. Two partly unblinded reason
readings are not independent human validation. Six of the eight main incidents involve Grok, whose
channels are unusually observable, so the selection cannot estimate or rank conduct prevalence. Exact
quotes and delivered-context checks make the interpretations inspectable. Full research roles and
artifact provenance are recorded in Appendix E.

Conclusion
The twenty games show agents negotiating over rival development, financing productive assets and
anticipating who will inherit when a player fails. Recipients sometimes refuse their own group
completion because of the rival position it enables; counters often preserve the exchange while seeking
better terms. Completed groups generate income through subsequent financing and construction, and
developed-property rent precedes most creditor acquisitions. Eight contextual incidents show bluffing,
correction, selective disclosure, financial-denial appeals and withdrawal alongside those economic
trajectories. Together, the population accounts and transcripts explain how stated strategies become
bargains, income and control of assets in these four configured systems.

C
Implementation and certificate scope
The preserved ruleset is audit_corrected_v2. Free Parking pays nothing, income and luxury taxes are
$200 and $100, and jail permits up to three doubles attempts before a forced fine. A player remaining
jailed has no management window that turn. Model-facing state includes opponents’ configured
names.
Transfer interest and redemption both use floating-point ceilings. Multiplying mortgage principal
by 0.1 agrees with the exact board-principal transfer charge. Multiplying by 1.1 for redemption exceeds
the exact mathematical ceiling by one dollar for principals $50, $90, $100, $110 and $200. A $100
mortgage therefore costs $111 to redeem in this implementation. Historical alternatives use that
actual charge. The present paper does not establish whether each overcharge changed an affordability
boundary.
Build validation checks evenness after each plan entry. Sale validation can accept a bundled
operation from final evenness and net stock. An atomic one-building-at-a-time search can therefore
miss a legal bundled sale. The corrected checker refuses negative graph certificates in states offering
building sales. Its retained negative graph result is the G7 mortgage-only state, whose menu offers
no sale action. Source inspection indicates that the separate sale-plan validator would accept a $650
group-clearing bundle if a sale were offered; that observation is not a new executed witness. G7’s lock
is a restriction of the authoritative legal menu, not a proof that every mathematical liquidation of its
buildings is impossible.
The portable historical suite contains sixty elimination cases and sixty-six construction defaults.
Fifty-six eliminations use the arithmetic upper bound, three use payment witnesses and one uses
the restricted search. The defaults contain seventy well-formed targets, one malformed target and
sixty-one attempts with no parseable target. Of the seventy well-formed targets, sixty-three execute
after reordering and seven exceed cash. Sixty-three of the sixty-six decisions contain at least one
reachable target. Fifty-five defaults are concentrated in G5 and G13, including thirty-nine in G13. These
are complete declared populations, not 126 independent mechanisms.
The portable package preserves original action streams, settings, event digests, a fixed case manifest
and unchanged engine/board copies. It reproduces its supplied cases. Population completeness,
original attempt extraction and semantic interpretation require the separately retained ledgers and
raw records. A successful reproduction cannot supply those missing claims by itself.
The broader reconstruction checks match 10,235 economic snapshots and all 113 accepted settle-
ments, including $431 mortgage-transfer interest across thirty settlements. Rebuilt initial memory
matches all 6,989 prompts. Jail-card transfers require transaction and bankruptcy context rather than
dedicated transfer events, though reconstructed card holdings match 27,956 player observations. This
is a limitation of the event vocabulary, not a reason to infer an unrecorded change.
The retained certificate replay compares every complete event after JSON serialization with sorted
keys, compact separators and ASCII escaping. It does not drop event fields or normalize away identifiers
in this comparison. Raw-file whitespace and key order therefore need not be byte-identical, while the
decoded event values must agree. Other state-only replay modes have different scopes and are not the
basis of the 50,918-event equality reported here. Engine determinism, correspondence to intended
Monopoly rules, and validity of a behavioral interpretation remain separate claims.

Recorded response and information conditions
Opus has 1,605 refusal-affected decisions but 1,604 first-attempt refusal recoveries: G2 d129 adds a
refusal on the second attempt. The configured policy names anthropic/claude-opus-4.8 as a third-stage
fallback, but it is never invoked; all 1,696 decision records return Opus 5.5, and no decision exceeds two
attempts. At 1,394 later memory windows, no model-authored private note is carried forward: 1,108
have an empty field and 286 carry fallback text. The jump in first-attempt refusals coincides with the
source-commit boundary. The inspected diff changes campaign concurrency, UI, documentation and
tests, with no engine, prompt or model-client change; it identifies no cause of the provider behavior.
Among Grok’s 1,663 decisions, the retained parser identifies 1,068 initial content action names:
1,035 from parsed JSON, twenty-three from malformed content with a name, and ten from flat action
forms. There are 139 disagreements with the received call name, fifteen with equal parsed argument
objects and five with equal literal argument substrings. The saved requests are non-streamed and
the responses are single received JSON bodies. The records therefore locate the disagreement in the
received response, without identifying how the provider or upstream service produced it.
The historical trade formatter reverses attribution for forty-four accepted settlements, also misstates
twenty-six rejected threads, and produces 232 self-partner references. Affected summaries occur in
2,054 delivered prompts. Current holdings remain correct, and all 920 current trade-response menus
were reconciled separately. Of 6,882 applied nonempty public messages, 149 never enter the public
timeline and thirteen enter it without a later recipient request carrying them. Transcript delivery
checks use the actual recipient request.
Structured board parameters vary by phase. All 547 purchase prompts supply the landed deed’s
price, house cost, rents and group; all 854 auction prompts supply its group. Management, trade, jail
and liquidation prompts identify deeds by space keys without those board-price fields, and none of
4,081 management prompts states redemption costs. Calculations can draw on prior observations,
messages or remembered board values, but the current structured menu is not itself a complete price
or rent reference.
Of 663 applied ends on menus offering construction somewhere, sixty-six are engine defaults and
twenty-four disagree with the action named in content. Twenty-two of those twenty-four content
actions request a build, one a mortgage and one a proposal. The remaining 573 are not thereby classified
as deliberate reserve choices. Opus accounts for sixty-one defaults among its 104 such ends. In the
separate construction-recovery population, exact within-decision multiset recovery occurs in 20/25 Sol,
6/18 Gemini, 5/5 Grok and 0/61 Opus cases; broader recovery definitions retain different denominators
