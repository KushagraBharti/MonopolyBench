Bargaining, Liquidity, and Execution in MonopolyBench
MonopolyBench research draft
10 November 2026
Abstract
Economic agents must negotiate with competitors whose resources and survival affect their
own prospects. We study these interactions in twenty complete four-player MonopolyBench games,
containing 6,989 decisions and 920 negotiation responses. Offers completing only the author’s color
group are rejected in 159 of 289 acceptance-legal opportunities; offers completing only the recipient’s
group are rejected in 38 of 140. These observational differences accompany explicit consideration
of rival consequences in reviewed refusal explanations. Negotiation styles differ sharply between
configurations, while their numbers of accepted offers are similar. Development, however, depends
on more than willingness to invest: of twenty trade-completed groups left unbuilt in the first
management window, nine never become buildable, eight end in defaults or conflicting response
channels, and three end in other valid END records. All sixty-one defaulted Opus construction
targets are reachable after reordering their invalid encodings. At elimination, fifty-six of sixty debts
exceed a generous own-asset financing bound, three are payable by legal sequences, and one is
blocked by hotel liquidation despite sufficient nominal assets. Seven contextual incidents document
bluffing, selective disclosure, financial-denial appeals, withdrawal after distress, and consequential
speech/action disagreements. Creditor inheritance links these local interactions to realized asset
concentration. We report a behavioral study of complete configured systems, supported by source-
linked accounting and historical feasibility checks. The record does not identify causal persuasion,
optimal policy, durable learning, or an intrinsic model ranking.
1
Introduction
A trade in a competitive economy changes more than the buyer’s inventory. It can finance a rival’s
development, preserve a blocker against another player, or postpone an insolvency that would transfer
productive assets to a creditor. An agent deciding whether to pay must therefore consider both the
exchange and the positions it creates. Negotiation becomes a problem of economic interdependence: a
temporarily solvent rival can be preferable to a stronger creditor, and a mutually completing trade can
still leave one party unable to develop its new holdings.
We study this interdependence in MonopolyBench, a recorded four-player economy with property
acquisition, auctions, repeated counteroffers, construction, mortgages, rent and bankruptcy. The corpus
contains twenty complete games by four configured frontier-model agents. It supports an account
of how participants bargain, turn holdings into productive assets, and respond to distress. Because
the engine records legal decisions and resulting events alongside response attempts and delivered
memory, the account can distinguish economic opportunities from textual plans and executed moves.
The distinction matters empirically. Some agents expressly refuse exchanges because of the rival’s
resulting development or cash position. Some buy time for a distressed opponent to prevent another
rival from receiving its estate. Yet an apparent decision to conserve cash can also be an engine default
after an invalid construction request, and a declaration of bankruptcy can contradict the same response’s

instructions to sell houses. Understanding the behavior requires tracing the transaction, its available

Existing economic-agent studies provide important precedents. Vending-Bench examines sustained
business operation, and its Arena communication study checks inter-agent messages against simulator
state and studies promise follow-through [1, 6]. AgentMonopoly studies behavioral differences
and persona interventions in Monopoly [10]. FinBoardBench evaluates financial decisions across
board games, while DECE compares complete agent configurations across games and execution
frameworks [4, 12]. We build on this work with an observational account that follows all four players
through negotiation, executable development, liquidation and creditor inheritance. We do not claim
that economic simulation, legal menus, state-checked communication or replay is new.
Three results organize the paper. First, the complete negotiation record shows different response
patterns depending on whose color group an offer completes, together with explicit rival-sensitive
reasoning in selected refusal records. The configurations differ more in countering versus rejecting
than in their absolute acceptance counts. Second, productive development depends on both economic
access and response execution. Submitted investment allocations may be feasible even when their
encodings fail, and available collateral may become accessible only after sales. Third, contextual
exchanges show participants bargaining over these same dependencies through omission, bluffing,
financial-denial appeals and changing offers. Later estate transfers make the stakes concrete, without
establishing that any particular message changed a price or winner.
The paper makes a finite-cohort empirical contribution. Its population counts, local financing
checks and contextual transcripts describe what these configured systems did under the recorded
conditions. Those conditions include erroneous trade-history summaries and highly unequal response
recovery. We put them before the results because they constrain interpretation, rather than treating
them as incidental implementation details. The released evidence supports reconstruction and scrutiny;
it does not transform this observational cohort into a controlled comparison of underlying models.
2
Environment, cohort and observation conditions
2.1
An economy with persistent strategic consequences
Four agents play on the forty-space United States Monopoly board, each starting with $1,500. Passing
Go supplies $200; the bank begins with 32 houses and 12 hotels. Players acquire properties by purchase,
ascending auction, trade or inheritance. Completing a color group enables construction subject to cash,
mortgage status, even-building constraints and stock. Mortgages provide liquidity while disabling
rent on the mortgaged deed. All money in the analysis is game currency unless explicitly identified as
OpenRouter USD.
The rules engine alone changes state and determines legality. It emits the acting player’s decision
menu, validates the selected action and records state transitions. Optional management can span
several decisions within one turn. Trade threads permit repeated offers and counteroffers, up to twenty
exchanges, before normal play resumes. A counteroffer changes which player authors the current
terms. Developed-group deeds cannot be traded. Recipients of mortgaged deeds pay transfer interest
to the bank, and redemption has a separate charge. Appendix A records implementation details
relevant to the financial calculations.
In debt liquidation, the player may sell buildings, mortgage eligible deeds or declare bankruptcy;
it cannot initiate a rescuing trade or build. A developed group’s mortgages become available only
after its buildings are removed. Downgrading a hotel requires enough houses in the bank. These

2.2
Cohort and response handling
The definitive cohort contains twenty completed games, eighty player-games, 50,918 events, 6,989
decisions and 8,679 response attempts. Five seeds each receive four cyclic seat rotations, so each
configuration occupies every seat five times. All twenty games terminate through bankruptcy without
a turn cap. A decision is an engine-issued opportunity; an attempt is a response within that decision;
the resolved action is what the engine applies. We use Gn dk for zero-based decision k in game n.
Multiple decisions can occur during one player’s turn.
Agents use OpenRouter with recorded medium reasoning effort. No temperature or nucleus
setting is supplied. The same effort label does not establish matched computation: provider-reported
completion tokens average 128 per decision for Sol and 4,041 for Grok. Their mean wall-clock latencies
are 4.8 and 65 seconds. Recorded charges total USD 141.71, a lower bound because 1,600 Opus attempts
and three Grok attempts lack charges. Token definitions differ across providers. These are usage
descriptions, not efficiency-normalized performance estimates.
Every configuration has a corrective retry for invalid outputs. Opus also has a first-attempt refusal
path that requests an action without a private note. That path consumes the remaining attempt; an
invalid construction response afterward does not receive another corrective request. Across Opus’s
1,696 decisions, 1,604 first attempts refuse, with one additional refused second attempt. The provider
returns the same Terms-of-Service message, but the triggering feature is not identified. The refusal
frequency changes across the first four versus remaining sixteen games. The coincident source commits
change campaign orchestration and application files, not the inspected engine, prompt, resolver or
model-client files. Neither coincidence identifies an upstream cause.
The response path also changes later context. In 1,108 Opus decisions its private-memory window
is empty; in another 286 it contains only synthetic fallback notes. Thus 1,394 decisions lack a model-
authored private note in that window. A private-reasoning comparison across configurations would
therefore compare different observation and memory conditions. The configured fallback model is
never invoked.

2.3
Information, communication and task norms
Agents see public cash, ownership, development, the decision context and opponents’ configured
model names. They do not see opponents’ private notes or future random and card state. Public
memory retains twenty timeline entries, private memory ten own notes, and trade responses include
structured negotiation history. Canonical logging does not guarantee later delivery: 149 of 6,882
nonempty public-message occurrences never enter the timeline, and thirteen inserted occurrences
have no observed later opponent timeline exposure before the record ends. We inspect actual recipient
prompts when interpreting a message’s reception.
A history formatter assumes that the thread initiator authors the current terms. It reverses directions
in forty-four accepted-trade summaries, misattributes twenty-six rejected offers, and creates self-partner
descriptions in 232 counters. At least one manifestation appears in 2,054 of 6,989 initial prompts. The
correct current holdings do not cancel misleading historical descriptions. However, current-offer
accounting agrees with the recipient’s delivered acceptance fields in all 920 response opportunities.
We can measure the exchange offered without assuming its recipient ignored faulty history.
The selected Grok records contain 139 action-name conflicts between assistant content and the
received tool call. Fifteen have equal parsed argument objects, and five have literally equal argument
substrings. These disagreements already appear in the saved nonstreamed JSON bodies received
from OpenRouter, excluding local streaming assembly for these checked cases. Generation, provider
processing and gateway behavior remain unresolved origins. The engine applies the received tool
name. Empty content in other configurations also limits cross-model detection comparisons.
Standard prompts permit public bluffing and deception and request private notes reflecting the
agent’s true reasoning. The action-only recovery prompt removes both that field and its honesty clause.
We therefore distinguish factual falsity, misleading presentation, explanation/action conflict and
instruction violation. A permitted bluff can be strategically relevant without being task misalignment;
a false private note can be an error. Provider explanations and private notes are fallible recorded text,
not direct access to internal computation [5, 9].
3
Measurement and evidence
We combine complete mechanical populations with explicitly selected semantic analyses. Counts use
the finite cohort as their population. Decisions share boards, players and negotiation threads; seed
rotations share initial random sequences but can consume them differently. Thousands of decisions do
not create thousands of independent trials of model capability. We report denominators and retain
game identifiers rather than assigning decision-level confidence intervals to dependent observations.
3.1
Transactions, investment and legal access
A response opportunity is one received offer. A thread begins with a proposal and ends in acceptance
or rejection; counters create additional opportunities within the thread. We reconstruct the author and
recipient of each current offer, including cash, mortgage interest, traded deeds and newly completed
color groups. Completion includes mortgaged ownership but excludes railroads and utilities. It is
an ownership fact, not buildability or utility. Accepted transactions are evaluated atomically, so an
intermediate transfer cannot create a fictitious investment opportunity.
For trade-completed groups, the first management window includes the owner’s actual manage-
ment decisions in its first such global turn after settlement. We distinguish buildability on entry,
buildability later in that window, and actual construction. Jail and other phases can delay access. An

3.2
Statements and trajectories
For conduct, we identify the proposition, available state, source channel, delivered recipient context,
subsequent response and economic consequence. An explicit correction is evidence against endorse-
ment of that proposition even if the trade settles. Silence is not evidence of absence of uptake. An
observed later action does not identify which message caused it. Selected transcripts show mechanisms
and counterexamples; they are not a moral severity ranking or a deception-rate sample.
The earlier model-assisted review considered 614 catalog entries, twenty trajectories and 240
complete original bundles. Catalog entries overlap and are not unique episodes. The common sample
has three public-unflagged decisions per player-game. Later coding of prospective statements uses
its public messages and applied private notes, excluding provider summaries. A separate closed-
population analysis reads all seventy-four outright rejections of acceptance-legal offers completing
the recipient’s group. Two LLM-assisted readings retain exact spans and disagreements; the second
reader was partly exposed to aggregates and one row during schema inspection. These are inspectable
coding judgments, not blinded human annotations.
The analysis uses both original-source checks and retained earlier audits. Event-derived economic
state agrees with all 6,989 initial prompts and 10,235 saved snapshots; all 113 accepted-transfer pre-
dictions match the next recorded state. All twenty historical action streams replay, and rebuilt initial
memory matches all 6,989 windows. These checks support reconstruction. Agreement with the
frozen engine is distinct from correctness against intended rules, and neither validates a semantic
interpretation. Appendix B states the certificate and review limits.

4
Bargaining over relative positions
4.1
Offer consequences and responses
The corpus contains 526 complete negotiation threads and 920 response opportunities: 113 acceptances,
394 counters and 413 rejections. Acceptance is legal in 918 opportunities; two offers would leave a party
cash-negative after mandatory charges. Table 2 separates the immediately completed color groups
under the offered terms.
Offers completing only the author’s group are rejected in 159/289 cases, or 55.0%; those completing
only the recipient’s group are rejected in 38/140, or 27.1%. In the former category the median cash
transfer pays the recipient $200, while in the latter the recipient pays $350. Recipients consequently do
not respond to incoming cash alone. Offers completing both sides have 36/81 outright rejections and
35/81 counters. These are substantial differences in observed response distributions, not a causal effect
of rival completion: properties, mortgage status, recipients, prices and game positions differ between
cells, and agents choose which offers to make.
The explanations give the comparison strategic content. Of seventy-four rejections that would com-
plete a recipient group, both LLM-assisted readings identify an explicit trade-linked rival consequence
in sixty-one. Four further cases have disputed interpretations, including whether transferred cash
alone implies funding and whether a wrong-role explanation states a coherent rationale. In G1 d207,
for example, the recipient rejects Boardwalk for New York plus $100 while discussing its own dark-blue
development reserves and the rival’s resulting orange development. This expresses a joint assessment
of relative positions. It does not prove that rejection maximizes expected survival.
Six of the 221 offers completing a recipient group also break another of its complete groups; only
one of the seventy-four outright rejections falls there. Most refusals therefore are not mechanically
explained by exchanging one complete group for another. Nonetheless, cash, mortgages, building
cost, remaining stock and third-party exposure still matter. Group completion is a useful descriptive
partition precisely because it can be paired with those costs, not because it defines a profitable deal.
Repeated offers concentrate evidence. G1 supplies twenty-two of the seventy-four refusals and G15
twelve. Of 526 threads, 414 follow an earlier thread between the same pair in the same game. A thread
can contain up to eleven counters in this cohort; the median has one response. The offer table measures
the transactions actually proposed and responses actually received. It is not an independent-sample
preference experiment.

4.2
Different styles of disagreement
The four configurations accept between twenty-six and thirty offers each, but differ sharply in how
they decline the current terms. Sol and Opus counter 140 and 141 times; Gemini rejects 180 times
and counters thirty-six. Grok rejects 149 and counters seventy-seven. These are behavioral profiles
under different exposure and response conditions, rather than negotiation-skill rankings. Countering
preserves a conversation but does not establish willingness to accept its eventual terms.
Only thirty-two settlements occur without a counter; eighty-one follow at least one. Of 388 no-
counter threads, thirty-two settle, compared with eighty-one of 138 countered threads. The association
is consistent with participants continuing conversations they judge negotiable. It does not show
that adding a counter to an otherwise identical conversation improves the outcome. In forty-four
settlements, the final offer author differs from the thread initiator, which is also why the history
formatter’s initiator-based interpretation fails.
Initiation gives a different profile. Sol begins 132 threads, Gemini eighteen, Opus 240 and Grok
136; nineteen, eleven, forty-four and thirty-nine respectively settle. A high settlement fraction among
few proposals can reflect selection, favorable inventories or counterparties’ demands. The corpus does
not specify an outside-option value or fair-price oracle. The overall 113/918 offer acceptance fraction is
therefore not a test for agreement bias, fairness or exploitability.
5
Turning holdings into income and accessible finance
5.1
Acquisition is followed by unequal development access
Recorded deed acquisitions comprise 475 direct purchases, seventy-two auction purchases, 207 trade
transfers and 397 inheritances, including repeated acquisition of the same deed. At stable transaction
boundaries, 209 group-completion episodes arise through sixteen purchases, nine auctions, sixty-three
trades and 121 inheritances. Sixty-nine inherited completions occur at the final bankruptcy and offer
no subsequent management decision. Treating all complete groups as equal investment opportunities
would therefore miscount both access and exposure.
All sixty-three trade-completed groups do have a first management window. Construction occurs
in sixteen of seventeen Sol groups, seventeen of eighteen Gemini groups, seven of fifteen Opus groups
and three of thirteen Grok groups. The remaining twenty split into nine never buildable during that
window, four engine defaults, four received END conflicts, and three other valid END records. All nine
never-buildable groups belong to Opus or Grok. These counts explain why the raw build fractions
cannot simply be named willingness to invest.

5.2
Reachable allocations fail at the action interface
Every one of the sixty-one defaulted Opus construction purchase multisets executes at its historical state
after splitting count-valued entries and ordering purchases legally. The original plans remain invalid
as encoded. Sixty decisions follow a refusal with an invalid action-only response; the exceptional G2
d129 has an invalid attempt followed by a refusal. The budget is exhausted and the engine applies END.
The result is a concrete failure to execute a requested investment, not evidence that the participant
preferred to keep the cash.
The full construction-default population contains sixty-six decisions and seventy well-formed
submitted targets, plus a malformed target and attempts with no parseable target. Sixty-three well-
formed targets are reachable after reordering; seven exceed current cash. At decision level, sixty-
three defaults contain at least one reachable target. All sixty-six allow some legal construction, a
weaker claim. Fifty-five defaults occur in G5 and G13, and repeated allocations limit the diversity
of the examples. The mechanism is well documented within this population, but it is not sixty-six
independent demonstrations of a general planning defect.
Successful correction also has more than one endpoint. Among uneven-build rejections ending
in construction, the initial purchase multiset is preserved in 20/25 Sol, 6/18 Gemini and 5/5 Grok

5.3
Current-debt finance and the hotel lock
Fifty-six of sixty eliminations have a current debt above the generous own-asset bound in Equation 1.
These debts cannot be paid by the allowed sales and mortgages. Of the remaining four, three have
legal payment sequences and one has sufficient nominal assets but inaccessible finance. This resolves
the historical current-debt question; it does not decide whether earlier purchases, trades or reserve
choices were wise.
The three payable cases have different mechanisms. Sol’s G12 d190 and Gemini’s G17 d234 accounts
omit collateral unlocked by building sales. In G12 d249, Grok’s content requests a sale and its attached
note opposes bankruptcy, but its received call declares bankruptcy. Five legal house sales pay the $250
debt from $43 cash and leave $43. That case is not a uniform failure to notice mortgages and does not
demonstrate deliberate surrender. Section 6 retains its contradictory channels.
The locked case is G7 d339. Opus holds $390 and a clear Oriental mortgage worth $50. Its pinks have
four houses, four houses and a hotel, while the bank has no houses. The hotel cannot be downgraded,
and even-selling prevents removing a lower sibling’s house. The only financing move mortgages
Oriental, producing $440 and automatic bankruptcy against $750 owed. Nominal resources total $1,310,
but the additional value is not accessible at that decision. Earlier opportunities to release houses
concern different historical prefixes; they are not available debt-phase actions.
Successful and unsuccessful actual liquidations provide contrasts. G20 d327–336 combines $700
building salvage and $665 mortgages with $49 cash, pays $1,400 and retains $14. G16’s extensive
liquidation instead reaches $1,393 against $2,000. The model’s original account already has the correct
total, while an early researcher label omitted unlocked collateral. These cases prevent a narrative in
which every eliminated player failed to finance or every favorable-looking alternative was overlooked
by the model.
5.4
Inheritance connects local decisions to collective outcomes
There are 103 spending units, with $103,450 of own construction outlay, $93,000 in whole-game paid
developed rent and $92,550 after first own expenditure. Forty units receive zero developed rent; twenty-
six of those also trigger no creditor elimination. These are distinct units from the fifty-seven built-color
rent elimination triggers. Zero receipts do not establish a bad investment: available exposure, unpaid
final rent, inherited development and salvage differ.
Eventual winners hold fifty-eight spending units. Thirty-four contain an inherited deed at first
own spending; only thirty first become complete through an estate. Four mixed-route groups contain
inherited deeds but finish through a later purchase or trade. This distinction shows how inheritance
can contribute to productive holdings without being their immediate completion route.

Collective cash and deed flows should also remain separate. Actual trades transfer $28,425 through

5.5
Maintaining and revising plans
The fixed 240-decision sample contains fifty-nine forward-looking own-action passages, thirty-six
prospective opponent-capacity or outcome passages and twenty-five correction or feedback references,
with overlap. A single LLM-assisted coding pass identifies these categories; fifty-three private fields
are absent, all for Opus. Statements with unspecified contingencies or horizons cannot be scored as
commitments. Of eight explicit named-property landing policies, six have no later matching purchase
opportunity. One is fulfilled; another, G16 d103’s Virginia purchase rule, meets an affordable buy
menu at d159 but resolves to an auction despite a note still favoring purchase. No plan-adherence rate
is inferred from these eight selected records.
Longer realized targets exist. Sol’s G9 dark-blue plan at d64 reaches three houses on each deed at
d163, spanning turns 29–60. G20’s plan to mortgage Vermont first detours through a sale offer, then
reaches the financing endpoint after rejection. Auction ceilings can also be revised or exceeded. These
are observable temporal behaviors, but changed opportunities and response conflicts prevent treating
every revision as learning or every divergence as forgetting.
The full recurrence ledger follows 114 uneven-construction error decisions to 109 later submitted
builds, with sixty-three repetitions. Fifty-four error decisions receive a later same-decision request
containing the validator error; fifty have a next submitted build, and nine repeat it. Fifty-four of all
sixty-three repetitions belong to Opus, whose refusal path usually consumed the corrective opportunity.
States, allocations and feedback exposure differ, and some successors occur within the same turn. The
record therefore documents repair and recurrence under unequal conditions rather than estimating a
causal feedback benefit or durable learning.

Conduct in economic context
The following seven incidents connect communication to the economic dependencies measured above.
They were selected for distinct mechanisms, concrete stakes, recipient context and observable conse-
quences. They are not the seven most severe events under a defined scale, nor a sample for estimating
prevalence. Public messages, applied private notes and provider explanations remain separately la-
belled. Excerpts are verbatim; their arithmetic and descriptions are not silently corrected. The analysis
outside the cards supplies the relevant state and interpretation.

Across these incidents, the economic themes recur: cash may preserve a blocking owner, finance
an opponent’s development, or prolong a debtor whose assets another player wants. The records
also distinguish an explicit bluff, a selective financing presentation, a contingent bargaining reversal,
a coordination appeal, and contradictory response channels. Calling all of them deception would
discard the differences that explain what occurred. Calling none strategically meaningful because
persuasion is unidentified would discard the conduct itself.

Related work and scope of the comparison
Vending-Bench studies long-term business operation, while the Arena communication study analyzes
2,583 emails from twenty year-long simulations using state-grounded communication classification [1,
6]. Arena also studies promise follow-through and replicates its pipeline with other judge families. Our
transcript analysis is purposive and does not supply a comparable communication rate. Its contribution
is contextual economic explanation within the game: the feasible collateral omitted by a pitch, the
15

=== PAGE 16 ===
recipient’s actual correction, and the estate that later changes hands. A conduct label defined for Arena
cannot be transferred unchanged to prompts that expressly permit public bluffing.
AgentMonopoly studies model families, size and persona effects through actions and outcomes as
well as language [10]. Its recorded-action interface and behavioral framing preclude broad novelty
claims for applying language models to Monopoly or comparing speech with action. Our cohort follows
repeated counteroffer threads, all four players’ economic flows, and the relation between submitted
targets and received actions. Rules also differ. In the inspected AgentMonopoly implementation,
cash-negative elimination has no debt-liquidation phase, although player-creditors inherit buildings
intact. Our current-debt findings concern opportunities that depend on that phase. We retain the exact
inspected versions rather than treating every implementation as the same task.
FinBoardBench studies wealth management in several board games, including legal decision
interfaces, correction and forced fallback after exhausted retries [4]. It is direct precedent for separating
system intervention from accepted agent output. Our construction results examine the purchase target
behind a rejected response, while the finance results test current-debt payment under the recorded
liquidation rules. The different creditor-building treatment also prevents a simple comparison of
developed rent with its investment-return summaries.
DECE evaluates complete model/execution configurations across ten Eurogames and uses matched
comparisons to study execution frameworks [12]. Its design supports an intervention comparison
that our observational cohort does not. NegotiationArena provides controlled bargaining studies and
saved games for counterfactual modification [2]. Restore-and-branch functionality is therefore not a
novelty claim either. Our evidence is complementary: a complete, inspectable record of interdependent
bargaining and economic consequences, with historical alternatives used to specify what was feasible.
Recent evaluation work further distinguishes the target of inference. Hardy et al. analyze how
reliability differs for complete systems and underlying models, while Yang et al. use matched controls
and scorer validation for a leaderboard auditing question [3, 11]. Our reconstruction checks do
not substitute for those validation designs. The field also distinguishes performance from other
behavioral constructs. Work on honesty, safety evaluation and explanation faithfulness cautions against
substituting accuracy, benchmark success or readable reasoning for the property being studied [7–9].
We follow that distinction at the case level. This paper does not validate a new honesty metric, measure
monitor prediction quality, or place four configurations on a general capability/alignment curve.
8
Discussion
8.1
Economic interdependence explains what the participants negotiate
The common subject of the bargaining and conduct results is the value of other players’ resources. A
property exchange changes one’s own set, a rival’s construction budget and the assets a future creditor
may receive. The offer-response table captures differences associated with immediate ownership
consequences. The refusal explanations explicitly discuss rival development and liquidity. The
transcripts show participants presenting solvency as insurance, asking others to withhold financing
and withdrawing an offer once preserving the seller becomes less attractive. These observations
support a richer account than an agent that values only its own newly completed group.
The evidence also establishes the limits of that account. A stated rival consequence can be arithmeti-
cally wrong, refer to the wrong trading role, or accompany an action inconsistent with the text. The
real explanatory unit is the economic claim in its available context, not an unconstrained attribution of
theory of mind. In particular, the bargaining contrast cannot identify a causal aversion to strengthening
16

=== PAGE 17 ===
rivals because the offers were neither matched nor assigned. The explanations make the contrast
interpretable; they do not solve that identification problem.
Creditor inheritance makes the interdependence longitudinal. Nonterminal estates already concen-
trate in eventual winners, and the G15 trajectory connects purchase-led development to subsequent
inherited assets. G13’s later Short Line bankruptcy shows why an insurance sale can postpone, yet
fail to prevent, the transfer discussed during bargaining. These are realized pathways under the
ruleset. They motivate studying liquidity and acquisition together without claiming a quantified causal
contribution to winning.
8.2
Execution is part of the observed strategy
The records contain economically meaningful choices to hold reserves as well as failed investment exe-
cution. Treating all nonbuilds as caution confounds unavailable opportunities, defaults and conflicting
channels. Treating all bankruptcies as overlooked liquidity is equally wrong: fifty-six current debts ex-
ceed even the generous own-asset bound, and the remaining cases require distinct explanations. These
results give a concrete answer to the difference between sounding strategic and acting strategically.
Some utterances correspond to realized targets and checked choices; some feasible targets fail in action
encoding; some received actions contradict both content and an attached rationale.
The configured-system perspective is necessary but not a cure for confounding. Opus’s refusal
path changes the current response, remaining corrective budget and later memory. Grok’s observable
content/tool discrepancies have unresolved origin. Historical trade summaries supply misleading
descriptions despite correct current state. These conditions are part of what happened, and excluding
affected records would select a different population rather than reconstruct a clean one. They restrict
transfer from the study to ordinary play under corrected interfaces.
The audits are useful because they settle specified questions in these records. They do not demon-
strate that a general-purpose auditor is more accurate, that fixes would improve performance, or that a
benchmark implementation perfectly realizes intended Monopoly rules. Positive feasibility witnesses,
finite impossible cases and semantic interpretations carry different evidence. The paper benefits from
stating that difference once and using it consistently rather than repeating broad disclaimers after
every number.
8.3
What the broader research questions receive
Table 4 maps the study to its motivating questions. The strongest empirical answers concern bargaining,
economic access, contextual conduct and realized collective transfers. Opponent modeling, temporal
planning and adaptation receive narrower observations. All four configurations share a cohort, not a
controlled general-capability axis or a held-out opponent distribution.
The broader speech-versus-execution question receives direct case-level evidence, including both
correspondence and failure. General intelligence versus multi-agent capability remains unanswered:
four different providers’ configured systems at one point in time do not define a capability curve. Skill
versus luck receives mechanistic examples and descriptive outcome contrasts, not a decomposition.
Sol wins one seed block from all four seats; another block is won from seat 2 by all four configurations.
Neither pattern alone identifies skill or a seat effect. Full causal answers would require different
evidence, and we do not substitute additional coding of these same games for that evidence.

Limitations and research provenance
The twenty games cover five seeds, cyclic rotations and one roster. Their participants, public names,
response policies, rule variant and prompt norms define the study’s domain. They are not independent
repeated tests of isolated models. No matched intervention changes history formatting, refusal recovery,
an opponent, a persuasive message or a capability tier. Reported rates describe declared populations
within this corpus; selected examples do not estimate prevalence.
Semantic extraction and review used LLMs, including Astra, Sol, Opus and Fable research instances.
Some share model families or identifiers with evaluated players. They are analysis agents, not inde-
pendent human annotators; their prompt settings and review roles differ from gameplay. Repeated
model-assisted agreement does not establish an external gold standard. The project retains original
judgments, source-backed corrections, unblinding and disputed codes. Researcher mistakes include a
wrong completion trajectory for G15 and a mistaken interpretation of mortgage-menu fields. These
corrections document improvements to this analysis, not a validated accuracy gain over a competing
method.
Reconstruction also depends on the engine and artifact contract. Exact replay can reproduce a rule
defect. Floating-point ceiling behavior in redemption and differences between atomic and bundled sale
validation are disclosed in Appendix A. A current-debt payment witness cannot make the historical
future after that intervention causally valid. Expenditure and rent accounting omit a counterfactual
return distribution and do not establish economic rationality.
No new controlled evaluations were performed for this rewrite. Earlier reference-policy pilots and
scenario checks remain archived historical QA, separate from the LLM cohort. They do not supply
a validated strategic reference policy or strengthen a claim about learning. The public repository
checkpoint preserves the twenty games and earlier research artifacts; the accompanying evidence map
distinguishes inherited results from checks performed for this manuscript.

10
Conclusion
The MonopolyBench cohort documents agents negotiating over relative development, liquidity and
the assets a rival may inherit. Their response styles differ, their explanations sometimes articulate
these dependencies, and selected exchanges show explicit bluffing, selective financing disclosure,
financial-denial appeals and changed offers under distress. Construction and liquidation records
reveal how those strategies encounter legal access and response execution. Some nonbuilds are stated
reserve choices, others are defaults after feasible allocations were encoded invalidly; most elimination
debts are unpayable by own assets, while three have payment witnesses and one is blocked by hotel
stock.
This account joins communication to economic trajectories without treating language as intention,
settlement as persuasion, or winning as a model-only capability measure. Its contribution is an empiri-
cal explanation of the observed interactions and the evidence needed to attribute their consequences.
Broader claims about optimality, learning and generalization remain open.

A
Rule and accounting details
The frozen ruleset is audit_corrected_v2. Free Parking pays nothing. Income and luxury taxes are $200
and $100. Jail permits up to three doubles attempts before a forced fine, and a player remaining jailed
has no management window that turn. Deeds returned to the bank are not immediately auctioned.
Public state includes opponents’ model names. Turn and decision counters are distinct; the latter are
zero-based throughout this paper.
Transfer interest and redemption use floating-point ceilings. Transfer interest computes mortgage
principal times 0.1 and agrees with the exact board-principal charge. Redemption computes principal
times 1.1 and exceeds the exact mathematical ceiling by one dollar for principals $50, $90, $100, $110
and $200. Thus a $100 mortgage costs $111 to redeem in this implementation. Historical financing
checks use the actual charge. The rewrite does not newly test whether the discrepancy changes any
affordability boundary.
The engine checks building-purchase evenness after each plan entry. Sale validation can instead
accept a bundled operation based on final evenness and net bank stock. An atomic sale search can
therefore miss a valid bundled sale. This matters for a claimed exhaustive negative result, even when
its positive witnesses all execute. The historical restricted graph result concerns G7’s mortgage-only
decision, where building sales are unavailable.
A color group can contain an inherited deed and still become complete through a later purchase
or trade. The four winner spending units with that mixed route are G3 p1 brown, G6 p1 light blue,
G12 p2 light blue and G14 p1 green. They explain the difference between thirty-four units containing
inherited deeds at first spend and thirty first completed by inheritance. Seats p1–p4 are one-based; the
decision index is not.
