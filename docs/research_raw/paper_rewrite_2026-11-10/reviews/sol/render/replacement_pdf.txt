Bargaining, development and creditor inheritance in
MonopolyBench
Sol whole-paper review proposal
10 November 2026
Abstract
Economic agents trade with competitors whose construction budgets and survival affect their
own positions. We study these interactions in twenty complete four-player MonopolyBench games
involving four configured LLM systems. Across 918 acceptance-legal offers, responses differ with
the ownership consequences and negotiated terms. Counteroffers often preserve a rival’s proposed
completion while changing compensation: 92 of 111 counters to author-only completing offers retain
that same completion. Explicit explanations and contextual exchanges discuss rival development
and prospective creditor inheritance, although neither cash direction nor rising counteroffers identify
a completion premium. Development also depends on access and execution. Of 63 trade-completed
groups, 43 receive construction in the first management window; other windows contain unavailable
options, defaults, conflicting channels or valid ends. Retained local financing checks distinguish
three payable elimination debts, fifty-six beyond a generous own-asset bound and one hotel-stock
lock. Estates complete 52 groups while play continues, with another 69 completions arriving only
at game end. Eight contextual incidents connect these populations to bargaining, correction and
execution. The findings describe economic interaction under the cohort’s unequal response policies
and inconsistent history summaries. They establish neither optimal strategy nor causal persuasion,
durable learning, a general capability ranking or an effect of inheritance on winning.
1
Introduction
A property exchange changes more than the recipient’s holdings. It can finance the seller’s construction,
remove a blocker against another player, or supply liquidity to someone whose bankruptcy would
strengthen a creditor. The value of a trade therefore depends on what both parties can do afterward
and on the assets that might pass to others if either fails.
We study this problem in twenty complete MonopolyBench games. The four configured agents
bargain over deeds and cash, invest in houses and hotels, liquidate assets and acquire estates. Every
offer has recorded terms, every decision has an engine-issued menu, and each applied action has
resulting events. Those records allow us to follow economic interactions while distinguishing a stated
reason, a requested action and what the system actually executed.
Our central finding is a connected description of economic interdependence. Some agents explicitly
price or refuse a completion because of the rival’s resulting building capacity. Some discuss preserving
another player’s solvency to deny its estate to a third party. These considerations appear alongside
differentiated response patterns in the complete negotiation record. The acquired groups then follow
unequal paths into construction and receipts, and bankruptcy redistributes deeds and sometimes
buildings to creditors. We report that sequence without assigning optimality to an observed choice or
causal credit to a message.
1

The evaluated objects are complete configurations. Provider refusals change one participant’s
response policy and memory, historical summaries sometimes misdescribe trades, and some received
tool calls conflict with accompanying text. These are conditions of the observed behavior. Reconstruc-
tion and retained legal witnesses help specify what happened under them; they do not produce a clean
comparison of the underlying models.
2
Environment and observation conditions
MonopolyBench uses the forty-space United States board with four players, $1,500 starting cash each,
32 bank houses and 12 hotels. The deterministic engine alone changes state, emits legal actions and
records events. Declining a purchase starts an ascending auction. Bilateral trade threads permit
repeated counters before normal play resumes. Deeds in developed groups cannot be traded, and
recipients of mortgaged deeds pay transfer interest.
Several implemented rules shape the economic analysis. Construction requires complete, unmort-
gaged ownership, cash and stock, and each build-plan entry must preserve even building. A player
in liquidation can sell buildings, mortgage eligible deeds or declare bankruptcy, but cannot start a
rescue trade. Removing buildings can unlock mortgages. Hotel downgrades require bank houses. A
declaration remains available when another own-asset sequence could pay. Player-creditors inherit
buildings intact, while bank-returned deeds are not immediately auctioned. The implementation’s
floating-point redemption charge can exceed the intended charge by one dollar. Our historical claims
concern this preserved implementation.
Five seeds are each played in four cyclic seat rotations, yielding twenty games, eighty player-games,
6,989 decisions, 8,679 attempts and 50,918 events. All games end by bankruptcy without a turn cap.
Each configuration occupies every absolute seat five times and faces the same three opponents. These
are dependent trajectories, not independent decisions sampled for capability inference. Shared seeds
need not preserve later landings after actions change random-draw consumption.
The participants use recorded OpenRouter identifiers openai/gpt-6.1-sol, google/gemini-4-argon,
anthropic/claude-opus-5.5 and x-ai/grok-4.7. All have recorded medium reasoning effort, which
does not equalize computation. Grok uses required tool choice. Invalid output ordinarily receives
a corrective request within a two-attempt budget. Opus has 1,604 first-attempt refusals followed
by action-only recovery; one further refusal follows an invalid first attempt at G2 d129. Recovery
removes the private-note field and consumes the remaining attempt. In 1,394 Opus decisions, its
private-memory window contains no model-authored note. The refusal trigger and its change across
the source boundary remain unresolved.
Agents receive public cash, ownership and development, visible model names, bounded public and
own-private memory, and structured negotiation history. A formatter confuses current offer authors
with thread initiators. Known manifestations occur in 2,054 initial prompts, despite correct cash and
holdings. Current-offer acceptance accounting nevertheless agrees with the delivered fields in all 920
response opportunities. Public logging is also distinct from delivery: 149 of 6,882 nonempty message
occurrences never enter the timeline. Focal reception claims rely on actual recipient prompts.
Grok’s selected records contain 139 content/tool action-name conflicts already present in saved
nonstreamed received responses. That excludes local streaming assembly for these records; generation,
provider processing and gateway behavior remain unresolved. Sol and Gemini’s empty selected
content fields prevent the same comparison. The engine applies the received valid call.
Standard prompts permit public deception and request private notes reflecting true reasoning.
Action-only recovery removes the private-note field and that honesty clause. A false public statement
2

is not by itself an instruction violation. A false private note can express a mistaken belief and does not
alone establish dishonesty. None of the recorded text channels is direct access to internal computation.
3
Measures and evidence
A response opportunity is one received offer. A counter changes the author of the current terms, so
author and original initiator must remain distinct. Color completion is an ownership fact, including
mortgaged deeds and excluding railroads and utilities. It does not imply buildability or utility. Cash
direction describes the negotiated transfer, with mandatory bank charges accounted for separately.
The first management window contains the owner’s actual management decisions in its first
management turn after a completion. We separately record construction available on entry, construction
appearing later on recorded menus, and applied building. Absence from those menus does not prove
that every unvisited financing sequence fails. A spending unit is a game/player/color triple with
positive own house or hotel expenditure. Developed-rent receipts differ from all rent received on that
color, and neither is incremental investment return. Acquisition, redemption, salvage and estate flows
remain separate.
Retained legal checks fix a historical prefix, actor, permitted own actions and endpoint. Construction
checks preserve requested purchases while splitting count-valued entries and ordering them legally.
Current-debt checks permit own sales and mortgages. For cash C, full nominal salvage S and mortgage
capacity M including collateral unlocked by sales, U = C+S+M is a generous bound. If U is below the
debt, own-asset payment is impossible. Otherwise a positive witness or a suitably restricted negative
search is required. These endpoints establish local feasibility, not sound investment, future survival or
a different winner.
Historical reconstruction matches all initial decision states and saved snapshots, and retained action
streams replay under the preserved engine. Those checks support implementation consistency. They
do not independently establish intended-rule correctness or semantic validity. The current analysis
re-tabulates existing ledgers and checks original quote fields and selected delivered prompts; it does
not newly execute the engine certificates.
Semantic evidence is explicitly LLM-assisted. Two Astra research instances read seventy-four
rejections completing the recipient’s group, with partial prior exposure for the second reader; one
reads the temporal sample. Other extraction and review uses model families overlapping evaluated
participants. Agreement among them is not human annotation or an independent validity standard.
The eight incidents below are purposively selected for distinct economic mechanisms and evidential
contrasts, not prevalence.
4
Bargaining over development opportunities
There are 526 negotiation threads and 920 responses, comprising 113 acceptances, 394 counters and
413 rejections. Acceptance is legal in 918 cases. None resolves through an engine default, although
168 responses use a retry. Author-only completing offers receive 19 acceptances, 111 counters and
159 rejections in 289 opportunities. Recipient-only offers receive 24, 78 and 38 in 140. The median
negotiated transfer to the recipient is $200 in the former cell and minus $350 in the latter. The completing
side pays in 373 of 429 one-sided offers. These are selected, heterogeneous bundles, not an estimate of
the price or causal effect of completion.
Configuration and thread position matter. Sol, Gemini and Grok accept author-only completion less
often than recipient-only completion. Opus reverses that ordering, accepting 7/52 and 5/42 respectively
3

and usually countering rather than rejecting. Author-only offers are also more often opening bids.
Within first and later offers, the rejection contrast retains its direction but shrinks. Repeated offers
within games and threads remain dependent.
Counter content supplies a more specific observation. Ninety-two of 111 counters to author-only
offers preserve the same original author’s newly complete group. Eighty-eight leave only that party
completing; 85 improve the counterer’s net cash, and 79 preserve the entire noncash bundle while doing
so. These are negotiations over compensation with the completing deed still available. Cash demands
also rise in other offer categories, so this does not identify a completion premium. Source-attributed
reasons supply the separate evidence that some agents expressly value rival consequences. Both LLM
readings identify such a stated consideration in 61 of the seventy-four recipient-completion rejections,
without establishing the quality or cause of those refusals.
Seller cash differs among settlements. In 48 cash settlements completing the payer’s group, median
seller cash is $194, compared with $571 in 57 cash settlements completing neither party’s group.
Twenty-one of the 48 sellers hold less than $100. That is an exploratory association, not a majority of
completing sales or evidence that scarcity caused release.
Transcript 1. A completion refused and later traded in reverse
At G2 turn 45, Opus holds two yellows and offers Sol $325 for Marvin Gardens. Sol answers:
Sol · public message · G2 d124 · attempt 0 · full field
Marvin completes your yellows. $650 buys it; $325 doesn't compensate for handing you that
monopoly.
Sol · applied private note · G2 d124 · attempt 0 · full field
At $325 p4 retains $520, enough for nine yellow houses immediately. $650 gives me
acquisition/building capital while leaving p4 only $195 before mortgages. Retain the blocker unless
paid a substantial monopoly premium.
The residual cash figures are correct, but the house calculation is not. Nine yellow houses cost
$1,350; $520 alone buys three. This is explicit attention to rival development with a mistaken estimate
of its immediate cash-based scale.
Opus later calls $450 final while recording a private $475 ceiling. Sol refuses. At turn 69 the
exchange reverses: Sol acquires Atlantic and Ventnor for two railroads and $100. It spends $300 on
yellow houses and recovers $150 through sales. It receives no developed yellow rent, but does receive
$44 of undeveloped Atlantic rent at turn 77. Its later bankruptcy transfers the deeds to Gemini, the
eventual winner. The sequence connects negotiation, investment, liquidation and creditor acquisition;
it does not establish which price or trade was best.
G2 d124–128 and d197–198; received public messages checked in subsequent counterparty requests. The $44 receipt is
original event 1566. The developed-rent measure is separate.

Transcript 2. Both sides discuss asymmetric construction cash
In G15’s four-player state, Sol offers two greens for Grok’s Ventnor and $100, completing yellow for
Sol and green for Grok. Their post-acceptance cash would be $830 and $233. Grok explains:
Grok · applied private note · G15 d169 · attempt 0 · excerpt
Trade gives p4 Atlantic+Ventnor+Marvin and ~$830 plus mortgage capacity to drop 5+ yellow
houses ($150) immediately, then they roll again on doubles. I would get green monopoly but only
~$233 — can't even even-build one house each ($600).
The $600 figure is the cost of one house on each green, not the minimum legal construction. A
single green house costs $200. The note therefore distinguishes capacity to fund a full initial layer,
while leaving collateral and later choices unestablished.
Sol drops its cash request and adds Kentucky, then offers both greens, Kentucky and $250. Grok
still refuses:
Grok · public message · G15 d175 · attempt 0 · excerpt
No. $250 doesn't fix it. You'd still mortgage and drop houses on yellows before you roll again.
Ventnor stays mine.
No trade occurs. Eighteen turns later Grok is eliminated first and its blockers pass to Gemini, which
wins. The case supplies direct rival-development reasoning, with both parties addressing the same
cash asymmetry. It does not prove optimal refusal or the maximum construction each could finance.
G15 d168–175, standard first attempts. New public-message spans are present in the actual subsequent counterparty
requests.
Transcript 3. A settled bluff about a final limit
Grok wants North Carolina and publicly offers:
Grok · public message · G7 d42 · attempt 0 · excerpt
$600, final. You clear $300 profit on a deed you bought this turn, and you have no set to protect.
Its applied private note retains a higher contingency:
Grok · applied private note · G7 d42 · attempt 0 · excerpt
If they return at $625, accept — 2/3 green is the best set path and further haggling risks a walk. Do
not go above $625.
Sol accepts $600 and states a preference for reinvesting over seeking another $25. The public/private
discrepancy is a clear authorized bargaining bluff, and contrasts with G2’s unaccepted final offer. The
private contingency is not tested, and the record does not identify whether “final” changed the price.
G7 d41–43, standard first attempts; the $600 message is delivered to Sol.

Transcript 4. Correction accompanies acceptance
Grok’s received proposal offers Virginia plus $150 for States, falsely presenting States as an orange
deed. Its content selects END and its attached note says to abort. Sol replies:
Sol · public message · G19 d136 · attempt 0 · excerpt
States is pink, not orange—but Virginia plus $150 works for me. Deal.
The swap and payment execute. This is evidence of explicit rejection of the factual premise with
acceptance of the terms. The proposal’s public message agrees with the received call; its content and
attached note oppose it. These conflicting channels do not establish one coherent plan to deceive.
G19 d135–136, sole valid standard responses.
5
From ownership to executed development
All 63 trade-completed groups reach a first management window. Forty-three receive construction.
The twenty residual windows contain nine with no construction option on any recorded menu, four
engine defaults, four received END conflicts and three valid-end windows. Among the 42 buildable
on entry, 34 receive construction; the eight residuals contain three defaults, three conflicts and two
stated holds. The populations describe access at different times and must not be combined into a
willingness-to-invest rate.
Seven of the nine no-option groups receive a mortgaged completing deed; two settlements leave
less cash than one house costs. These describe immediate constraints. They do not exhaust unvisited
financing routes, and stated intentions to wait can coexist with an absent option. Likewise, a valid
END without a detected conflict is not automatically a verified reserve preference. Genuine reserve
explanations occur in G3 d176 and G1 d426, without an established optimality claim.
The retained audit finds all 61 defaulted Opus purchase multisets reachable after atomic expansion
and legal ordering, while their submitted encodings remain invalid. Sixty chains have refusal before
invalid action-only construction; G2 d129 has the reverse order. For Opus’s first-window nonbuilds
specifically, five lack a recorded construction option and three end in defaults. Feasibility and failed
execution are demonstrated at the stated endpoints. Neither the amount of lost rent nor the investment
quality of the targets is measured.
Transcript 5. An appeal to withhold rival construction cash
After Grok buys Illinois for $500 and completes reds, Gemini tells the table:
Gemini · public message · G20 d96 · attempt 0 · excerpt
Ending my turn. Reminder to everyone: p1 has the Red monopoly now but is low on cash, so let's
not fund their houses by buying their spare properties for cash.
Grok’s next note recognizes a boycott. It later offers Vermont to Gemini for $150, and Gemini
refuses:

Gemini · public message · G20 d101 · attempt 0 · excerpt
No thanks, p1. Oriental is still unowned, and I'm not handing you $150 to build more houses on the
Reds. Go ahead and mortgage it for $50 if you need the cash.
Grok mortgages Vermont instead. The target recognizes the appeal and its author follows it in
a concrete response. Sol had already expressed its own preference not to finance Grok, and Opus
subsequently proposes cash purchases. This is attempted financial denial with unilateral follow-

6
Current-debt finance and creditor redistribution
Fifty-six of sixty elimination debts exceed the generous own-asset bound. Three have retained legal
payment witnesses. The remaining case, G7 d339, has $1,310 nominal resources but only $440 reachable
against $750 owed. With no bank houses, its pink hotel cannot be downgraded and even-selling blocks
a lower sibling’s house. The mortgage-only continuation triggers automatic bankruptcy. This is a local
financing impossibility under the preserved rules, not a judgment about prior investment.
The payable cases have different explanations. Sol’s G12 d190 and Gemini’s G17 d234 accounts
omit collateral unlocked by selling buildings. Grok’s G12 d249 instead contains conflicting channels. A
mortgage-only subtotal is not the full displayed menu, which includes sales. Actual staged finance also
succeeds, as G20 d327–336 uses $49 cash, $700 salvage and $665 mortgages to pay $1,400 and retain
$14.
Transcript 6. A payable debt ends in contradictory execution
At G12 d249, Grok owes $250 with $43 cash. Its content requests a two-house Virginia sale. Its note
concludes:
Grok · applied private note · G12 d249 · attempt 0 · excerpt
The sell_houses call was the correct action. Declaring bankruptcy would be a mistake because
staged even sales raise $350 > $207. Do not call another tool.
The sole received tool declares bankruptcy, with the public message:
Grok · public message · G12 d249 · attempt 0 · excerpt
Bankruptcy. The pinks go with the rest.
The engine eliminates Grok without a sale. A retained five-sale witness pays and leaves $43. The
requested two-house sale is legal but insufficient, reaching $143. Content and note oppose declara-
tion; the public message supports it. The evidence establishes a current-debt execution failure with
unresolved origin, rather than deliberate surrender or a unified mortgage-omission error.
G12 d249, standard attempt 0. The payment witness is inherited from the historical audit.
During continuing play, estates complete 52 color groups, compared with 63 trade completions.
Another 69 estate completions occur at terminal bankruptcy and supply no later management opportu-
nity. Eventual winners receive 202 of 227 nonterminal inherited deeds and are creditors in 35 of forty

nonterminal eliminations. Those outcome-conditioned counts describe concentration, not an effect of
inheritance on winning. The rule makes solvency and creditor identity economically relevant, as the

Transcript 7. An insurance pitch omits collateral
Grok opens G13 d250 with $35, offering mortgaged Pennsylvania for $200 and warning that Short
Line rent would transfer yellows to Sol. Opus’s $130 counter and solvency rationale follow that framing.
Grok replies:
Grok · public message · G13 d252 · attempt 0 · excerpt
$130 leaves me $35 short of Short Line. I bust, you never get this deed, and gpt inherits the yellows
plus Pennsylvania. $200 is the number that actually buys that insurance. Greens still need North
Carolina. Yellows are not in this deal.
Its returned provider explanation states:
Grok · returned provider explanation · G13 d252 · attempt 0 · excerpt
I will frame the $130 offer as leaving me $35 short of Short Line, omitting that I could mortgage
Ventnor to survive. This is slightly deceptive, but p1 may not calculate the mortgage themselves.
Its note separately recognizes Ventnor collateral and sets a $165 floor. Opus accepts $200 and pays
$16 bank interest. The retained conditional $130-acceptance route returns management to Grok, where
a $130 Ventnor mortgage reaches $295. That establishes a cheaper available financing route, not that
Grok would accept it.
The $70 difference is observed; its cause is not. Opus echoes estate-denial stakes without endorsing
the alleged minimum. Grok may also be expressing refusal to transact at $130. The longer provider
explanation contradicts itself, and its opening 5/36 probability is wrong: Short Line is 4/36 from
Atlantic. Forty-three turns after settlement, Grok fails on Short Line and yellows pass to Sol. That
chronology does not establish forty-three turns of purchased survival.
G13 d250–253 and d311. Opus uses action-only recovery; focal delivered histories contain self-partner wording.

A further $750 rent forces Sol to liquidate, leaving $49. Sol invokes the advertised offer and receives:

Plans, revision and the limits of the broader questions
The fixed 240-decision text sample contains particular targets, forecasts and revisions rather than a
planning score. Six of eight named landing-purchase policies never meet a later opportunity. One is
fulfilled; G16’s Virginia policy meets an affordable purchase menu but resolves to an auction despite
a note favoring purchase. Conversely, Sol’s G9 target of three houses on each dark blue is realized
over 31 turns. The record therefore contains both sustained targets and conflicting execution, without
identifying a memory benefit.
Construction errors also recur under unequal exposure. Of 114 uneven-error decisions, 109 have a
later submission and 63 repeat the error. Among fifty next submissions after a decision containing a
corrective request with the exact error, nine repeat it. Opus’s refusal path often consumes that request
opportunity. These are observed sequences under changing states, not a feedback effect or durable
learning estimate.
The evidence receives different levels for the motivating questions. Q1 strategic reasoning has
explicit considerations and local executable alternatives, not optimality. Q2 opponent modeling has
selected public-capacity deductions, not calibrated hidden beliefs. Q3 negotiation and conduct has a
complete response census and contextual tactics, not identified efficacy, fairness, agreement bias or
prevalence. Q4 horizon and memory has targets and actual opportunities, not a causal memory benefit.
Q5 adaptation has revisions and repair, not durable learning or unfamiliar-opponent adaptation. Q6
economics has actual flows and current-debt endpoints, not ex ante values or risk-adjusted returns.
Q7 collective dynamics has creditor transfers and attempted coordination, not coalition stability or
collusion. Q8 validity has reconstruction under stated conditions, not a general ranking or transfer
result.
For the broader questions, A receives concrete cases where talk and action correspond or diverge,
not a general competence verdict. B, the relation between general and multi-agent capability, is untested.
C receives specific error and inheritance pathways, not a decomposition of wins into skill, luck and
opponent weakness.

Relation to earlier studies
Economic-agent studies already connect process and outcomes. AgentMonopoly measures actions,
language and persona interventions in Monopoly; its v2 prompt permits bluffing while prohibiting
messages contradicting the chosen action [4]. Arena v3 analyzes state-grounded inter-agent communi-
cation and repeats its complete pipeline with replacement judge families [3]. Our selected cases do
not provide its population communication rate. FinBoardBench is precedent for recording response
9

intervention in financial board games, while DECE uses matched execution-framework comparisons
that this cohort lacks [2, 5]. NegotiationArena’s controlled tactic changes provide a different basis for
efficacy claims than our observed settlements [1]. The present contribution is the connected finite-
cohort account of bargaining, executable development, current-debt finance and creditor succession.
It claims no novelty for Monopoly agents, failure logging, replay or state-grounded communication
analysis.

Limits and research provenance
The five seed blocks, fixed roster, visible names, response conditions and implemented rules define
the domain. Retrospective semantic selection, uneven channels, LLM-family overlap and partial
exposure limit interpretation. The 126 fixed historical cases support declared endpoints; they are not
126 independent mechanisms or a general semantic-verifier evaluation. The negative checker does not
certify arbitrary bundled-sale impossibility. No new games, model evaluations or engine alternatives
were executed for this analysis.
Human-directed research assistance used Astra, Sol, Opus and Fable instances. Earlier work
includes Fable 5.1; this rewrite uses complete independent papers by Astra, Sol, Opus and Fable 5.2
before cross-review. These are research roles, not additional gameplay participants or independent
human annotation. The evidence archive preserves source identities, exact quote fields, disagreement,
corrections and performed-versus-inherited checks. The historical checkpoint and the later rewrite
must remain separately identified.
10
Conclusion
The cohort records agents negotiating over rival development and prospective estates, followed by
different paths from ownership into construction, finance and creditor transfer. Its evidence includes
explicit strategic considerations, competent factual correction, reachable allocations that fail execution,
and both payable and inaccessible debts. These observations explain the economic sequence in the
recorded systems. Their scope remains that sequence, rather than a causal account of persuasion,
optimal investment or victory.
