Who completes, who builds, who inherits:
bargaining and economic execution by four LLM systems
in twenty games of Monopoly
MonopolyBench project
Independent full draft (Fable 5.2), 10 November 2026
Abstract
An agent that trades against rivals has to judge what a deal gives the other side, turn what it
acquires into income, and stay solvent. We study these three steps in twenty complete four-player
Monopoly games played by four configured LLM systems (6,989 decisions, every game ended
by bankruptcy), using legal menus, response records, delivered prompts and canonical events.
Bargaining is priced and sensitive to the rival’s gain. Across 918 acceptance-legal offers, the side
that would complete a color group is the side paying cash in 373 of 429 one-sided cases. Offers that
complete only the writer’s group are rejected outright in 159 of 289 cases and accepted in 19, while
offers that complete only the recipient’s group are usually countered. The four systems accept 26
to 30 offers each and differ in how they decline. Completing deeds change hands mainly when
their holder is short of cash. In 21 of 48 completing sales the seller held under $100. A completed
group is not yet a developed one. The systems complete 13 to 18 groups each by trade, build on
them at the first opportunity in 16 of 17, 17 of 18, 7 of 15 and 3 of 13 cases, and later collect $20,818,
$26,070, $5,930 and $4,560 in rent on them. Mortgaged or illiquid purchases, engine defaults and
response-channel conflicts account for 17 of the 20 unbuilt groups. Bankruptcy then moves more
property than trade. Fifty-six of sixty eliminations could not have been paid from the debtor’s
own assets, estates complete 121 groups against 63 by trade, and the eventual winner receives the
first estate in 18 games. Seven contextual incidents show what the systems say at these points.
The counts describe configured systems under unequal response conditions. They do not rank
models, and they do not establish causal effects, optimal play or transfer to other opponents.
1
Introduction
An agent that trades against rivals faces three problems in sequence. It must judge a deal by what the
deal gives the other side as well as by what it gets. It must turn what it acquires into income, which
takes cash, a legal opportunity and a correctly specified action. And it must stay solvent, because a
debt it cannot pay hands everything it built to someone else. Evaluations of language-model agents
usually examine one of these problems at a time, in a bargaining game, a business simulation or a
planning task.
Monopoly puts all three on one path. A player completes a color group, often by trade. Houses on
the group raise its rent many times over. A rival who lands there and cannot pay goes bankrupt, and
in the implementation studied here the creditor inherits the debtor’s deeds with their buildings intact.
This paper asks where along that path twenty games were decided, and what four LLM systems did
and said at each step.

=== PAGE 2 ===
The data are twenty complete four-player games among four configured systems, which we call
Sol, Gemini, Opus and Grok after their underlying models. The games contain 6,989 decisions and
all end by bankruptcy without a turn limit. For every decision the record holds the legal menu, each
response attempt, the action the engine applied and the events that followed. We reconstruct all 526
negotiation threads, all 209 episodes in which a player came to own a complete color group, all 60
eliminations and every transfer of cash and deeds. Three findings organize the paper.
Bargaining is priced and sensitive to the rival’s gain (Section 4). More than half of all offers would
complete a color group for someone, and the side that would complete is nearly always the side
paying. An offer that completes only its writer’s group is rejected outright more than half the time
and accepted about one time in fifteen. An offer that completes only the recipient’s group is usually
answered with a counter. The four systems accept at similar frequencies and decline in different
ways, and completing deeds are released mostly by holders who are nearly out of cash.
A completed group is not yet a developed one (Section 5). The four systems complete similar numbers
of groups by trade. Two build on nearly every one at the first opportunity. The other two build on
fewer than half, and their groups collect about a fifth as much rent. Most of the unbuilt groups are
not decisions to wait. They are purchases that arrived mortgaged or left no cash, engine defaults
after invalid build plans, most of them following a refused first attempt, and responses whose text
named one action while the tool call named another.
Bankruptcy moves more property than trade (Section 6). Fifty-six of sixty eliminations could not have
been paid from the debtor’s own assets even on a generous bound. Estates complete almost twice as
many color groups as trades do, and the eventual winner receives the first estate in eighteen games.
The systems bargain over this. They sell a deed as insurance against a rival’s inheritance, pay above
their own stated ceiling to keep a deed out of an estate, and withdraw an offer once its recipient is
nearly broke.
Seven incidents with verbatim excerpts accompany the counts. The task permitted public bluffing
and deception, so a misleading public statement is bargaining conduct here and is not a breach of
instructions.
These findings matter for anyone who expects language-model agents to hold and trade assets.
The systems did not close deals readily. They declined or countered 88 percent of offers, often with
stated reasons about a rival’s position, though frequencies alone cannot show whether they declined
the right ones. The value of a negotiated asset was then set by the execution layer as much as by the
bargain, and one configuration lost most of that value to an interaction between a provider refusal
and an action format. And when default transfers assets to the creditor, a counterparty’s solvency
becomes something to bargain over.
This is an observational study of four complete configurations, including their prompts, memory,
retry policies and provider behavior. Two configurations ran under conditions that change what
their records mean, and Section 2 states those conditions before any result. The counts are censuses
of a finite cohort with strong dependence within games and seed blocks. We do not rank models,
estimate the effect of a rival’s completion on refusal, or claim that any trade, purchase or bankruptcy
was optimal. Section 8 maps the evidence onto eight broader questions about strategic agents and
says which ones this cohort cannot answer.
The work builds on economic and bargaining evaluations that record process as well as outcome [2,
3, 8, 13, 27]. It adds a connected account of one complete economy, from every offer through every
traded monopoly to every bankruptcy.

2
Environment, cohort and conditions
2.1
The game as implemented
MonopolyBench is a deterministic four-player Monopoly engine. The engine alone holds the state,
emits each decision with its menu of legal actions, validates the selected action and records the
resulting events. Players start with $1,500 on the standard United States board, with 32 houses and
12 hotels in the bank. Declining a purchase opens an ascending auction. No turn limit applies, and
all twenty games end when three players are bankrupt.
Five rules shape the results. Trades are bilateral threads of offers and counters, at most twenty
exchanges long, resolved before play resumes. Mortgaged deeds can be traded, and the receiver
pays ten percent of the mortgage value. A player may build only on a complete color group with no
mortgaged deed, and each entry of a build plan must keep the group even. A player in debt can sell
buildings, mortgage deeds or declare bankruptcy, and the menu offers the declaration even when the
debt could be paid. Finally, a bankrupt player’s deeds pass to the creditor with their buildings intact,
where many rule sets return the buildings to the bank.
2.2
Four configured systems
The unit of evaluation is a configured system: a model with its prompt, memory, tool schema, retry
policy and provider behavior [10, 30]. Table 1 lists the four. All ran through OpenRouter at the
recorded medium reasoning setting, with no temperature set. The shared label does not mean a
matched budget. Recorded completion tokens average 128 per decision for Sol and 4,041 for Grok.
Every system gets one corrective retry after an invalid action. If the retry is also invalid, the engine
applies a deterministic fallback, which on a management menu is end_turn. Two configurations depart
from this path.
Opus played mostly through a recovery path.
In 1,605 of its 1,696 decisions the provider refused
one attempt, returning the same Terms-of-Service message about duplicating model outputs each
time. The refusal hit 215 of 306 Opus decisions in games 1 to 4 and all 1,390 in games 5 to 20. The
harness then resends an action-only request, which drops the private-note field and the sentence
asking for honest private notes. That request uses up the corrective retry. Three consequences follow.

Opus’s applied actions carry no private note in 1,544 decisions, so its reasons are mostly unobserved.
Its later prompts lack its own earlier notes. And when the action-only attempt is invalid, nothing
remains but the fallback. We do not know what triggers the refusal. The change at game 5 coincides
with a source revision that touched no engine, prompt or resolver file, and the record does not identify

Grok’s text and tool call sometimes disagree.
Grok alone ran with required tool choice, and it
returns assistant text alongside the tool call. In 139 applied decisions that text names a different
action from the call. The engine applies the call. In 60 of them the text says to end the turn and the
call proposes a trade. In 22 the text says to build and the call ends the turn. In 11 the text says to buy
a property and the call starts an auction. In 7 the call accepts a trade that the text rejects or counters.
Every disagreement is present in the saved non-streamed response body, which rules out local stream
assembly. Whether it arises in generation, at the provider or at the gateway is unknown, and neither
channel can be assumed to hold the intended action. Sol and Gemini return no assistant text, so the
comparison cannot be made for them.
2.3
What the systems were told and what they saw
The standard prompt sets the goal to “win the game by maximizing long-term advantage and being
the last non-bankrupt player” and allows “any legal strategy or personality (aggressive, deceptive,
cooperative, conservative, manipulative, opportunistic, friendly, hostile, etc.)”. Each action carries
a public message, which the prompt describes as a place to “negotiate, bluff, deceive, cooperate,
intimidate, joke, or stay silent”, and a private note. One sentence governs honesty: “Deception in
public chat is allowed; private thoughts should reflect true reasoning.” A false public statement
therefore breaks no instruction. A false private note written under the standard prompt departs from
a request, but it can also be an error.
Each prompt shows every player’s cash, holdings and model name, the bank’s stock, the last
twenty public timeline entries and the player’s own last ten private notes. Trade responses also show
the thread so far. Two defects affect this view. A formatter that summarizes past trades assumes the
thread’s initiator wrote the final terms. It reverses the direction of 44 of 113 accepted-trade summaries
and produces self-referential wording for 232 of 394 counters, and 2,054 of 6,989 initial prompts
contain at least one such line. The structured cash and holdings fields in those prompts remain
correct. Separately, 149 of 6,882 public messages never reach a timeline. These counts come from
the earlier audit. Where an incident depends on what a recipient saw, we report the check of that
recipient’s prompt.
2.4
Cohort and dependence
Five seeds are each played in four cyclic seat rotations, so every system sits in every seat five times.
Games run from 75 to 386 turns. Sol wins eight, Gemini seven, Opus three and Grok two (Ap-
pendix A).
These totals are not a ranking, and the cohort shows why. In seed block 3 the dice give seat 2
both dark-blue deeds by turn 29 in all four rotations. Each of the four systems buys them, and each
wins from that seat. In block 4 the player in seat 1 completes the orange group by direct purchase on
turn 47 in three rotations and wins all three. In block 2, by contrast, Sol wins from every seat. Seven
games thus follow the seat that drew an early monopoly whichever system sat there, and four follow

attach no confidence intervals to dependent decisions.
3
Measurement
3.1
Ledgers
All counts come from five ledgers built from the canonical event, action and decision logs.
The offer ledger has one row for each of the 920 times a player had to answer a trade offer. A
row records the offer’s writer and recipient, the bundles each would give, and the consequences of
accepting for both sides: cash after mandatory charges and any color group newly completed or
lost. The writer is the author of the current offer, who need not be the player that opened the thread.
In 44 of 113 settlements the accepted terms were written by the other party. Acceptance is on the
legal menu in 918 rows. The two exceptions would leave a party with negative cash, and they are
excluded wherever a rate requires that acceptance was possible. We classify each offer by who would
newly complete a color group if it were accepted: the writer only, the recipient only, both or neither.
Completion here means ownership of every deed in a color group. It does not mean the group can
be built on.
The completion ledger has one row for each of the 209 episodes in which a player came to own a
complete color group, with the route of the completing acquisition (trade, direct purchase, auction
or estate) and the rent paid on the group until the owner lost it or the game ended. For each of the 63
groups completed by trade, the first-window ledger follows the owner’s first management turn and
records whether building was legal, what was applied, and whether any decision in the window
ended in an engine default or a text and tool-call disagreement.
The elimination ledger covers all 60 bankruptcies with the debt, the debtor’s cash and assets, and a
classification described below. The flow ledger holds every paid rent, every trade transfer and every
estate transfer as typed rows. Cash and deeds are never summed.
The earlier audit established that the ledgers agree with the engine. Reconstructed cash, owner-
ship, buildings and bank stock match all 6,989 decision prompts, all 113 predicted post-trade states
match the next recorded state, and the applied action streams replay to the same 50,918 events in
every game.
3.2
Legal-sequence checks
Two questions need more than counting. Could an eliminated player have paid? Could a rejected
construction plan have been carried out? Both are answered by restoring the exact historical prefix
and running the player’s own legal actions through the unchanged engine to a stated endpoint.
For a debtor with cash C we use the bound
U = C + salvage of every building + mortgage value of every unmortgaged deed.
(1)
It ignores bank stock and even-selling rules, so it can only overstate what is reachable. If U is below
the debt, no sequence of the debtor’s own sales and mortgages pays it. If U is at or above the debt, the
bound decides nothing, and the case needs either an executed payment sequence or an exhaustive
search. For rejected build plans, the check splits each count-valued entry into single purchases, orders
them so the group stays even, and executes them with the same cash and bank stock.

3.3
Reading text
Four text channels accompany actions: the public message, the applied private note, any reasoning
text the provider returns, and Grok’s assistant text. None of them is a measurement of internal
computation, and they can disagree [12, 24]. We label the channel of every excerpt and check claims
about the board against the state.
Semantic coding was done by language models, not people. Research-assistant instances of GPT-6
Astra coded the stated reasons in 74 rejections (Section 4.5) and the forward-looking statements in
a 240-decision sample (Section 7). Earlier review passes used GPT-6.1 Sol, Claude Opus 5.5 and
Claude Fable 5.1, and this draft was written by Claude Fable 5.2. Several of these share a model or
developer with a participant, and no pass was blinded to that. We keep coded counts separate from
mechanical counts and base no conclusion on a coded rate alone.
The incidents were chosen to show the mechanism each section measures. They are not a sample,
and their number says nothing about frequency. Every excerpt is a verbatim span of a named field.
For this draft all spans were re-checked against the canonical files, and the spans newly added here
were extracted from those files by script (Appendix E.1).

4
Bargaining is priced and rival-sensitive
4.1
Volume, roles and settlement
The twenty games contain 526 negotiation threads and 920 responses. One thread in five settles (113).
An opening offer is rarely taken as written. Of 524 acceptance-legal first offers, 32 are accepted, 137 are
countered and 355 are rejected outright. Once a counter has been made the picture changes. Of the
394 later offers, 81 are accepted and only 57 are rejected outright. Eighty-one of the 113 settlements
follow at least one counter.
The four systems take different roles. Opus opens 240 threads, Grok 136, Sol 132 and Gemini 18,
and 44, 39, 19 and 11 of these settle. Measured against own turns on which a proposal was legal,
Opus proposes on 24 percent, Grok on 16, Sol on 10 and Gemini on 2. Grok’s count carries a condition
from Section 2. Sixty-four of its 136 threads were opened by a tool call that proposed a trade while
the accompanying text named another action, usually ending the turn. Seventeen of those threads
settled. Recipients answered these offers like any others, so they stay in the offer population, but
Grok’s count of openings is not a clean measure of decisions to negotiate.

4.2
The completing side pays
Bargaining in these games is mostly about monopolies. Of the 918 acceptance-legal offers, 510 would
complete a color group for at least one party: 289 for the writer only, 140 for the recipient only and 81
for both.
The cash terms follow the completion. When only the writer would complete a group, the writer
offers net cash in 238 of 289 offers, with a median of $200. When only the recipient would complete a
group, the writer asks for net cash in 135 of 140 offers, with a median of $350. In the 429 one-sided
offers the completing side is therefore the paying side 373 times (Figure 1, right). The accepted offers
have the same structure. The completing side pays in 18 of 19 accepted writer-only offers and 23 of
24 accepted recipient-only offers, at medians of $200 and $275. Among the 63 groups completed by
trade, the completing player paid net cash for 48 and received net cash for 9.
No list price asks for this premium. A deed that completes a group costs the same from the bank
as any other. The systems price the completion itself, on both sides of the table.
4.3
Responses depend on who would complete
Figure 1 (left) gives the response to every acceptance-legal offer. An offer that completes only the
writer’s group is accepted 19 times in 289 (7 percent) and rejected outright 159 times (55 percent).
An offer that completes only the recipient’s group is accepted 24 times in 140 (17 percent), countered
78 times (56 percent) and rejected 38 times (27 percent). Offers completing both sides’ groups fall
between the two, with 10 of 81 accepted and 36 rejected. Offers completing nothing are accepted 60
times in 408.
The figure does not measure the effect of a rival’s completion on refusal. The rows differ in price,
in what else changes hands, in who responds and in where the offer falls in a thread.

4.4
Four ways of declining
The systems accept at similar frequencies and differ in what they do otherwise (Table 2). Each accepts
between 26 and 30 offers, or 11 to 15 percent of those it receives. Sol and Opus mostly counter. Gemini
and Grok mostly reject. Gemini rejects three offers in four outright and opens almost no threads of
its own, yet it accepts about as many offers as any other system.
Two rows carry conditions. Opus answered 162 of its 189 offers through the action-only path, with
no private note. Three of the nineteen accepted writer-only offers are Grok tool calls that accepted
while the text rejected, and all three completed a group for Opus. Low acceptance does not settle the
worry, raised in studies of bilateral bargaining, that language models concede too readily [5, 19]. A
system could accept one offer in eight and still accept the wrong ones. The record shows only that
agreement is not the default response of any of the four.
4.5

outright. In 36 of them, accepting would also have completed a group for the writer, which is a fact

have a private note. One language-model reading finds a stated consequence for the rival in 63 of
the 74, such as the rival completing a set, gaining cash to build, or surviving. A second reading

4.6
Completing deeds are released by cash-poor holders
If holders refuse to sell the deed a rival needs, how do 63 groups get completed by trade? Mostly
when the holder runs short of cash. Call the party that receives net cash in a settlement the seller.
In the 48 settlements where the paying side completes a group, the seller’s median cash before the
offer was $194, and in 21 it was under $100. In the 57 settlements with a cash payment that complete
nothing, the seller’s median cash was $571 and 9 sellers held under $100. The $100 line is arbitrary. At
$50 the completing sales number 14 of 20 cash-poor sales, and at $200 they number 24 of 40. Across
all 30 settlements with a seller under $100, Opus is the seller in 16. Six of the 21 cash-poor sellers of a
completing deed went on to win.
Being short of cash does not make a system easier to deal with (Table 3). Offers of cash to a
recipient holding under $100 are accepted about as often as other cash offers, 12 of 85 against 47
of 407. They are rejected outright much less often and countered much more. Requests for cash
written by a player holding under $100 are accepted as often as other requests (18 of 132 against 30 of
207) and rejected more often. Recipient style confounds these comparisons. We read them only as
showing no surge of agreement when one side is nearly broke.

5
From completion to development
5.1
Similar completions, different conversion
A complete color group doubles its base rent. Houses multiply it. The value of a negotiated monopoly
therefore depends on what happens at the owner’s next management decision, which arrives in the
same turn for 35 of the 63 trade-completed groups and within five turns for all but two.
The four systems complete similar numbers of groups by trade: 17 for Sol, 18 for Gemini, 15 for
Opus and 13 for Grok. What follows differs sharply (Figure 2, Table 4). Sol builds on 16 of its 17
groups in the first management window and Gemini on 17 of 18. Opus builds on 7 of 15 and Grok on
3 of 13. The rent later paid on these groups, until the owner loses them or the game ends, is $20,818
for Sol and $26,070 for Gemini against $5,930 for Opus and $4,560 for Grok. The 43 groups built on in
the first window collect $53,810 of the $57,378 total. The 20 others collect $3,568, although 11 of them
are built on later. Construction spending as a whole divides the same way. Sol and Gemini account
for $74,400 of the $103,450 spent on houses and hotels (Appendix D).
The order of these figures matches the order of wins, which invites a causal story the data cannot
carry. Rent depends on where rivals land and how long the owner survives, seven wins followed an
early monopoly bought from the bank (Section 2), and the two low rows belong to the configurations
with nonstandard response conditions. The table does establish where the value of these bargains
went, and the reasons are identifiable.

5.3
The default path
Across all management decisions the engine applied a default end_turn 66 times, always after failed
construction attempts: 61 for Opus, four for Sol and one for Gemini. Fifty-five of them fall in two
games. The failures share a cause. The engine checks the even-building rule after each entry of a
build plan, so a plan such as three houses on one deed followed by three on the next is rejected even
when the final layout is even.
The earlier audit tested every one of these plans at its exact historical state. All 61 Opus plans
execute once each entry is split into single houses and ordered evenly. The cash, the bank stock and
the requested purchases are unchanged. Opus asked for affordable, legal allocations in a form the
validator rejected, after its one corrective retry had been spent on the provider refusal. Table 5 shows
the other systems meeting the same rule with a retry available. They usually end the decision with
some construction applied, though Gemini often changes what it buys.
The defaults matter beyond the first window. Of 663 end-of-turn actions applied on menus that
offered construction, 66 are these defaults and 24 are Grok decisions whose text named another
action. The remaining 573 show no detected conflict, which is not the same as 573 verified decisions
to hold cash. An unbuilt monopoly in these games is weak evidence of restraint.
Opus negotiated 15 monopolies and paid for them. It then lost most of their early value to an
interaction among a provider refusal, a retry budget and an action format. A count of deals would
rate the negotiation as productive and a count of wins would blame the model. The failure sits in the
execution layer of the configured system.

Stated plans meet opportunities unevenly.
An earlier pass read the public message and private

8
Discussion
8.1
What the account says about an economic agent
The systems judge a deal partly by what it gives the other side.
This shows in three independent
places: the cash terms, which charge for completion in both directions; the responses, which turn
against offers that complete the writer’s group; and the stated reasons, which name the rival’s set,
cash or survival. Studies of bilateral bargaining report that language models anchor on opening offers
and concede without compensation [5, 19]. The setting here has four players, a single winner and
full visibility of everyone’s cash and holdings, and in it no system treats agreement as the default. We
cannot say the systems refuse the right offers. Incident 1 shows a refusal that was coherent, checkable
and followed by the refuser’s bankruptcy.
The bargain’s value is realized or lost in execution.
Two systems turned negotiated monopolies
into houses almost every time. Two did not, and for 17 of 20 unbuilt groups the reason lies in the
acquisition terms or the response path. The largest single loss came from a provider refusal that
removed the one retry in which a build plan could have been corrected. Scoring these games by wins
would attribute that to the model, and scoring them by deals closed would miss it. Results from
one harness should not be read as properties of the model inside it, a point made elsewhere with
matched harness comparisons and with reliability analyses of agent leaderboards [7, 30].
When default transfers assets, solvency becomes a traded good.
Under intact creditor inheritance
a bankrupt player hands a portfolio to one rival, and estates completed 52 groups during play. The
systems bargained over this. A seller offered its own survival as insurance, buyers paid above their
limits to keep deeds out of estates, and a likely creditor withdrew an offer that would have prolonged
a debtor’s life. Cash-poor holders released the deeds that liquid holders refused to sell, without
becoming more agreeable. Several protective purchases were undone by a later bankruptcy. The
Vending-Bench Arena study reports that misaligned messages become more likely when an agent’s
inventory is low [13]. Our cash-poor counts have no comparable outcome measure, but both records
suggest that scarcity deserves separate attention when agents trade.

8.2
Coverage of the broader questions
The study was motivated by eight questions about strategic agents and three questions that cut across
them. Table 7 states, for each, what this cohort supports and at what level. “Census” means a count
over a complete population of recorded events. “Cases” means checked incidents with no rate.
Three rows need a sentence more. On question A, the record separates four things that a transcript
blurs: a statement about the board, a stated plan, a submitted action and the action the engine applied.
They can each be right or wrong independently. The Opus build plans were sound as plans and
invalid as submissions, and Grok’s game-12 response argued correctly for selling houses while its tool
call declared bankruptcy. Neither case says anything simple about whether the model is strategic.
On question B, the honest answer is that this study has nothing to add. Others report that larger
models do not consistently win more Monopoly games [27] and that performance rank does not
predict misaligned communication [13]. Four heterogeneous configurations cannot test either claim.
On question C, the seed blocks give an unusually direct view. The same dice put the same early
monopoly in the same seat four times, and four different systems won from it. In another block one
system won from all four seats. Both luck and system differences are visible in a twenty-game cohort,
and their shares cannot be estimated from it.
9
Related work
Bargaining by language models.
NegotiationArena studies bilateral scenarios and shows that
bargaining tactics change outcomes [3]. Abdelnabi et al. build scorable multi-party, multi-issue
negotiations that include greedy and adversarial players [1]. More recent bilateral studies find
that models represent a counterparty’s preferences accurately without converting that into better
agreements, and that they anchor at the extremes of the bargaining zone [5, 19]. In a market with one
seller and several buyers, standard models are reported to fixate on the current highest bid instead of
exploring the buyer pool [14]. Cattle Trade combines auctions, bluffing and bargaining in one game
and finds that coherent spending predicts rank better than any single skill [17]. These studies score
outcomes against a defined utility, which this paper does not. We add bargaining inside a long game,
where the traded asset is worth what its buyer can later build on it and each offer can be described by
what it would complete for each side.
Long-horizon economic agents.
Vending-Bench and CEO-Bench evaluate sustained operation
of a simulated business, and in CEO-Bench every model falls below a rule-based baseline [2, 4].
The Vending-Bench Arena communication study checks 2,583 inter-agent emails against simulator
state, replicates its classifier with other judge families, and ties misaligned messages to scarcity and
to the counterparty’s behavior [13]. FinBoardBench evaluates financial decisions in three board
games including Monopoly and reports that models favor acquiring assets over keeping liquidity [8].
AgentMonopoly studies the same underlying game across four model families and two sizes, with
persona interventions, and reports differences in how often models trade and how they manage
cash [27]. Both record invalid outputs and fallbacks. In the versions inspected for the earlier draft,
FinBoardBench returns buildings to the bank when a creditor inherits, AgentMonopoly transfers
them intact as this engine does, and AgentMonopoly’s trade interface accepts or rejects a single
offer where ours allows counters. Our account differs in grain. It follows every offer, every traded
monopoly and every bankruptcy to its consequence and identifies the response path behind each
failure to build.

Strategic and social game benchmarks.
GTBench, GAMA-Bench and SPIN-Bench measure strategic
reasoning across many games [6, 9, 29]. Agent Island ranks 49 models over 999 elimination games and
finds same-provider favoritism in votes [16]. MINDGAMES reports fragile rule-following and uneven
leaderboard validity from a large competition [25]. CoopEval compares mechanisms that sustain
cooperation in social dilemmas [23]. DECE evaluates model and harness configurations across ten
Eurogames and shows that the same model performs differently under different harnesses [30]. These
designs buy statistical power with many matches. Ours reconstructs a few long games completely
and cannot support the rankings theirs can.
Evaluating configured systems.
The Holistic Agent Leaderboard argues for evaluating complete
agent systems and inspecting their logs [10]. Hardy et al. show that rankings of fixed model and
scaffold systems are reliable while rankings of underlying models are much less so [7]. AgentBoard
reports process metrics beyond final success [15]. Yang et al. audit leaderboard claims with matched
controls and scorer validation [28]. Our legal-sequence checks serve the behavioral description. We
have not evaluated them as an auditing method on held-out cases.
Commitments, deception and explanations.
Work on agent promises separates infeasible commit-
ments from feasible ones that are not kept and asks who may release a promise [20, 22, 26]. Designed
settings elicit collusion against a verification protocol in most trajectories [21], and MASK separates
honesty from accuracy [18]. The norm matters for comparison. Our prompt permits public deception,
AgentMonopoly’s encourages bluffing while prohibiting messages that contradict the action, and
the Arena study applies an external taxonomy to agents given no conduct instruction. The same
sentence can be a violation in one and a legal move in another. Work on explanation faithfulness and
monitorability motivates our caution about text channels [11, 12, 24].
10
Limitations
A small, dependent cohort.
Twenty games come from five seeds. Decisions within a game share
a board, rotations within a block share early dice, and the four cyclic rotations cover four of the 24
possible seat orders. Every system faced the same three opponents, whose model names were visible.
The counts are censuses of this cohort. They carry no confidence intervals and support no ranking,
seat effect or forecast for other opponents.
Unequal conditions.
Opus played 95 percent of its decisions through an action-only recovery path
whose trigger is unknown. Grok’s text and tool calls disagree in 139 decisions for reasons the record
cannot locate. Token use differs by a factor of thirty across systems. Thirty percent of prompts contain
a garbled line of trade history. None of these was assigned as a treatment, so comparisons across
systems are comparisons across these conditions.
One rule set.
Intact creditor inheritance, per-entry even-building checks and a bankruptcy option
that is always on the menu shape the results. The inheritance findings in particular describe this
variant. The engine also overcharges some mortgage redemptions by one dollar through a floating-
point ceiling, which the replay faithfully reproduces. Replay shows that the record is consistent with
the implementation. It does not certify the implementation against an independent statement of the
rules.

Observation without intervention.
Offers differ in price, contents, responder and thread position
within every category we tabulate, so no contrast estimates an effect. The cash-poor threshold is
arbitrary, and splits by eventual winner condition on an outcome the same events produce. No trade,
refusal, purchase or bankruptcy is shown to be good or bad play, because no reference policy or
continuation value was computed.
Text.
Private notes are absent for most Opus decisions, returned reasoning is available unevenly,
and no channel is privileged. Semantic codes were assigned by language models that overlap with the
participants in model or developer, with one partly unblinded second reading and no human labels.
The incidents were selected to illustrate mechanisms, and most involve Grok because its channels are
the most observable.
Checks inherited by this draft.
The engine executions behind the payable and unpayable classifica-
tions, the rebuilt construction plans and the replay were performed in an earlier audit, and this draft
re-tabulated their retained outputs without rerunning them. The negative classification rests on an
arithmetic bound in 56 cases and on an exhaustive search of a one-action graph in one. A general
search over bundled building sales is not supported by the retained checker. Appendix B separates
what was recounted here from what was inherited.
No new evaluation.
The study is confined to existing records. Whether the bargaining is effective,
whether feedback produces learning, and whether any of this transfers to other opponents or rules
are questions that need interventions or new runs.
11
Conclusion
In twenty complete games, four configured LLM systems bargained hard, built unevenly and were
sorted by bankruptcy. They priced the completion of a color group on both sides of a trade, refused
or repriced most offers that would arm the proposer, and released blocking deeds mainly when
short of cash. Two systems converted nearly every negotiated monopoly into houses. Two lost most
of that value to illiquid purchases, to a refusal that consumed the only retry, and to tool calls that
contradicted their own text. Nearly every bankruptcy was unpayable when it came, and under intact
inheritance the estates completed almost as many monopolies during play as trade did and delivered
them to a single creditor in most games.
For evaluation, the practical consequence is that a multiplayer economic game has at least three
places to look, and they can disagree. The record of deals, the record of what was built and the record
of who inherited each tell a different part of how these games ended. A win count, a deal count or a
striking transcript read alone would have misdescribed all four systems.

G11: a false completion claim, a correction, and two refusals
Late in game 11 only Grok and Sol remain, and Grok holds a developed dark-blue group. Sol

Virginia completes your pinks. Pennsylvania completes my greens, but comes mortgaged: add $150 to

Grok holds one pink deed and Sol holds the other two, so Virginia alone does not complete the

No. Virginia doesn't complete the pinks — you still have St. Charles. Pennsylvania completes your

You're right—I missed St. Charles. Both pinks for mortgaged Pennsylvania plus $250: you get the

No. Pennsylvania completes your greens. I'm not handing you that set, with or without the pinks.

Record and replay.
The engine, the orchestration harness, the telemetry layer and the display are
separate components, and only the engine changes state. The earlier audit reports the following,
which this draft inherits. Reductions of the event logs reproduce cash, ownership, buildings and
bank stock in all 6,989 decision prompts and 10,235 saved snapshots. The 113 settlements reconcile
with the accepted terms, including $431 of mortgage-transfer interest in thirty settlements. The
applied action streams replay to the same 50,918 events in all twenty games after a documented
canonicalization. A portable suite re-executes 60 elimination cases and 66 default cases from their
exact prefixes with an unchanged copy of the engine. That suite refuses to certify impossibility by
graph search whenever a visited state offers a building sale, because a reviewer found that the engine
accepts bundled sales that a one-building-at-a-time search misses. The one graph-based negative,
game 7, has only a mortgage and a declaration on its menu. Code and the twenty saved games are
public at https://github.com/KushagraBharti/MonopolyBench. This draft and its scripts are local research
outputs.
Prompt and memory conditions.
The trade-history formatter error has three visible forms that
come from one assumption: 44 of 113 accepted-trade summaries with the direction reversed, 26 of
413 rejections attributed to the wrong party, and 232 of 394 counters described with the player as
its own partner. In 1,108 of Opus’s 1,696 decisions its private-memory window is empty, and in 286
more it holds only the engine’s synthetic fallback notes. One recorded response explicitly notices a
reversed summary and reasons from the correct holdings, which shows that reliance on the garbled
line is not inevitable and nothing more.
