Bargaining, Building and Bankruptcy:
An Observational Study of LLM Agents in Twenty Complete
Monopoly Games
MonopolyBench project
Independent full draft by the Opus research-assistance run
10 November 2026
Abstract
Language-model agents increasingly negotiate and transact with one another, yet evaluations
usually score a final outcome or an isolated decision. We study twenty complete four-player
Monopoly games played by four configured LLM systems, linking every offer, construction attempt,
financing route and bankruptcy to the engine’s legal menus and recorded events. Recipients resist
completing a rival’s color group. Offers that would complete the offer author’s group are accepted
19 times in 289 and rejected 159 times, although the median such offer pays the recipient $200.
Counters to these offers rarely remove the completing deed: 92 of 111 keep it, and nearly all that
give the counterer nothing ask for more cash. Three configurations accept such offers less often than
offers completing their own group; the fourth, which ran mostly under a refusal-recovery condition,
rarely rejects but prices the completion in its counters. Completion becomes construction in 43 of 63
trade-completed groups, and in one configuration most of the shortfall comes from engine defaults
on builds that were executable after legal reordering. Nominal wealth misstates liquidity in both
directions: three eliminated players could legally have paid the debt that ended their game, while
one with $1,310 of nominal assets could reach $440. Creditor inheritance sends 54 of 60 estates to
the eventual winner, and the most consequential bargaining conduct in the selected transcripts
occurs where solvency and inheritance are at stake. The study is observational and estimates no
causal effects, optimal play or model ranking.

Introduction
Language-model agents now buy, sell, price and negotiate on behalf of people and firms, and a growing
number of studies have them do so with each other [6, 19, 20]. Economic competence in such settings
has several links. An agent must price an exchange, carry out the investment the exchange was meant
to enable, and keep enough accessible money for later obligations; when it fails, its assets pass to
someone else. An evaluation that scores one link, such as a win, a settled deal or a cash reserve, can
describe the agent wrongly. A rejected trade may be good bargaining or a missed opportunity, an
unbuilt property may be prudence or an action the agent could not express, and a bankruptcy may be
unavoidable or a payable debt.
Multiplayer Monopoly ties these links together under simple rules. A complete color group
multiplies rent and permits construction, so a bilateral trade that completes one player’s group changes
the risk faced by everyone at the table. Construction turns cash into income that depends on opponents’
movement. Mortgages and building sales decide whether a debt can be paid, and under the rules

We study twenty complete MonopolyBench games played by four configured systems reached

response attempt, the applied action and the resulting events. We can therefore tie bargaining text
to the terms on offer, a construction request to whether it could execute, and an elimination to the
financing routes that existed at that moment. The study is observational.
Several run conditions shape what the records can show, and we state them before any result
(Section 2.3). The provider refused an Opus response attempt in 1,605 of its 1,696 decisions, and
the recovery path removed the private-note field and used up the corrective retry, so Opus played
most of the cohort under a different response condition from the others. A trade-history formatter
misdescribed transactions in 2,054 of 6,989 prompts. We therefore describe configured systems, not
base models.
One thread in game 2 shows the chain the paper follows. At turn 45 Sol holds Marvin Gardens, the
deed Opus needs to complete its yellows. Sol answers a $325 offer with $650, writing that $325 does
not compensate for “handing you that monopoly”, and later refuses Opus’s “final” $450. Twenty-four
turns later the direction reverses: Opus sells Sol the two yellows it lacks for two railroads and $100. Sol
builds on the yellows, collects no rent on them, and is eliminated by Park Place rent owed to Gemini,
which inherits the yellow group intact and wins the game (Incident 1).
Across the cohort we find four connected results.
1. Recipients refuse or price a rival’s completion. Offers that would complete the offer author’s color
group are accepted 19 times in 289 and rejected 159 times, against 24 acceptances and 38 rejections
among 140 offers completing the recipient’s own group. Counters to rival-completing offers keep
the completing deed in 92 of 111 cases and almost always ask more for it. Three configurations
show the acceptance gradient; Opus, which rarely rejects, prices the completion in its counters
instead (Section 4).
2. Completion becomes construction only when a build can be executed. First-window construction
follows 43 of 63 trade-completed groups. Opus’s shortfall comes largely from engine defaults after
refusal recovery, and all 61 of its defaulted purchase multisets were executable after reordering
(Section 5).
3. Nominal wealth is not accessible liquidity. Three eliminated players could legally have paid their
debt, in each case only through building sales that the displayed mortgage options did not reveal.
One player held $1,310 of nominal assets but could reach only $440 (Section 6).
4. Inheritance closes the chain, and conduct concentrates near it. The eventual winner receives 54
of 60 estates, 35 of 40 before the final elimination. In the selected transcripts, an omitted financing
route, a standing offer withdrawn from a nearly bankrupt seller and an appeal to deny a rival
building cash all occur where solvency or inheritance is at stake (Section 7).
For evaluating economic agents, the results argue against treating agreement rates as a quality
measure in multi-party markets, against separating an agent from its action interface and retry policy,
and for measuring liquidity as legally reachable money (Section 10). The paper does not estimate
causal effects, show that any choice was optimal, or rank the underlying models. Section 10.2 maps the
eight research questions and three broader questions that motivated the project to the evidence this
cohort supports, and Section 11 collects the limits.

2
Environment, cohort and conditions
2.1
The game as a small economy
MonopolyBench is a deterministic four-player Monopoly implementation on the United States board,
with $1,500 starting cash, $200 for passing Go, and 32 houses and 12 hotels in the bank. Declining a
purchase opens an ascending auction. In a negotiation thread one player proposes a bundle of cash,
deeds and jail cards, and the recipient accepts, rejects or counters, for at most twenty exchanges; deeds
in a group with buildings cannot be traded. The engine alone decides legality: at each decision it emits
a menu of legal actions, validates the selected action and records the resulting events.
Five rule choices matter for the economic analysis.
1. Mortgaged deeds can be traded, and the recipient pays ten percent of the mortgage value as transfer
interest.
2. Construction checks even building after each entry of a build plan. A count-valued request such as
“three houses on each pink” can therefore be invalid as written even though the same purchases
are legal in another order.
3. A player who owes more than its cash enters liquidation, where it can sell buildings, mortgage
deeds or declare bankruptcy, but not trade. Declaration is allowed even when a legal sequence
could pay. Mortgaging a deed in a developed group requires first removing that group’s buildings,
and downgrading a hotel requires houses in the bank.
4. A bankrupt player’s deeds and buildings pass intact to a player creditor. Deeds owed to the bank
return to it and are not auctioned at once.
5. Redemption uses a floating-point ceiling that overcharges by one dollar for some principals, for
example $111 rather than $110 on a $100 mortgage. The applied records include this behavior.
Appendix A lists the remaining settings. Results about financing and inheritance are results about this
implementation.
Agents see public state, including every opponent’s cash, holdings and configured model name, but
not opponents’ private notes or future dice and cards. Memory holds twenty public timeline entries,
the agent’s ten most recent private notes, and negotiation history inside a trade thread.
The standard system prompt asks each agent to “win the game by maximizing long-term advantage
and being the last non-bankrupt player” and allows “any legal strategy or personality”. It states:
“Deception in public chat is allowed; private thoughts should reflect true reasoning.” A false public
statement is therefore an authorized move, not a violation of the task. The private-honesty request
applies only when the standard prompt is used (Section 2.3).

3
Measurement
3.1
Units
An attempt is one provider response; a decision is one engine menu resolved into one applied action. A
negotiation thread runs from a proposal to its final acceptance or rejection, and each received offer is
a response opportunity. The offer author is the player who wrote the terms currently on the table; after
a counter it is the former recipient, so it is often not the thread’s initiator. For each offer we record
whether acceptance would give the author, the recipient, both or neither a newly complete color group,
computed from the offered bundles (railroads and utilities excluded, mortgaged deeds counted).
These are the four completion cells. For a counter we compare the counteroffer with the offer it answers:
whether it keeps the original author’s completion, whether it gives the counterer one, and how the
counterer’s net cash changes.
A trade-completed group is a color group completed by a settled trade. Its first management window
begins at the owner’s first optional-management decision after settlement and runs to the end of that
global turn. A spending unit joins a game, a player and a color group with any own construction
spending. An elimination is one bankruptcy, declared or automatic, and its estate is the cash and deeds
that pass to the creditor. We cite decisions as Gn dk, with k zero-based within game n.
3.2
Ledgers and checks
The economic results rest on event-derived ledgers retained with the baseline manuscript: offers
(920 rows), threads (526), typed transfer flows that keep trade cash, traded deeds, paid rent, es-
tate cash and estate deeds separate, first-management windows, spending units, eliminations and a
construction-resolution census. Earlier audits showed that the reconstructions match the cash, own-
ership, development and bank stock in all 6,989 initial decision prompts and 10,235 state snapshots,
that all twenty action streams replay through the engine to the same 50,918 events after documented
canonicalization, and that a separately written implementation reproduces all 920 offer rows and all
113 accepted-trade transitions.
For this draft we re-tabulated the ledgers with a short script (tools/retabulate.py), which repro-
duces the completion table, the estate counts and the elimination partition and computes the new
quantities: counteroffer content, thread outcomes, configuration and seed-block breakdowns, and
cash medians. We also checked ten new quotations against the canonical decision files and found
each quoted message in the recipient’s next prompt. We did not re-run the engine or re-execute the
legal-sequence certificates. Appendix H separates checked from inherited results.
3.3
Legal-sequence tests
Claims about what a player could have done are stated for a restored historical prefix, an acting player,
a class of permitted actions and an endpoint, such as “pay the current debt using only the debtor’s
own sales and mortgages.” A witness is a legal action sequence that the unchanged engine executes to
the endpoint. It proves feasibility and nothing about optimality, survival or what any player would do
next. An upper bound rules feasibility out. For a debtor with cash C owing D,

3.4
Text, channels and coding
The records hold four text channels. The public message accompanies the applied action and can reach
other players. The applied private note is an instrumented field the agent writes with its action and sees
again in later memory. A returned provider explanation is reasoning text that some providers return, and
assistant content is free text that may accompany a tool call. None is direct access to the computation
behind an action [7, 17, 31], so we report what each says and where they conflict.
Mechanical facts come from the ledgers. Two semantic results come from LLM-assisted coding. Two
readers, both instances of the GPT-6 Astra research-assistance model, coded the stated reasons in the
74 direct rejections of offers that would complete the recipient’s group; the second had seen aggregate
counts and one row’s labels beforehand. One such reader coded a fixed sample of 240 decisions for
plans and forecasts (Section 8). No human annotation study was conducted, and agreement between
model readers is not evidence of validity. Earlier project phases also used GPT-6.1 Sol, Claude Opus 5.5
and Claude Fable 5.1 research-assistance runs, two of which share families with evaluated participants,
and this draft was written by a Claude Opus 5.5 run.
The incidents in Sections 4–7 are a purposive selection for distinct mechanisms, available recipient
responses and checkable consequences. They support no prevalence estimate. Each gives the setup,
verbatim excerpts labelled with speaker, channel, game, decision and selected attempt, the observed
outcome, and our reading.
4
Bargaining over color-group completion
4.1
The negotiation population
The twenty games contain 526 negotiation threads and 920 response opportunities. Recipients accept
113 offers, counter 394 and reject 413. In two cases the engine does not offer acceptance, because
accepting would leave a party with negative cash after transfer interest; excluding them leaves 918
acceptance-legal offers for comparisons that require a real choice to accept. Threads are short. The
median thread has one response, the longest has eleven counters, and none spans a new global turn.
Players return to the same partners often: 414 of 526 threads follow an earlier thread between the same
pair in the same game.
The 113 settlements complete a color group for neither party in 60 cases, for one party in 43 and
for both in 10, giving the 63 trade-completed groups followed in Section 5. In 44 settlements the final
terms were written by the player who did not start the thread, so assigning settled bundles to the
initiator would misdescribe who proposed what.

4.2
Rival completion is refused or priced
Offers that would complete only the author’s group fare worst (Figure 1). Recipients accept 19 of 289
(6.6%) and reject 159 (55%), although the median such offer pays the recipient $200 net. Offers that
would complete only the recipient’s group are accepted 24 times in 140 (17%) and rejected 38 times
(27%), although the median one asks the recipient to pay $350. Offers completing neither party fall
between (60 accepted and 179 rejected of 408), and offers completing both are accepted 10 times in 81.
Within the author-only cell, cash barely separates outcomes: the median payment to the recipient is
$200 for accepted offers, $230 for countered offers and $180 for rejected ones.
What recipients do when they counter says more than how often they reject. Table 3 compares
each counter with the offer it answers. Of 111 counters to author-only offers, 92 keep the author’s
completion. In 88 of these the counterer gains no group of its own, and 85 of the 88 ask for more
cash, with a median increase of $125. Thirteen counters replace the deal with one that completes the
counterer’s group instead and pay for it. Only six strike the completion and give neither side a group.
Counters in every cell usually ask for more cash, including 135 of 164 counters that involve no
completion, so the premium itself is ordinary bargaining. What is specific is that the completing deed
stays on the table: recipients who keep negotiating over a rival’s completion almost always negotiate
its price rather than its existence. Thread outcomes point the same way. Of 187 threads whose opening
offer would complete only the initiator’s group, 26 settle, and in 24 of these the initiator still completes
its group after raising its cash offer by a median $97.50. Threads that open with an offer completing
the recipient’s group settle 17 times in 53.
In this population a rival’s completion is something recipients refuse or charge for. This is the
observational counterpart of the first research question’s example: agents do turn down trades that
hand an opponent a dangerous advantage, including trades that pay them. The ledger cannot show
that any refusal was correct, that the premiums were efficient, or that rival completion caused the
responses. The offers differ in price, deeds, timing and position, and repeated threads make the
observations dependent.

4.4
Stated reasons and roles
All 74 direct rejections of offers that would complete the recipient’s own group have public text, and 66
have a private note. Two LLM readers agree that 61 state a trade-linked consequence for the rival, such
as completing its group or funding its houses (Section 3.4). Their four disagreements turn on whether

and two notes adopt the wrong buyer or seller role (G15 d106, G17 d186). The readings describe stated

Initiation also differs. Opus proposes on 160 of its 660 eligible own turns, Grok on 106 of 676, Sol

What happened. The turn-45 thread ends without a trade. At turn 69 Sol receives Atlantic and Ventnor
for the railroads and $100. Sol spends $300 on yellow houses, collects no rent on them and recovers
$150 in sales. At d239 it holds $77 against $1,300 of Park Place rent owed to Gemini and is eliminated.
Gemini inherits the yellow group, spends $900 more on it and wins the game.
What this shows. Both sides reason about the other’s building cash after the trade. Sol computes what
Opus would keep at $325 and at $650, and Opus argues from Sol’s thin cash; since cash is public, these
are capacity calculations rather than inferences about hidden beliefs. Opus’s “final” $450 sits below its
private $475 ceiling, an authorized bluff that ends the thread, so whether Sol would have sold at $475
is unknown. The sequence shows the whole chain studied here: a completion is first priced, then sold
in the other direction, built on without return, and passed through bankruptcy to the eventual winner.
It does not show that any party misjudged the trade.

6.2
Spending and receipts
The 103 spending units incur $103,450 of own construction spending and collect $93,000 in paid
developed rent, $92,550 of it after the owner’s first purchase. Forty collect none, and 26 of those also
trigger no elimination. These are realized flows, not returns: owners inherit buildings, rent depends on
opponents’ movement and survival, and unpaid terminal demands, salvage and estates are separate
flows. Fifty-seven of the 60 eliminations arise from rent on developed groups, so a unit with little
paid rent can still end an opponent’s game. Successive owners of the same group can have opposite
experiences. In G19 Opus spends $1,200 on the dark blues, receives $175 in developed rent and recovers
$600 in sales; Gemini inherits the deeds, spends $2,000 and receives $1,400. Appendix C plots all 103

6.3
Creditor inheritance concentrates assets
Under intact creditor inheritance, a bankrupt player’s development and deeds pass to whoever it owes.
The eventual winner is the estate recipient in 54 of 60 eliminations. Excluding the final elimination of
each game, which hands everything to the last player by construction, the winner still receives 35 of 40
estates, 202 of 227 inherited deeds and $7,148 of $7,843 in estate cash. The winner also collects $78,737
of the $121,154 paid in rent. One estate (G13, Luxury Tax) goes to the bank.
These counts condition on the eventual winner, so they describe how assets concentrated; they do
not estimate how much inheritance contributed to winning. Creditor identity, development, cash and
landings evolve together. The records do show a recurring mechanism that matters for bargaining. A
blocking or denial position lasts only as long as its holder stays solvent, and the blocker then passes to
the creditor. Grok’s G11 blocker came from Gemini’s estate. In G18 Grok buys Illinois to keep Sol off
the reds, and Illinois passes to Sol when Grok goes bankrupt to Sol eighteen turns later. In G13 the
yellows that Opus paid to keep away from Sol pass to Sol at d311 (Incident 4). In G2 the yellow group
Sol finally completed passes to Gemini. Players also argue explicitly about who would inherit, which
is the subject of the next section.
7
Distress, coordination and conduct
This section follows what players said when solvency, inheritance or a rival’s development was at stake.
The task allows public deception, so a false public statement is an authorized tactic and not by itself
evidence of misalignment with the instructions; the private-honesty request applies only under the
standard prompt. For each incident we separate whether a proposition is false or misleading under a
stated reading, whether another channel from the same speaker contradicts it, whether it reached the
recipient, what the recipient’s record shows, and what changed afterward. A recipient’s acceptance
does not show that it believed the speaker.
The offer ledger shows distress only crudely. Of 339 acceptance-legal offers in which the author
asks for net cash, 132 come from authors holding less than $100; these are accepted 18 times and
rejected 62 times, while the 51 from authors holding $700 or more are accepted 13 times and rejected 6
times. The threshold is arbitrary, cash on hand is not accessible finance (Section 6), and the offers sell
different things, so this is description only. The incidents show what the ledger cannot.

What the incidents add
The stakes players name are the ones the ledgers measure: completion (G2, G11, G20), liquidity (G13,

responses carry a content/tool name conflict; in 15 the arguments are identical and only the function
name differs, and four of these accept trades while the message argues for rejection. One, G18 d83,
accepts $130 for Baltic and completes Opus’s browns in a game Opus won. These conflicts constrain
which channel can be read as the agent’s plan; they do not measure deception. Appendix E gives the
remaining chronologies, a five-case proposition table, a bluff that settled at its advertised limit (G7)
and a private note delivered as public chat (G20 d1).
8
Plans, forecasts and repeated errors
Plans need an opportunity before they can fail.
A fixed sample of 240 decisions, three per player per
game drawn by hash rank from decisions not flagged in an earlier public-text extraction, was coded
by one LLM reader for forward-looking statements in public messages and applied notes. Fifty-three
sampled private fields are empty, all Opus’s. The reader found forward-looking own-action passages
in 59 records, statements about opponents’ capacity or likely outcomes in 36, and references to earlier
corrections in 25. Eight records state an explicit policy of buying named properties on landing. Six of
these never meet a later purchase opportunity for the named deed. One is executed: in G2 Grok buys
Mediterranean at its d256 opportunity. One fails at a real opportunity. At G16 d103 Grok writes “buy
Virginia on sight, then house pink”; at d159 it has $371, Virginia costs $160 and the menu offers the

purchase, and its note says “Buy at list is the only way to close pink”, yet the applied action starts an
auction, which Gemini wins at $380. Because the action contradicts its own note, this is not a deliberate
change of plan. Plans also succeed over many turns: at G9 d64 Sol targets three houses on each dark
blue as funds arrive and reaches that state at d163, thirty-one turns later. Appendix F gives these
chronologies.
Most forecasts concern public capacity.
The opponent statements in the sample, and those in the
incidents above, mostly compute what a rival can afford or would gain from a trade, using cash and
holdings that every player can see. Some are right: at G2 d161 a forecast that withdrawing from an
auction leaves New York to a named player is realized. Others have no test, as when Grok predicts at
G19 d220 that rivals will not pay it to survive and no offer arrives before its elimination. These are
financial deductions. The records contain no probability forecasts and no pre-registered targets, so
they cannot score calibration or inference about hidden beliefs.
Corrections are local.
Among 114 decisions with an uneven-building error, 54 are followed within
the same decision by a corrective request that contains the exact validator error. Fifty of these error
decisions are followed by another build submission from the same player in the same game, and nine
of those submissions repeat the error. Restricting to the first such episode per player-game leaves 29
later submissions and two repetitions. Opus’s 60 refusal-path error decisions never receive a corrective
request, and Opus accounts for 54 of the 63 repetitions in the full error population. With feedback this
unequal, the aggregate cannot show that one configuration learns less than another. Within-auction
ceilings are also revised: at G20 d40–46 Grok tightens its stated limit from $235 to $230, bids $232
while acknowledging the overrun, then withdraws. None of these sequences shows durable learning.
Testing that would need controlled feedback and comparable later opportunities.

Related work
Economic simulations and board games.
Vending-Bench measures long-horizon coherence in run-
ning a simulated vending business, and CEO-Bench asks agents to run a simulated startup for 500
days; both connect decisions to cash over long trajectories [3, 6]. The Vending-Bench Arena com-
munication study analyzes 2,583 emails among competing agents, checks extracted claims against
simulator records, and reports that misaligned messages rise under low-inventory conditions [19].
FinBoardBench evaluates financial reasoning in Cashflow, Acquire and Monopoly with a focal model
against fixed opponents, and reports that models favor immediate asset purchases over keeping enough
cash [12]. Its Monopoly rules return buildings to the bank on creditor transfer, whereas ours pass
them intact, which changes the stakes of a rival’s insolvency. AgentMonopoly studies behavioral
profiles, model size and persona instructions in the same game, records language and actions together,
and finds that persona instructions change wording and some actions, though not consistently across
decisions [33]. Its inspected trade interface accepts or rejects a single offer and its prompt forbids public
messages that contradict the chosen action, so our counteroffer analysis and our authorized-deception
contract have no direct counterpart there. DECE evaluates complete model–harness configurations
across ten Eurogames and finds that the same model can perform differently across harnesses [36],
which supports treating response policy as part of the evaluated system. Earlier Monopoly work
trained reinforcement-learning agents against fixed-policy opponents [5].
Negotiation and bargaining.
Learned negotiation dialogue agents were studied before large language
models [18], and CICERO combined a language model with strategic planning to play Diplomacy at
23

=== PAGE 24 ===
a human level [22]. NegotiationArena varies behavioral instructions in bilateral games and shows
that some tactics change negotiation outcomes [4]. LLM-Stakeholders defines scorable multi-party,
multi-issue negotiations [1]. Cattle Trade studies bluffing, bidding and bargaining among several
LLM agents [23]. Two recent bilateral studies are close to our bargaining results. One finds that agents
model a counterparty’s preferences accurately but that agreements follow opening anchors more than
true utility [8]. Another benchmarks supply-chain negotiation against a Bayesian equilibrium and
finds that the provider of each model predicts surplus division better than capability rank does [20].
Our setting differs in the respect that drives the main result: a bilateral Monopoly trade changes the
positions of two players outside it, and a completion strengthens its owner against everyone, a cost
that a two-party utility does not capture.
Strategic reasoning benchmarks.
GTBench and γ-Bench evaluate LLMs on game-theoretic tasks and
multi-agent games with defined scoring [9, 13], and SPIN-Bench combines planning, board games and
negotiation [35]. They offer cleaner strategic targets than a complete Monopoly game, which instead
lets bargaining, investment, liquidity and insolvency interact.
Deception, commitments and collusion.
Park et al. survey deceptive behavior in AI systems [24],
and Scheurer et al. show a model deceiving its overseer under business pressure in a simulated trading
task [26]. MASK separates honesty from accuracy [25]. Studies of public promises and repeated
games examine when agents break commitments or lie [27, 28]; Empty Commitments separates
promises a runtime cannot fulfill from feasible promises not acted on [30]; and work on standing over
commitments asks who may release a promise in multi-party chat [32]. LLM pricing agents can reach
supracompetitive prices [10], and collusion can emerge in long-horizon cooperative tasks [29]. Our
task authorizes public deception and assigns no standing-offer norm, so our incidents describe conduct
and recipient responses rather than violations. Work on explanation faithfulness and monitorability
explains why we treat private notes and provider explanations as fallible channels [7, 16, 17, 31].
Evaluating complete systems.
Agent evaluation increasingly treats the model, scaffold and policy as
one system. AgentBoard argues for process-level measurement [21]; “AI Agents That Matter” and the
Holistic Agent Leaderboard emphasize cost, reproducibility and harness effects [14, 15]. Hardy et al.
find that rankings of fixed model–scaffold systems are far more reliable than rankings of underlying
models [11], consistent with our decision to describe configured systems. Yang et al. audit leaderboard
contamination claims with matched controls and scorer validation and decline conclusions their
evidence cannot support [34]. Agarwal et al. show how few-run evaluations can mislead without
attention to variability [2]. We do not claim novelty for replay, failure logging, state-grounded claim
checking or counterfactual restoration, all of which appear in the work above. What this paper adds
is an account of how one cohort of configured agents bargained over rival completion, converted
completion into construction, financed or failed to finance debts, and passed assets on in bankruptcy,
with each link checked against the legal options and the information actually delivered.
10
Discussion
10.1
What the chain implies for evaluating economic agents
Agreement rate is a poor quality measure in a multi-party market.
A bilateral exchange that
completes one player’s group raises the risk for every other player, including the recipient. In these
games recipients mostly refused such trades or asked for more, and they did so even when the offer
24

=== PAGE 25 ===
paid them. Low acceptance in this population therefore reflects, at least in part, recipients pricing a
rival’s gain. It does not show that the agents lack a bias toward agreement, because low acceptance can
coexist with generous accepted terms, and the cohort has no valuation against which to call a price
generous. An evaluation of negotiating agents should report offers by whose position they change and
should read counteroffer content, not only accept and reject counts.
Execution is part of economic competence, and the interface helps decide it.
The defaulted Opus
builds were affordable and requested, yet the game recorded no development. For a deployed agent
the same gap appears whenever a planned allocation must pass through an action language with
ordering constraints and a limited retry budget. The relevant measurement separates three things:
whether the requested allocation was reachable, whether its encoding was valid, and whether it was
executed. Construction counts that merge them measure the interface as much as the agent.
Liquidity is what the rules let you reach.
Displayed options understated finance in all three payable
eliminations, and nominal equity overstated it in the G7 lock. An agent or analyst that reasons from
either will misjudge solvency. Checking a debt against legally reachable sales and mortgages is a
modest computation in this game, and it changed the description of four eliminations.
Terminal transfers belong to the market outcome.
With intact creditor inheritance, a rival’s insol-
vency is a strategic event. It decides who receives the estate, and players argued about this explicitly
(G13). Denial purchases held only as long as their holders stayed solvent. Evaluations that score each
agent’s survival separately miss where its assets went, and in this cohort they went to the eventual
winner in 54 of 60 cases.
Conduct audits can start at pressure points.
The selected incidents with the largest stakes occur at
solvency and inheritance decisions, and the Arena study reports more misaligned messages under low
inventory [19]. Both observations come from selected or coded material, so they do not establish a
rate. They do suggest where a conduct review of an economic agent should look first: decisions where
a party is near insolvency, where a deed would complete a rival’s set, or where an estate is about to
change hands.
10.2
The research questions
The project began with eight questions about multi-agent behavior and three broader ones. Table 8
states what this cohort supports for each and what remains open. Several questions need controlled
designs, such as varied opponents, randomized messages or feedback, held-out tasks and independent
capability measures, that this observational study does not have. We report that as the state of the
evidence.
Two of these answers deserve a sentence more. For question A, the cohort gives no general verdict
on whether these systems are “genuinely strategic”. It shows that a complete game contains both kinds
of record. In G11 and G2 the stated reason, the applied action and the later game state cohere, while in
G12 and G19 the applied action contradicts every text channel in the same response. An evaluation
that reads only text, or only actions, would misdescribe some of these decisions. For question C, the
seed-block pattern shows why win totals from this design say little about skill. Shared seeds do not
preserve equivalent opportunities once actions change how random draws are consumed, and each
configuration always faced the same three opponents.

Limitations
Design.
Twenty games from five seed blocks are one observational cohort of four configured systems.
Cyclic rotations balance seats but cover four of the 24 seat orders, every configuration faces the same
three opponents, and shared seeds stop matching opportunities once actions diverge. Decisions and
offers within a game are dependent, so their counts are not independent trials. We report finite-cohort
counts and do not attach confidence intervals that would imply a sampling design the cohort lacks.
Nothing here ranks the underlying models.
Conditions.
The Opus refusal condition, Grok’s required tool choice, the trade-history formatter,
message delivery gaps and unequal text channels are part of what was evaluated. They limit compar-
isons across configurations, and their effects cannot be estimated from these games. The cause of the
refusal shift between games 1–4 and 5–20 is unresolved. The origin of Grok’s content/tool conflicts is
localized to the received response but not further.
Rules.
Results about financing and inheritance hold for this implementation: intact creditor in-
heritance, per-entry even-building checks, finite stock, declaration permitted while payable, and a
one-dollar redemption overcharge at some principals. Engine-grounded facts are facts about this
ruleset.
Measurement.
Mechanical ledgers reconcile with the records, but the completion cells are ownership
facts, not values, and the counter analysis describes terms, not intentions. Legal-sequence witnesses
prove feasibility of a stated endpoint and nothing about optimality, survival or what any player would
have done next. Negative search is certified only where no building sale is offered. Semantic results
(the 74 rejection reasons and the 240-record temporal coding) come from LLM readers from one model
family, with a partly unblinded second reader and no human annotation. Several definitions were
refined during exploration and were not preregistered.
Selection and authorship.
The incidents are purposive and several involve Grok, whose text channels
make conflicts visible. They support no prevalence estimate or moral ranking. LLM research-assistance
runs from model families that overlap with evaluated participants performed earlier extraction and
review, and this draft was written by an Opus-family run. Other drafts written in parallel will check
this one.
Scope.
The paper does not establish causal persuasion, intent, durable learning, opponent-model
accuracy, transfer to unfamiliar opponents or games, optimal play, the relation between general and
multi-agent capability, or a decomposition of skill and luck. An unpayable debt at elimination does not
justify or condemn the choices that led to it, and concentration of estates in winners does not measure
an effect of inheritance on winning.
12
Conclusion
In twenty complete Monopoly games among four configured LLM systems, the economic decisions
that matter are linked. Recipients refuse or charge for trades that would complete a rival’s group,
sometimes turning down their own completion to deny it. The groups that trades do complete
27

=== PAGE 28 ===
become development only when the owner can execute a build, and one configuration lost most of
its development to defaulted plans that were affordable as requested. Whether a debt ends a game
depends on which assets the rules let a player reach, which differs from both the displayed options and
nominal wealth. When a player fails, its assets pass intact to its creditor, usually the eventual winner,
and the sharpest bargaining conduct in the selected transcripts appears where that transfer is near.
For evaluating economic agents, the practical lesson is to follow the chain. Score offers by whose
position they change, separate requested from executed actions, measure liquidity as legally reachable
money, and record where a failing agent’s assets go. The questions this cohort cannot answer, about
causal influence, learning, transfer and optimal play, need designs built for them.

luxury taxes are $200 and $100; a player in jail may try three times for doubles before a forced fine; and

computes 1.1 times the principal with a floating-point ceiling, which exceeds the exact charge by one

The twenty baseline games applied 19,494 decisions. Nineteen reached the 1,000-decision obser-
vation limit and were treated as censored, with no winner assigned; censoring at that horizon is not
evidence that the games would never end. No policy initiated a trade, and the games contain only

the static scenarios, 75 of 780 policy–scenario choices and 11 of 130 supplied reference actions failed
the engine’s legality check. Fifty-five scenarios failed at least one static consistency screen, mostly
through house-stock discrepancies, and 68 passed both the reference-legality and static-invariant
screens. Scoring rubrics in 128 of 130 scenarios award points for keywords in the private note, and all
six policies choose identical actions in 41 scenarios.
These policies and scenarios are useful infrastructure, but they are not competent negotiating or
developing opponents, and passing a legality screen does not certify a scenario as a capability probe.
Any comparison of the LLM cohort against reference behavior would first need adjudicated tasks and
opponents that trade and build. The pilot games are a separate population and are never pooled with

Provenance: what this draft checked and what it inherits
This manuscript was written from retained project artifacts. No game, model query, engine branch,
reference-policy run or synthetic test was executed for it. Table 23 separates checks performed while
writing it from results carried over from earlier audits. “Inherited” means the result was produced
and reviewed in an earlier phase and is reported here with that phase’s scope; it was not re-derived.
Research-assistance runs.
Earlier extraction, review and drafting used four LLM research-assistance
runs: GPT-6 Astra, GPT-6.1 Sol, Claude Opus 5.5 and Claude Fable 5.1. The Sol and Opus runs share
model families with evaluated participants, and review was not blinded to this. The later agenda
extension and its checks used Astra instances. This draft was written by a Claude Opus 5.5 run working
independently of the parallel drafts. Agreement among these runs is not human annotation and is not
evidence of label validity.
Quotations.
Shared quotations come from the baseline manifest, which records each span’s source
file, JSON pointer, line, code-point offsets and hashes. Quotations added here are generated by
tools/build_opus_quotes.py, which reads the canonical files, checks that each excerpt is a contiguous
substring of the applied field, and writes the macros and data/opus_quote_manifest.json. Bracketed
ellipses mark omitted text, and quoted text is not corrected.
Literature.
Version numbers and venues of works added in this draft were checked on arXiv abstract
pages, the ACL Anthology, or proceedings listings on 10 November 2026, as recorded in each bibli-
ography note. For several recent preprints we read only the abstract page, and for CICERO and the
AI-deception survey we relied on bibliographic records found by search; we cite these works only for
claims their abstracts state.
Build.
From this folder, run python3tools/retabulate.py, python3tools/build_tables.py and python3t
ools/build_opus_quotes.py, then latexmk-lualatexmanuscript.tex. The figures and shared quotation
macros are read from the shared ../../assets/ and ../../sections/ folders.
