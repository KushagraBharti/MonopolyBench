MonopolyBench project

Abstract
An agent trading with competitors must consider who can develop the exchanged assets and
who may inherit them after bankruptcy. We examine this interdependence in twenty complete
four-player MonopolyBench games involving four configured LLM systems, with 6,989 decisions
and 920 negotiation responses. The systems complete 17, 18, 15 and 13 color groups through trade,
but build on 16, 17, 7 and 3 in their first management windows. Construction gaps mix deferred
financing, engine defaults and received actions conflicting with accompanying text. One participant
receives action-only refusal recovery on 1,604 of 1,696 decisions. Bargaining explanations discuss
rival development and creditor inheritance. Response distributions vary with completion, recipient
and negotiation stage; counters preserve and reprice exchanges both with and without completion.
At elimination, 56 of 60 current debts exceed a generous own-asset bound, three are payable by legal
sequences, and one is blocked by a hotel-liquidation constraint despite sufficient nominal assets.
Eventual winners receive 202 of 227 deeds transferred through estates before the final elimination.
Eight contextual incidents connect these populations to rival-sensitive refusals, authorized bluffing,
selective disclosure, financial-denial appeals and changed assurances, alongside factual correction
and contradictory execution. The resulting account follows bargaining through construction, distress
and creditor succession under documented response and information conditions. It does not identify
optimal strategy, causal persuasion, learning or an underlying-model ranking.

Introduction
A property that completes a rival’s monopoly is not an ordinary item in a bilateral exchange. Selling it
may finance the seller while giving the buyer a source of rent. Refusing may preserve a blocker while
leaving both parties exposed to a third player. If either later becomes insolvent, the deed can pass to a
creditor without another negotiation. An economic agent must therefore consider the exchange, the
development it enables and the positions that persist after it.
Monopoly makes these dependencies observable over a complete game. Players buy, auction and
trade properties, build houses, mortgage collateral, pay rents and liquidate assets. In the implemen-
tation studied here, a player-creditor inherits a bankrupt debtor’s properties with their buildings
intact. The recipient of an estate can consequently matter to players who were not parties to the debt.
This paper asks how LLM agents bargain over such dependencies, and what happens between an
acquisition, an investment request and a realized economic outcome.
