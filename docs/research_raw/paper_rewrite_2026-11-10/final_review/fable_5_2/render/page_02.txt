The results follow an economic sequence. First, agents negotiate over completion and development
capacity. Responses differ depending on whose group would complete, but the pattern varies across
recipients and negotiation stages. Counteroffers often preserve and reprice exchanges, both with
and without completion. Explicit explanations supply evidence that some participants consider rival
consequences; heterogeneous offer distributions alone cannot identify those preferences.
Second, ownership does not guarantee executed investment. Trade-completed groups encounter
different cash and mortgage conditions, and submitted construction plans can fail despite requesting
reachable allocations. The first-window record separates absent construction options, defaults, con-
flicting received calls and valid ends. This distinction changes the interpretation of an unbuilt group
without assigning a counterfactual value to the missed construction.
Third, liquidation and inheritance connect local choices to later positions. Most elimination debts
exceed even a generous financing bound. Three exceptions have payment sequences, while another
nominally wealthy position cannot liquidate enough because of the hotel rule. Eventual winners
receive 202 of 227 deeds transferred through estates before the final elimination. Selected exchanges
explicitly bargain over which rival might receive such assets.
Eight contextual incidents accompany these results. They include a refusal based on rival devel-
opment, a public bargaining limit contradicted by a private contingency, an omitted financing route,
an appeal to withhold a rival’s building cash, and withdrawal of a repeated offer after the seller’s
deterioration. Correction followed by acceptance and a contradictory bankruptcy response provide
contrasts. The task permitted public deception. These episodes can be economically and socially
consequential without constituting instruction violations, and a settlement alone does not establish
successful persuasion.
Prior work already studies sustained economic operation, state-grounded communication,
Monopoly behavior and complete model-plus-execution configurations [1, 5, 8, 13, 15]. Our contri-
bution is a connected observational account of bargaining, executable development, current-debt
finance and creditor succession. Historical feasibility checks support specific interpretations of that
record. They are not proposed as a generally validated auditing method. The paper places material
information and response conditions before the results, credits their consequences, and distinguishes
the broader research questions the corpus informs from those it cannot answer.

Environment, cohort and conditions
2.1
The implemented economy
Four players start with $1,500 on the forty-space United States Monopoly board. The bank has 32
houses and twelve hotels. A declined property purchase opens an ascending auction. Bilateral trade
threads allow proposals and counteroffers, up to twenty exchanges, before ordinary play resumes. All
reported money is game currency unless identified as provider USD.
The engine alone changes state, determines legality and emits events. Each decision presents one
player with explicit legal actions. Optional management may span multiple decisions in a turn. A
complete color group can be developed only when cash, mortgage status, stock and even-building
constraints permit it. Build plans are validated entry by entry, so buying three houses on one deed
before its siblings is invalid even if the requested final allocation is even.
