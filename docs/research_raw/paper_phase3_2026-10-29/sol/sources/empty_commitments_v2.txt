Abstract
A chatbot that says “I will remind you tomorrow” will not run again until
the user writes. We call such a promise an
empty commitment
: a promise
of an action after the current turn that nothing in the agent’s tools or
runtime can carry out. Unlike a broken promise, its emptiness is decided by
the agent’s configuration at the moment of speaking, so it can be detected
from a single turn, before deployment or at run time. We define empty
commitments on top of commitment semantics, with three failure types, an
anchoring condition for promises that a tool could make real, and an outcome
taxonomy that keeps the failures apart from honest deferrals and from
over-refusal, and we build a checker (a setup-blind detector, deterministic
feasibility rules and a response judge) validated against
400 human labels. On a controlled benchmark of
293 follow-up requests in five setups that add one
persistence affordance at a time, four open-weight models of 8–14B
parameters fail in 45.9% of responses when no tool
exists and nothing is stated; a frontier model fails in
4.4%, but it gets there by deferring and
asking, not by using the tools it has: promises made without the enabling
call remain in every model. Telling the model its runtime, the cheapest fix,
cuts the open-weight failures nearly in half where nothing is doable and
changes nothing where a scheduler exists; a directive capability card removes most failures
at the largest cost in over-refusal; running the checker in the loop and
rewriting flagged replies removes more at a smaller cost. Code, prompts,
model outputs and human labels are released.

Introduction
Users ask agents for things that outlive the conversation:
remind me
tomorrow
,
tell me when the build finishes
,
have someone call me
back
,
remember this next time
. Whether the agent can do any of these
depends on its deployment: whether it has a scheduler, a memory store or a
ticketing tool, and whether it ever runs again without a user message. LLM
agents now reason and call tools in a loop
(
Yao et al., 2023
;
Schick et al., 2023
;
Shinn et al., 2023
)
and manage their
own memory
(
Park et al., 2023
;
Packer et al., 2023
;
Shen and Li, 2026
;
Zhong et al., 2024
)
,
but the model is rarely told what persists after the turn ends. Deployments
pass tool schemas; they almost never say whether the agent will run
again.
A promise presupposes that the speaker can perform the act
(
Searle, 1969
;
Searle and Vanderveken, 1985
)
. When a tool-less agent answers
“Sure, I’ll remind you at 9 am”, that condition fails at the moment of
speaking: the agent will not run at 9 am. The user leaves believing something
has been arranged, and nothing has. We call this an
empty commitment
.
A close cousin is the
false action claim
: “Done! Reminder set for
9 am”, with no reminder anywhere. A third case appears once the right tool
exists: the agent promises the reminder but never calls the tool, so a
feasible promise is left
unanchored
. All three leave the user with a
false belief about the future, and all three can be read off a single turn
and the deployment’s configuration: whether the promise can be kept is
decided when it is made.
Recent work studies what agents do
after
they commit: they accept a
process instruction and break it in the same turn
(
Shin, 2026
)
,
report work they did not finish
(
Smyth et al., 2026
;
Kraishan and Jitkajornwanich, 2026
)
, fail to act on delayed
intentions
(
Liu and Gabriel, 2026
;
Zhang et al., 2026
)
, or break promises
when it pays
(
Shi et al., 2026
)
. In all of these the promised act was
possible, and finding the gap needs the later trajectory. We ask the prior
question:
was the promise possible at all?
The answer depends only
on the agent’s configuration and the current turn, so it needs no
long-horizon evaluation, can be checked before deployment, and can be caught
at the moment of speaking. We build that checker, validate it against human
labels, and use it to measure how models behave across deployments and how
far three fixes of increasing cost go.

•

Turns and future-directed commissives.
A

Capability statements (“I can set reminders”), suggestions to the user

other systems, plan narration within the turn (“let me check that” followed
by a tool call), and phatic closings (“feel free to ask anytime”) are not
commitments. Text the agent writes on the user’s behalf, such as a drafted
email, carries no commitment of the agent’s own.

Judge model.
One fixed judge model, GPT-6.1 Sol through the OpenAI API, labels every
tested model in every stage. It is from a family outside the four open-weight
models, so any self-preference affects their rows equally; the one exception
is the frontier run, where the judge labels its own replies, which is why
those numbers are kept apart and marked. The API accepts neither a
temperature of 0 nor a no-reasoning setting, so the judge runs at its lowest
reasoning effort; on repeated calls it returned identical outputs and used no
reasoning tokens, but determinism is not guaranteed. All judge outputs are
cached, so every analysis is reproducible from the cache without new calls.
Every judgment record carries the hash of the prompt version that produced
it.
Validation against human labels.
Two annotators labelled 300 main-run responses
(400 labels; 100 responses labelled by
both), stratified by setup and category, half from responses where the
detector found a commitment or the judge a false claim and half from the
rest. They marked commitment spans, feasibility, failure types and
anchoring, the five booleans and the outcome, without seeing the pipeline’s
output. §

Statistics.
Confidence intervals are 95% cluster bootstrap intervals over requests

setups and arms never count as independent. Differences between
arms (stated vs. inferred, a fix vs. its baseline) use the paired
bootstrap: requests are resampled once and both arms are computed on the
same resample.

Three Fixes of Increasing Cost
An empty commitment is a mismatch between what the model believes about its
runtime and what the runtime is. A deployment can act on that mismatch at
three points, and we test one fix at each: tell the model its runtime
(prompt facts), instruct it how to act on that knowledge (prompt
directives), or check its reply before it reaches the user (a guard that
uses the checker of §
3.4
). The three fixes rise in cost and
in what they require, and they differ in what they can fix by design
(Table
4
). Each is applied to the inferred baseline of the
same model and setup, so every effect is paired.

A directive block in the system prompt that ties every later action to the

the model, so it works for any model and any prompt, and it is the only one
that targets unanchored promises directly. Its cost is one checker pass per
reply and a rewrite for the flagged ones; its ceiling is the checker’s
precision, which §

Failure Rates in the Naive Deployment
In the naive deployment (C0, inferred), 45.9%
[41.7, 50.1] of responses fail
(Figure

empty commitment and 8.7% claim an action
that never happened (“Done! I’ve set a reminder”). Every model fails often,
from 35.5% (Qwen3.5) to
59.7% (Ministral 3; Table

). Most
other responses are transparent deferrals (41.2%):
the models know how to decline; they just often do not. Tools that give no
persistence barely help: the failure rate in C1 is
32.8%. Among empty commitments, temporal failures
(“I’ll remind you tomorrow”) and capability failures (“I’ll remember that
next time”, “I’ll email you”) dominate; agentive ones are rare under the
generic persona (Appendix Figure

).
Adding one affordance lowers the failure rate to
14.8% with a scheduler (C2),
28.8% with memory (C3) and
23.7% with tickets (C4). Memory is where false
claims are most frequent: 10.4% of C3
responses say a preference “has been saved” without a
memory_write
call. The support persona fails less than the generic one (C1:
18.3% vs. 32.8%;
Appendix Figure

The result does not depend on the templated requests. In C0-inferred (both
personas), requests mined from MASSIVE voice-assistant commands fail in

requests in 12.0%; templated requests, which
fill the memory and deferred-delivery categories that mined corpora lack
(§

), fail in 51.7%,
because those are the categories where models promise most (memory
requests fail in 82.0% of

naive-deployment response in four fails, and the effects reported below
hold on every source and every category: stating the environment and both
fixes lower the C0 failure rate on each of them, and detect-and-rewrite
does so in C2 as well (Appendix Table

In C2, 54.8% of responses back the request with

16.4% [11.0, 22.2] were

replies that mainly ask the user for what the call needs (“What time tomorrow would you like me
to remind you?”): the detector reads them as a promise with a time, but the

6
Related Work
Speech acts and commitments.
Felicity conditions make a promise defective when the speaker cannot perform
the act
(
Searle, 1969
;
Searle and Vanderveken, 1985
)
. Commitment has been
central to agent theory since its beginnings
(
Shoham, 1993
;
Wooldridge and Jennings, 1995
;
Rao and Georgeff, 1995
)
: first as an
agent’s persistence in its own intentions
(
Cohen and Levesque, 1990
;
Jennings, 1993
;
Castelfranchi, 1995
)
,
then as a social relation between agents, with a formal life cycle and a
separation of what an agent can commit itself to from what it can commit
others to
(
Singh, 1999
;
Singh, 2000
;
Yolum and Singh, 2002
;
Fornara and Colombetti, 2004
)
.
We apply these notions to an LLM agent whose abilities are fixed by its
deployment.
LLM agents and memory.
LLM agents interleave reasoning with tool calls
(
Yao et al., 2023
;
Schick et al., 2023
;
Shinn et al., 2023
)
and extend their
reach across sessions with managed memory
(
Park et al., 2023
;
Packer et al., 2023
;
Zhong et al., 2024
)
. Work on what
an agent should write into a carried state
(
Shen and Li, 2026
)
studies the
quality of the write; anchoring asks only whether a write happened. These
systems supply the affordances our setups vary; whether a model knows which
of them it has is what we measure.
Say–do gaps.
Agents agree to process instructions and then break them
(
Shin, 2026
)
, overclaim completed work
(
Smyth et al., 2026
)
, and write reports that diverge from their
action logs
(
Kraishan and Jitkajornwanich, 2026
)
. In strategic settings, models break
public promises for gain
(
Shi et al., 2026
)
. These gaps open between a
feasible promise and later behavior; ours is fixed when the promise is made.
Prospective memory.
PM-Bench
(
Liu and Gabriel, 2026
)
and TriggerBench
(
Zhang et al., 2026
)
test whether an agent later acts on an intention. They presuppose an agent
that is still running when the trigger arrives; we ask whether it will be.
Tool hallucination.
Tool-augmented models invoke tools that do not exist or attempt unsolvable
tasks
(
Zhang et al., 2024
;
Xu et al., 2024
)
. Empty commitments are the
conversational counterpart: no call is made, but the reply relies on an
affordance the agent lacks.
7
Conclusion
An agent’s promise about the future is only as good as its runtime. Empty
commitments, false action claims and unanchored promises can all be read off
a single turn and the deployment’s configuration, so operators can measure
them before deployment and check for them at run time with the detector and
rules used here. Three things follow from our measurements. First, the
behavior is not a corner case: in the naive deployment close to half of all
responses to follow-up requests fail, in every open-weight model we tested;
a frontier model has largely learned to defer instead, so the behavior tracks
model quality, and the many deployments built on small models are the ones
exposed. Second, the
cheapest fix, telling the model what its runtime is, works only where nothing
is doable, by turning promises into honest deferrals; once a scheduler exists
it does not lower the failure rate, and it does not make the model call the
tool more often. Third, the stronger fixes trade differently: a directive card
removes most failures but makes models refuse requests they could serve,
while rewriting only the flagged replies removes more at a smaller cost but
depends on the detector. Neither makes a model call the tool it has for every
promise it makes, which is the failure that remains, at the frontier too,
once deployments gain the affordances their models are not told about. In 2023–24 chat logs of tool-less assistants the behavior was rare
(Appendix
G
), because few users asked for later action;
as assistants are asked to remind, monitor and follow up, the setting our
benchmark measures becomes the common one.
Limitations
The benchmark is single-turn and English, with mocked tools, and anchoring
checks that an enabling call exists rather than matching its arguments to
the promise; multi-turn settings, other languages and argument-level
anchoring are natural extensions of the same definition. The checker’s
failure-level precision bounds the absolute rates
(§
5.6
); differences between arms do not depend on it. The
frontier model is also the judge and runs at its API’s fixed temperature,
so its contrast with the open-weight models is a comparison across
deployments, not a controlled scaling study. The WildChat estimate
(Appendix
G
) is a historical upper bound whose recall is
unknown.

Sample.

Sheet.

Appendix G
Real-World Prevalence (WildChat)
The assistants behind the 2023–24 WildChat logs
(
Zhao et al., 2024
)
,
GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 behind a chat interface, had no tools. Any agent-initiated
commitment they made that needs a later time, an event, a later session, a
third party or an outside channel was therefore empty by construction, and any
claim of a completed persistent action false by construction.
Sample.
We drew 49,998 English, unredacted, non-toxic (user message,
reply) pairs, an equal number from each of 6 model versions.
Some WildChat users drove the model through an autonomous-agent framework that
executes the commands the model outputs (“write to file”, “google”,
“memory add”); those assistants did have tools, so we leave their
conversations out (630 sampled turns), which keeps
49,368.
Two-stage design.
Judging every turn would cost five times more than judging the turns that
could contain a promise at all. Every turn whose reply contains promise- or
claim-like wording (“I’ll”, “let you know”, “remind you”, “I’ve set”,
“saved”; 6,569 turns, 13.1%) is judged, and
of the remaining turns a random 1,994 are judged as well;
their hit rate stands in for all of the rest. The cue words only decide which
turns are judged in full; every label comes from the detector and the response
judge, with the no-tool (C0) setup card. Per-model rates combine both strata
and are pooled with weights proportional to each model’s share of the
893,101 tool-free turns; intervals bootstrap each stratum.
