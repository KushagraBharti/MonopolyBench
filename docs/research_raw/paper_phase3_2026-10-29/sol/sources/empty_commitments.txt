Abstract
A chatbot that says “I will remind you tomorrow” will not run again
until the user writes. We call such a promise an
empty commitment
: a
promise of an action after the current turn that nothing in the agent’s tools
or runtime can carry out. Unlike a broken promise, its emptiness follows from
the agent’s configuration alone; no later trajectory is needed. We define
empty commitments on top of commitment semantics, with three failure types,
an anchoring condition for promises that a tool could make real, and a
response-level outcome taxonomy. We then describe a measurement protocol:
follow-up requests run in five setups that add one persistence affordance at
a time, with the environment either left implicit or stated.
1
Introduction
Users ask agents for things that outlive the conversation:
remind me
tomorrow
,
tell me when the build finishes
,
have someone call me
back
,
remember this next time
. Whether the agent can do any of
these depends on its deployment: whether it has a scheduler, a memory store or
a ticketing tool, and whether it ever runs again without a user message.
LLM agents now reason and call tools in a loop
(
Yao et al., 2023
;
Schick et al., 2023
;
Shinn et al., 2023
)
and manage their own memory
(
Park et al., 2023
;
Packer et al., 2023
;
Shen and Li, 2026
;
Zhong et al., 2024
)
,
but the model is rarely told what persists after the turn ends.
A promise presupposes that the speaker can perform the act
(
Searle, 1969
;
Searle and Vanderveken, 1985
)
. When a tool-less agent
answers “Sure, I’ll remind you at 9 am”, that condition fails at the moment
of speaking.
A close cousin is the
false action claim
: “Done! Reminder set for
9 am”, with no reminder anywhere.
Prior work studies the later trajectory: whether agents break instructions
they just accepted
(
Shin, 2026
)
, overclaim finished work
(
Smyth et al., 2026
;
Kraishan and Jitkajornwanich, 2026
)
, forget delayed intentions
(
Liu and Gabriel, 2026
;
Zhang et al., 2026
)
, or break promises for gain
(
Shi et al., 2026
)
. In all of these the promised act was possible. We ask
the prior question:
was the promise possible at all?
Nothing the
agent does later can redeem an empty commitment, so it can be caught at the
moment of speaking.
We contribute (1) a definition of empty commitments, decidable from the
agent’s configuration and one turn; (2) an outcome taxonomy that separates
failures from desirable responses and from over-refusal; and (3) a protocol
for measuring them.

Setups.
Each of five setups (Table
3
) runs in two variants.
In
inferred
, the model sees a persona prompt and tool schemas, and
nothing about persistence, as in most deployments. In
stated
, a short
paragraph of facts about the runtime is added (for C0: “You run only when the
user sends a message …”), with no advice. Two personas (generic, customer support) give 20 conditions.
Requests.
Single-turn requests cover five categories: reminders, monitoring an external
state, callbacks and escalation, deferred delivery, and cross-session memory.
A doability matrix decides whether declining is
deferral
or
over_refusal
.
Labeling.
A setup-blind detector extracts future-directed commitments from each turn.
Deterministic rules
then decide feasibility, failure type and anchoring from the setup
configuration. A judge returns five booleans per response (e.g., false action claim,
declines, hands back), from which the outcome follows by the ordered rules.
Metrics.
The primary result is the outcome distribution per setup and variant, with
the
empty-commitment rate
(empty over all future-directed
commitments), the
unanchored rate
(among feasible commitments needing
an enabling call), and the
over-refusal rate
(among doable requests).
4
Open Questions
(Q1)
When persistence is not stated, how do responses distribute over
the outcomes, and which failure types dominate?
(Q2)
When the enabling tool exists, how often do models promise
without calling it?
(Q3)
Does stating the environment lower failures, and how much
over-refusal does it cause?
(Q4)
How common were they in public logs of tool-less agents
(
Zhao et al., 2024
)
?
5
Conclusion
An agent’s promise about the future is only as good as its runtime. We
defined empty commitments, false action claims and unanchored promises, all
decidable from one turn and the deployment’s configuration. The protocol is
single-turn with mocked tools, and its LLM-based labels need human
validation. A multi-model evaluation is under way.
