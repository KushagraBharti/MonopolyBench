Abstract
Interactive environments offer a practical way to evaluate large language
model (LLM) agents, and competitive games extend this evaluation to decisions
shaped by competing goals and other agents’ choices.
Yet existing evaluations confined to individual games or execution frameworks leave unclear
which competitive advantages persist across settings.
We introduce
DECE
(
D
iverse
E
urogame
C
ompetitive
E
nvironments), a unified arena spanning ten
Eurogames with shared interfaces and evaluation protocols.
These games combine lasting strategic commitments, competition over shared
resources, and multiple routes to victory.
Our primary study evaluates 12 model–harness configurations, spanning nine models and four execution harnesses, in 480 three-player matches across ten games. The main and supplementary studies comprise 1,032 completed matches in total.
These comparisons reveal broad cross-game
ranking agreement alongside game-specific strengths.
Matched studies show that the same model can perform differently across
execution harnesses, adding another source of variation to these profiles.
These performance advantages do not consistently translate into better token
or cost efficiency, making resource use an additional consideration when
comparing configurations.
Together, the findings support evaluating complete agent configurations by both
their competitive performance across games and the resources needed to achieve it.

Introduction
Large language model (LLM) agents increasingly act through tools in external
environments
(
Yao et al., 2023
)
. Interactive benchmarks evaluate their
decisions over extended trajectories
(
Liu et al., 2024
;
Zhou et al., 2024
)
.
In competition, opponents pursue their own objectives and change an agent’s
available opportunities.
An action’s value depends on both its future consequences and opponents’
choices. We ask:
How can competitive environments support reliable evaluation of
complete agent systems across diverse strategic demands?
Strategy board games make this question tractable. They combine explicit
rules, programmatically executable states and actions, and independently
verifiable outcomes
(
Gaina et al., 2020
;
Balla et al., 2024
)
.
Their interactions can be represented as
partially observable stochastic games (POSGs)
(
Hansen et al., 2004
)
.
Agents act on their observation histories; their actions determine state
transitions and outcomes.
We focus on
Eurogames
(German-style strategy board games), which combine
persistent investments, competition over shared opportunities, and multiple
routes to victory. In
Splendor
, development cards reduce later purchase
costs;
Azul
placements constrain future spatial scoring
(
Space Cowboys, n.d.
;
Next Move Games, n.d.
)
. These commitments shape both a player’s
future options and opponents’ opportunities.
Existing multi-game benchmarks and harness studies provide foundations for
competitive evaluation
(
Costarelli et al., 2024
;
Hua et al., 2026
)
.
Turning these games into a common benchmark presents three challenges.
Agents need consistent observation–action interfaces across diverse rules
and information structures, while retaining their tools and context management.
Analysis must distinguish recurring competitive strength from game-specific
advantages and account for execution settings and resource use.
Reliable comparisons require consistent rule enforcement, a common
participant roster
, seat-balanced protocols, and verifiable outcomes.
Matched scenarios and uncertainty estimates support comparisons of execution
settings.

Related Work
Games provide a natural setting for evaluating LLM agents because they combine explicit rules, sequential interaction, and verifiable outcomes. Recent benchmarks span both digital and tabletop environments: Orak, BALROG, and LMGame-Bench evaluate agents across video games
(
Park et al., 2026
;
Paglieri et al., 2025
;
Hu et al., 2026a
)
, while GameBench and MindGames study competitive and social reasoning in board, card, and strategy games
(
Costarelli et al., 2024
;
Wang et al., 2026

FinBoardBench studies financial decisions and PTCG-Bench examines
card play, harness ablations, self-evolution, and cost
(
Hu et al., 2026b
;
Hua et al., 2026
)
.
Complementary work such as TAG and From Code to Play provides executable tabletop environments or evaluates components used by search-based players
(
Gaina et al., 2020
;
Eberhardinger et al., 2024
)
, while LudoBench studies reasoning from fixed game states
(
Peper et al., 2026
)
.

3
DECE: Benchmark Design and Evaluation Protocol
3.1
Game suite and strategic diversity
DECE selects ten score-based Eurogames that share persistent strategic
commitments and competition over limited opportunities, while differing in how
current decisions shape future options. Table
2
groups them
by three dominant, overlapping demands: building future capacity, making
persistent spatial commitments, and coordinating multiple action, resource,
and scoring systems. These differences allow the suite to test whether
competitive advantages recur across related strategic settings or remain
specific to individual games. The games also vary in interaction and
information structure: players compete entirely through legal game actions,
without dialogue or negotiation, and decisions may involve public or private
information as well as sequential or simultaneous choices.
Figure
2
characterizes this diversity along six
rule-based dimensions: engine construction, spatial planning, action
sequencing, opponent coupling, hidden information, and scoring coordination.
Values 0/1/2 denote absent, limited, or prominent mechanisms.
These annotations organize descriptive cross-game comparisons
(Appendix
B
). The suite also spans substantially different interaction
lengths, with median whole-match decision counts ranging from 54 in
7 Wonders
to 473 in
Ark Nova
. These counts summarize interaction
length at each interface’s action granularity (Appendix
C
).

3.2
Unified game interface and participant abstraction
All games share an interface for setup, seat-specific observations, legal
actions, state transitions, and terminal rankings. The engine enforces rules
and scoring. Simultaneous choices use a frozen state and advance only after
all required seats submit actions. Public information, private state, and
unrevealed randomness remain separate. MCP and native adapters expose this
interface; persisted states and execution records support auditing, replay,
and resume
(Appendix
A.3
).
Each participant specifies a model snapshot, execution harness, prompts,
tools, memory scope, inference settings, and resource budget. The shared
interface permits different internal histories and orchestration strategies.
The primary panel contains 12 configurations spanning nine model identities
and four harnesses (Appendix
A.2
). Comparisons evaluate these
complete configurations.

Experimental Evaluation
4.1
Cohorts and uncertainty
The main comparison contains 480 three-player matches across ten games:
144 from the original Splendor, 7 Wonders, and Brass cohort, plus 336 from
the seven-game expansion. Each game contributes 48 matches and 12
appearances per participant, balanced across seats. Another 252 two- or
four-player matches support player-count analysis, giving 732 native-system
matches in total. All outcomes reconcile with schedules and final records
(Appendix
D.7
). Table
3
reports the three-player cohort; PWS means weight games equally.
Its column groups follow Figure
2
.
Pointwise 95% intervals use 5,000 whole-match bootstrap draws per game,
recomputing PWS and refitting Elo while retaining the ten games
(Appendix
D.3
).
We completed 180 matches for three non-LLM algorithms—Random, Heuristic,
and ISMCTS—in separate 60-match cohorts. Their PWS scores gauge their approximate
competitive standing relative to the 12 LLM participants
(Table
3
). Each cohort contains six three-player
matches per game against two LLM opponents. The three cohorts use
identical games, rules, setup seeds, opponent configurations, and seat
assignments; only the baseline algorithm changes
(Appendix
D.8
).
Two independent studies cross OpenCode and Qwen Code in Splendor and
7 Wonders. The 72-match bridge pairs DSV4-Flash with Qwen3.8-Flash across
nine scenarios per game; the 48-match within-Qwen-family study pairs Qwen3.8-Flash with
Qwen3.8-Max across six. Within each study, those models under OpenCode serve
as anchors. Each scenario fixes the setup seed and focal seat across four
matches, with balanced seats. Pointwise intervals resample whole scenarios.
Each study retains its own anchors and analysis, separate from the native
ratings (Appendices
G
and
G.3
).

5
Discussion and Conclusion
DECE finds both cross-game ranking agreement and local differences in
competitive performance. The games share persistent investments, contested
opportunities, and alternative scoring routes, while differing in rules
and information structure. The shared roster reveals where
advantages recur and where they reverse. Aggregate rankings summarize
broad competitiveness, while game-level profiles identify settings in
which a configuration becomes more or less effective.
Complete systems as the evaluation unit.
Matched comparisons connect these profiles to agent execution. The same
model can perform differently across harnesses under matched scenarios.
Competitive results characterize the complete model–harness configuration.
Reporting tools,
context management, and execution settings makes that system explicit.
Cross-game comparisons show whether configuration gains recur or
concentrate in particular strategic settings.
From profiles to configuration selection.
The resource frontiers identify candidate configurations for a game and budget.
Future work can select configurations from game rules and results on
training games. Evaluation should compare against the best generalist
selected on those games, keeping target-game outcomes out of tuning.
Applying the same budget to both methods would test whether game-specific
selection improves competitive performance at a given resource cost.
Token and dollar budgets would test distinct deployment constraints.
Limitations.
The native and matched studies evaluate complete configurations within their
respective game and opponent panels. Component interventions, combined with
action–outcome analyses, can test how planning and memory contribute to
these performance differences.
DECE combines executable Eurogames, common tournament protocols, and
resource measurements linked to competitive outcomes. Together, these
elements support evaluation that reports where complete agent configurations
perform well, how execution choices change their results, and what
resources they require.

Appendix B
Mechanism Profiles and Annotation Rubric
Figure
2
describes the selected games using six rule-based annotations. The authors assigned ordinal values from publisher rules and the corresponding observation interfaces. RQ1 uses these annotations to organize descriptive comparisons, with group definitions in Appendix
D.4
.
Annotation rubric.
Each axis uses the following anchors. A value of zero means that the defined mechanism is absent, not that a game requires no reasoning. Hidden information concerns current private holdings; uncertainty about future random draws is excluded. Spatial planning concerns geometry and connectivity, not the mere presence of rows in a tableau.
EC: Engine construction.
0: No persistent production or action modifier; 1: Localized recurring benefit or modifier; 2: Acquired assets repeatedly change action yields, costs, or available conversions.
SP: Spatial planning.
0: No geometric adjacency or connectivity constraint; 1: Geometry affects a subsidiary mechanism; 2: Adjacency, connectivity, or packing is central to placement or scoring.
AS: Action sequencing.
0: No material ordering dependency; 1: Resource or placement prerequisites create ordering constraints; 2: Explicit reuse, activation chains, action-strength cycles, or staged production dependencies.
OC: Opponent coupling.
0: Independent boards and supplies; only final score comparison; 1: Shared offers, resource pools, or races; 2: Trade or shared infrastructure, contested ownership, neighbor military comparisons, or opponent-shaped compulsory leftovers.
HI: Hidden information.
0: Current decision-relevant holdings public; future random draws excluded; 1: Limited or optional private holdings; 2: Private hands or goals are a recurrent central part of decisions.
SC: Scoring coordination.
0: One principal additive scoring conversion; 1: Main scoring route plus a secondary bonus or threshold; 2: Overlapping, nonlinear, comparative, or staged scoring objectives.
Reading the profiles.
The colors identify three overlapping strategic emphases: building future capacity, committing space and pieces, and coordinating several systems. Opponent coupling follows the main competitive mechanism in the evaluated configuration. Wingspan’s habitat order is represented under action sequencing. Century’s information annotation follows DECE’s public merchant-hand observation. Ark Nova receives OC=1 for its shared displays and project races.

D.7
Cohort provenance and reproducibility
The native study includes 396 matches in the original cohort and 336 in
the expansion. We reconcile individual match results with the combined
outcome records and check each match’s game, player count, roster, seats,
and seed against the schedule. All 732 final game records pass integrity
checks and agree on match identity, game, rules version, seed, and terminal
status. Their 528 stored terminal ranking events agree with the reported
ranks; Splendor and Century rankings agree between individual and combined
outcome records. Checksums verify all 672 result and game-state records
in the expansion.
Deterministic analyses compute all 120 primary PWS cells from final ranks,
fit per-game Elo, and generate 5,000 whole-match bootstrap replicates and
the supplementary ranking diagnostics. Independent calculations reproduce
aggregate PWS and Elo estimates to rounding precision.
The native configuration contrasts use the same whole-match draws.
All 120 per-game and 12 equal-game PWS intervals reconcile exactly.
DECE supports replay and resume through persisted game states and execution
records. The analysis associates each scheduled match with its final
terminal outcome. Native telemetry covers all 540
Codex appearances across the two source cohorts, including 360 in the
primary three-player cohort. Native session records also cover six
GPT-5.6-Sol / OpenCode appearances.
The resource accounting and verification procedures are described
in Appendix
E
.
Matched schedules and separate baseline records reproduce PWS
for all 180 non-LLM reference matches
(Appendix
D.8
).
The native performance, horizon, execution, and same-model contrast figures
use the ten-game three-player cohort. Additional player-count analyses remain
separate; their coverage is explicitly limited to observed strata.
The within-Qwen-family study includes 48 complete outcomes and 24 matched
harness pairs, with independent outcome verification
(Appendix
G.3
). Its final game records pass integrity
and terminal-identity checks, and all focal PWS values agree with exact
rank-based arithmetic. Figure
3
and the within-Qwen-family
statistics are computed directly from the matched outcomes.
