## The comparison that matters

The direct comparison has to include AgentMonopoly, FinBoardBench, Cattle Trade, DECE, and the Vending-Bench line including the Arena research paper. Together they already cover Monopoly, asset finance, persistent competition, economic consequences, state-grounded communication analysis, legal actions, replay and execution-system effects. A first-in-category claim would be weak. The defensible proposal is an integrated, inspectable analysis of complete economic and social trajectories, with exact-prefix legal-sequence tests used to correct both agent interpretations and analyst labels. This is a contribution of method and evidence. Exclusivity is not established by this review.

### L01. Vending-Bench

[Backlund and Petersson, v1, 20 February 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.15840v1), sections2.1–2.5, 3.2 and 3.5. The original environment studies sustained vending operations, context management and persistent memory tools. Its primary score combines cash and inventory value as terminal net worth. Supplier communication is simulated; trace analysis accompanies outcome scores. Comparison: the shared question is maintaining economic coherence across individually simple actions. MonopolyBench studies strategic counterparties and rule-constrained finance in a different setting. Do not compare token counts to human-time horizons or transfer the original score definition to Vending-Bench2.

### L02. Vending-Bench2

[Andon Labs author evaluation page, accessed 29 October 2026](https://andonlabs.com/evals/vending-bench-2), improvements, operation, scoring and system-prompt sections. It uses a simulated year and terminal bank balance, with adverse supplier behavior, disrupted supply, refunds and planning tools. Comparison: economic realism is not a categorical advantage MonopolyBench can claim. This page is a changing first-party evaluation report, not the 2025 paper. Freeze the page when citing design or current results. We do not reproduce its changing leaderboard as a cross-benchmark model ranking.

### L03. Vending-Bench Arena

[Andon Labs author evaluation page](https://andonlabs.com/evals/vending-bench-arena), introduction, rounds8–9/13–14 and selected traces. Co-located businesses compete while communicating and transferring goods or money. Round-level narrative reports discuss strategic conduct. Comparison: multiplayer economics, collaboration and deception-relevant cases already exist here. Distinguish the changing page from L04's versioned systematic study. A lack of frequencies in selected page sections does not imply no systematic Arena analysis exists.

### L04. Arena communication study, a close social comparator

[Li, Petersson, Acquisti and Bakker, v3, 21 August 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.14825v3), sections2, 4, 6, appendicesA/D/F. The paper combines a speech-act taxonomy, simulator-grounded factual verification and a reasoning-trace audit; it separates operational follow-through and observational associations. Its reported norm is broader than instruction adherence. The study uses research-access Arena data. Comparison: state-grounded social analysis and commitment/action coupling are occupied. Our case-based attribution differs in scope and does not support their prevalence estimand. Important methodological lesson: intraday cash ambiguity is excluded from decisive falsity checks. Their component-validation limits prevent treating cross-judge agreement as ground truth. Do not import their misalignment label into a game that explicitly licenses bluffing.

### L05. AgentMonopoly

[Wu et al., v2, 22 October 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.29330v2), sections3–5, limitations and appendixA. It compares behavior, outcomes and personas with public messages, private generated explanations and checked actions. It explicitly limits explanation-as-intent and hardship-as-dishonesty interpretations. Its prompt permits bluffing but prohibits public/action contradiction. AppendixA.3 reports 140 games by summing 40/40/40/20. Comparison: neither speech/action linkage nor normative caution is new. Different rules and instructions prevent direct outcome comparisons. See L06 for software discrepancies. The proposed additional analysis concerns multistep accessible finance and attempt-resolution consequences, not a claim that AgentMonopoly lacks legal choices or replay.

### L06. AgentMonopoly software

[SuELS-Group repository README, accessed 29 October 2026](https://github.com/SuELS-Group/Agent_Monopoly), Architecture, Key Features, Specific Rules and Experiments. Documentation explicitly describes a deterministic engine, legal_actions in prompts and wealth trajectories. Its experiment table totals120, differing from paper appendixA.3's140. Record both source versions; do not infer which experiments were actually executed. Repository-wide code and run artifacts were not audited here. Comparison: a README claim is evidence of documented capability, not independent execution verification. The discrepancy does not justify dismissing the paper or claiming a unique artifact feature.

### L07. FinBoardBench

[Hu et al., v1, 27 May 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.27896v1), sections2–4, appendicesC/E. Cashflow, Acquire and Monopoly cover cash management, acquisition, bargaining and investment consequences. Section4.3 reports Monopoly investment profiles and Acquire monetization/redeployment. AppendixE describes validation, error feedback and forced fallback after repeated execution failures. Comparison: longitudinal financial-process motivation and distinguishing forced actions are shared. We propose explicit paid-versus-unpaid rent, inheritance, access timing and local-sequence finance accounting. Per-turn trajectory analysis and equivalent exact-prefix audits were not found in inspected sections; their absence from all code or research material is unverified. Do not call their ROI labels equivalent to causal investment returns.

### L08. Cattle Trade

[Müller and Müller, v1, 14 May 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.14537v1), sections3.1–3.3, 4.3–4.6, appendicesB–D. This competitive economic game combines auctions, hidden offers and resource allocation; agents choose structured bids/card compositions with scratchpad memory. Engine transitions and differing correction/retry/penalty rules are recorded. Code-agent comparisons provide calibration. Comparison: integrated long-horizon economic skills, logging and legal observations are shared. Exact money-card accounting highlights why simulator payment behavior belongs in the evaluated system. We did not establish a free-form persuasion channel in the inspected action design; hidden-offer bluffing is distinct from semantic financial framing.

### L09. DECE

[Zhang et al., v1, 26 October 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.35773v1), sections3–5, appendicesA.3/C/G. Ten Eurogames share observations, legal actions, transitions and recorded state. Participants are model/execution configurations; matched studies hold seed, seat and anchors fixed to compare execution systems. Resource and competitive outcomes are analyzed jointly. Comparison: replay, legal menus and configuration-conditional evaluation are shared. Its matched tests provide a stronger causal execution contrast than our observational response-condition difference. Point rank reversals and local effects have uncertainty and multiplicity limits. We cannot turn its results into evidence for the size of a MonopolyBench wrapper effect.

## Social conduct and normative measurements

### L10. SOTOPIA

[Zhou et al., v1, 18 October 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.11667v1), sections2–3, 5.2, appendicesC/D. The framework evaluates role-played social interactions across several goals and social dimensions, with human comparisons for automated ratings. Criterion agreement varies by dimension and by whether humans or models supply the dialogue. Comparison: social performance needs a declared construct and independent validation. Monopoly's ledger grounds financial propositions and realized actions, but does not automatically validate broader social or moral constructs. Our four model coauthors are not an equivalent human-validation procedure.

### L11. Concordia

[Vezhnevets et al., v1, 6 December 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2312.03664v1), sections2.1–2.3 and4.3. A generative game master links agent action descriptions, observations and grounded variables for social simulations. The design discussion emphasizes interventions and model validation. Comparison: social simulation with consequences is shared. MonopolyBench's fixed engine gives a narrower executable rule system; this is a design trade-off rather than a general realism advantage. Do not characterize every Concordia task as unconstrained chat or claim the framework lacks grounded state.

### L12. NegotiationArena

[Bianchi et al., v1](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.05863v1), sections2–5, appendixD.3; [ICML2024 proceedings](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v235/bianchi24a.html) independently confirms publication. Resource exchange, ultimatum and price negotiation allow multiple dialogue turns, logged state, and tactic comparisons. Comparison: natural-language bargaining and social-tactic analysis are shared. Persistent property holdings and delayed exposure create a different downstream analysis question in MonopolyBench. Its manipulated tactic studies can estimate comparisons that our selected closed deals cannot. No persuasion effect follows simply from a message preceding settlement.

### L13. MACHIAVELLI

[Pan et al., v1](https://arxiv.org/html/2304.03279v1), sections2.2–2.5,4–5; [ICML2023 proceedings](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v202/pan23a.html). Behavioral labels and rewards are separate axes in narrative games, and steering methods are compared through their trade-offs. Automated labels are evaluated against expert reference labels. Comparison: this is a precedent for an explicit capability/conduct research program. It does not validate a morality score for a Monopoly rule variant. Expert reference annotation is a stronger external criterion than our overlapping model adjudication. Preserve that distinction when motivating future safety studies.

### L14. Cheap Talk, Empty Promise

[Shi et al., v1, 6 April 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04782v1), sections3–4 and appendixA. Normal-form games distinguish announced from final actions and enumerate deviation opportunities. Opportunity-conditioned exploitation rates are defined separately from raw disagreement frequency. Comparison: promise/action deviation and opportunity normalization are not new. An externally specified announcement game differs from an exposed standing offer that can change with wealth and terms. We inspect the arXiv manuscript; workshop acceptance reported in earlier notes is not independently certified by this source check.

### L15. When Agents Lie

[Shi et al., v1, 6 July 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.05132v1), section3 and appendicesA/E/F. Repeated games separate private planning, public announcements, final decisions and trust updates. Their operational typology compares stage-specific outputs. Comparison: planned/public/applied distinctions are shared. A staged private-plan output is an elicited record rather than privileged access to cognition. Our asynchronous economic cases require additional referent, action-slot, exposure and conditional-offer checks. A workshop best-paper designation is not main-conference acceptance, and was not independently verified here.

### L16. Standing over commitments

[Wei et al., v1, 15 October 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.21203v1), sections2–6, appendixD. The norm specifies who may release a conditional promise; controlled chat episodes vary authority and information, with unauthorized release separated from acting after release. Comparison: commitment judgments require creditor, conditions and release rules. G18's changed standing offer cannot be treated as a violation under an assumed immutable-offer policy. This source is a close design precedent for prospective contract treatments, not evidence that our current cohort measures authorized-release accuracy.

### L17. Empty commitments v1, historical protocol

[Tang et al., v1, 1 October 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.01045v1), sections2–4. It defines future commitments relative to runtime persistence and proposes an affordance-based protocol with mocked tools. Comparison: action availability and timing constrain whether a promise can be carried out. This is especially relevant when distinguishing an engine affordance from an implemented learning or oversight system. The inspected document proposes a measurement protocol; do not present it as a completed large empirical benchmark.

## Task execution, reliability and validity

### L18. TheAgentCompany

[Xu et al., v1, 18 December 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.14161v1), sections2–4 and6. A self-hosted workplace combines applications and simulated colleagues. Task checkpoints, programmatic evaluators and some rubric-based evaluation score full and partial completion. Comparison: interactive colleagues, long tasks and partial process evidence are shared. The task contract often supplies an external goal, unlike our relative victory objective. Monopoly cannot claim that realistic workflow evaluation is absent from prior agent benchmarks.

### L19. AgentBench

[Liu et al., v1, 7 August 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2308.03688v1), sections3–4, especially3.4/4.3. Eight environments include competitive card play against a baseline and several tool environments. Analysis discusses action validity, context and multi-turn consistency. Comparison: agent evaluation was already interactive and included games. Our economic/social combination and detailed saved-history analyses are a specific focus, not the invention of multi-turn evaluation or legal-action errors.

### L20. WebArena

[Zhou et al., v1, 25 July 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2307.13854v1), sections2–3,5.2, appendixA.2. Self-hosted websites support functional tasks; evaluation combines state/program checks and answer comparisons. Some tasks are deliberately unachievable, and evaluation annotation has multiple checks. Comparison: distinguish outcome verification from text plausibility and distinguish infeasibility from failed execution. Its mixed scoring is not entirely deterministic. Restoring a website state is not evidence of exact Monopoly event-stream replay, nor does our replay validate its type of task quality.

### L21. OSWorld

[Xie et al., v1, 11 April 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.07972v1), sections2.2–2.4/3.2/5. Execution-based evaluation retrieves application state and files; task-specific checks and human quality control address diverse computer outcomes. Comparison: authoritative external state is a shared evaluation principle. Environment realism does not imply deterministic replay, and reproducible simulation does not imply real-world task transfer. We inspect the original version and do not make claims about the evolving benchmark's current success rates.

### L22. tau-bench

[Yao et al., v1, 17 June 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.12045v1), sections3–5 and appendixB. Domain policy, API tools and simulated users create task-specific interactions; final database outcomes and repeated-trial reliability are evaluated. Comparison: an explicit policy and repeated trials clarify what compliance and reliability mean. The pass^k notion cannot be estimated by treating 20 different Monopoly trajectories as repeated identical tasks. Rules enforced by our engine also differ from policies an agent can violate through otherwise valid API calls.

### L23. AgentDojo

[Debenedetti et al., v1, 19 June 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.13352v1), sections3–4 and appendixB. Benign tasks, attacker goals and untrusted tool data define a security evaluation with separate utility and attack outcomes. Comparison: its specified trust boundary supplies a violation criterion. Ordinary authorized Monopoly bargaining does not instantiate prompt-injection compromise. A future treatment could add a protected instruction and malicious communication channel, but that would be a new experiment and prompt contract.

### L24. MLE-bench

[Chan et al., v1, 9 October 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.07095v1), sections2–4/6, appendixA.3. Kaggle-derived ML tasks use performance thresholds, resource budgets and rule/plagiarism checks. Comparison: benchmark capability and compliance can be separate empirical objects under explicit prohibitions. A high task score need not imply acceptable conduct. Our analysis similarly preserves actual outcomes while auditing how actions arose, but has not measured rule-breaking opportunities or learned engineering skill.

### L25. METR time horizons

[Kwa et al., v1, 18 March 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.14499v1), sections3–4/8.1; [author page last updated8 September2026](https://metr.org/time-horizons/). The horizon is tied to human-expert task duration at a specified success probability and task distribution. It is not elapsed autonomous runtime. Comparison: Monopoly turns, decisions, token count, simulated days and human task time require separate units. Do not translate our uncapped game length into a METR horizon. Current author-page measurements and original paper versions should not be conflated.

### L26. AgentBoard

[Ma et al., v1, 24 January 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2401.13178v1), sections3.3/4.5/5.3/6. A progress metric, subgoals and analytical views supplement terminal success, with manual checks of annotations. Comparison: process-aware evaluation and visual analysis already exist. Monopoly group completion is not necessarily monotonic progress to victory; funding an opponent may outweigh a new set. Our process exhibits should retain losses, reversals and contextual limitations rather than imply a validated universal progress score.

### L27. HAL

[Kapoor et al., v1, 13 October 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.11977v1), sections2–4 and appendixA7.2; [author about page](https://hal.cs.princeton.edu/about), [software README](https://github.com/princeton-pli/hal-harness). Cost-aware evaluation, flexible execution systems and trace analysis are shared goals. The paper's flagged-run validation reports precision rather than overall accuracy. Comparison: our selected corrections cannot estimate extractor recall. Provider token semantics and missing refusal usage constrain cost accounting. Software documentation establishes available tooling, not that a particular MonopolyBench analysis used it. The inspected repository is archived; this does not invalidate the paper's historical claims.

### L28. AI Agents That Matter

[Kapoor et al., inspected v1 URL](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.01502v1), sections2/4–6. It argues for cost-controlled evaluation, appropriate task distributions and reproducible reporting, and examines benchmark shortcuts. Comparison: retries, execution policy and usage accounting belong to the evaluated system. Our most reproducible scientific object is the frozen configured-agent cohort and its artifacts. A statistically precise win fraction cannot fix poor construct validity. The downloaded HTML carries a later displayed manuscript date; we preserve URL and content hash rather than assert they match an unseen historical PDF.

### L29. Empty commitments v2, empirical study

[Tang et al., v2, 8 October 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.01045v2), sections2–5 and appendixE. This revision adds controlled requests, implemented checking and interventions, and human annotation of 300 responses yielding 400 labels. Tools remain mocked. The separate frontier-model comparison uses the judge model itself and different generation settings, both disclosed. Comparison: configuration-relative promise feasibility and anchoring now have empirical validation; the source is not merely a proposal. Its single-turn claim differs from our exposed multi-turn standing offers. External annotator independence was not established by this inspection; mocked execution and request composition limit direct transfer of measured rates. The exact v1 source is preserved for provenance, while the outline now cites v2.

## Draftable comparison sentence

Prior work already studies persistent economic agents, strategic communication, configured-system performance and state-checked actions. MonopolyBench contributes a source-linked study of complete competitive trajectories that uses exact historical decision states to test bounded legal alternatives, separates paid receipts and inherited assets, and connects recorded speech to delivered information and applied consequences. These measurements support calibrated capability and conduct findings; they do not identify hidden motives, a causal communication effect, or a capability–alignment growth curve.
