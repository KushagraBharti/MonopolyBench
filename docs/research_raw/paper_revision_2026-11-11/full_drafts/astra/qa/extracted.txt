Strategic Attention and Economic Execution
in LLM Monopoly Agents
Research draft∗
11 November 2026
Abstract
Economic agents must consider what a bargain enables for competitors and carry financial plans
into action. We study these links in twenty complete MonopolyBench games played by GPT-6.1 Sol,
Gemini 4 Argon, Claude Opus 5.5 and Grok 4.7. Across 920 negotiation responses, systems differ
sharply in countering versus rejecting despite similar acceptance counts. Offers completing only
their author’s group are rejected more often than recipient-completing offers within both opening
and later negotiations; the conditional gaps are about thirteen percentage points. Seller asks are
higher for completing deeds within six seller/mortgage categories, but same-deed comparisons are
mixed. Recorded explanations and eight contextual transcripts show attention to rival development,
financing and prospective inheritance, alongside bluffing, factual correction and changed assurances.
A census of 188 episodes requiring liquidation finds 128 debts paid through sales or mortgages,
including sixty-one multi-action payments. These provide positive evidence of executed financial
reasoning, while a small set of payable declarations and failed construction encodings distinguishes
expressed plans from completed actions. The findings concern strategic attention and local execution
in the observed configurations, under explicitly reported response and information conditions.
They do not identify optimal strategies, tactic effects or an underlying-model ranking.
1
Introduction
An LLM agent negotiating with a competitor must reason about more than the object being exchanged.
A sale can finance the seller, complete the buyer’s productive assets, and alter the position of a third
party. A convincing explanation of those consequences is one form of evidence; a useful agreement
and a correctly executed investment are others. Understanding economic agency requires connecting
them while keeping their success criteria distinct.
Monopoly provides a compact persistent economy in which these links can be observed. Negotiated
deeds become opportunities to build, development creates rent obligations, and bankruptcy transfers
assets to a creditor. The player who pays for a completing deed may not be the player who ultimately
earns its rent. Other participants can anticipate those consequences when pricing an asset, refusing a
swap or deciding whether to keep a rival solvent. The game therefore connects language-mediated
bargaining with checkable resource constraints and subsequent multi-player outcomes.
We study twenty complete games played by four configured frontier-model systems: GPT-6.1 Sol,
Gemini 4 Argon, Claude Opus 5.5 and Grok 4.7. The records contain 6,989 decisions, 526 negotiation
threads and every applied action. The study is observational. Its central question is how strategic

Three findings organize the paper. First, bargaining responds to what an exchange enables for
another player. Refusals, counteroffers and recorded reasons reveal attention to rival development
and liquidity. Asking-price differences persist within seller and mortgage categories, but the small set
of same-deed comparisons is mixed. This identifies a behavioral pattern worth explaining without
turning it into an estimated strategic premium. Second, agents execute many multi-step financing
tasks: 128 of 188 episodes requiring liquidation end in payment, sixty-one through multiple actions.
This positive census complements the smaller set of payable declarations and construction requests
that do not execute. Third, the selected conduct trajectories connect bargaining to future ownership.
Agents bluff about limits, selectively describe collateral, ask others to deny financing, and withdraw an
assurance while continuing to bid for the same group.
Our contributions are an empirical account of relational bargaining using complete offer and
counteroffer populations; a census of cash-shortfall resolution linked to development and estate flows;
and eight source-grounded communication trajectories that distinguish what was said, received and
done. The artifact trail supplies the method supporting these findings. It is not evidence that a new
general auditor has been validated. Essential prompt and response conditions are reported before the
results, including provider refusals that change one participant’s private-note and retry opportunities.
The motivating questions span strategic reasoning, opponent modeling, negotiation and deception,
planning, adaptation, economic risk, collective behavior and evaluation validity. We preserve this
breadth as a coverage map, not as a claim that twenty games measure every construct. The most direct
evidence concerns stated strategic considerations and local execution. General-versus-multi-agent
capability, causal learning, persuasion and unfamiliar-opponent transfer require a different design.
This distinction lets the paper explain what the agents do without asking outcome counts to answer
questions they cannot identify.
2
Study design and evidence
2.1
A persistent competitive economy
MonopolyBench places four agents on the forty-space United States Monopoly board with $1,500 each.
They buy and auction deeds, negotiate bilateral exchanges, build on complete color groups, and use
mortgages and building sales to meet debts. A declined purchase opens an ascending auction. Trade
threads permit up to twenty exchanges, with acceptance, rejection or counteroffer at each response.
Play resumes after the thread closes. All monetary amounts below are game currency unless explicitly
marked USD.
The game makes one transaction relevant to several agents. A completing deed permits its buyer to
develop, subject to cash, mortgage status, bank stock and even-building rules. Sale proceeds can finance
the seller’s competing development. When a player fails, a player-creditor receives its properties with
buildings intact; mortgaged inherited deeds incur no immediate interest. Bankrupt estates returned
to the bank are not immediately auctioned. Developed groups cannot be traded. These conventions
make financing and creditor identity part of the stakes in a bilateral exchange. Appendix C specifies
the implementation, including building-sale constraints.
The deterministic engine emits the legal menu and applies the selected action. A language-model
response can contain a tool call, public message, private note, assistant content and provider-returned
explanation. The engine applies the received valid tool call, not a separately stated plan. Agents
can take several management actions in a turn. During liquidation they can sell buildings, mortgage

2.2
Cohort and information
The observational cohort consists of twenty complete games: five seeds, each with four cyclic seat
rotations of the same roster. There are 50,918 events, 6,989 decisions and 8,679 response attempts.
Games last 75–386 turns and end in bankruptcy without a turn cap. Every system occupies each seat
five times, but the cyclic opponent order is fixed. Rotations share a random sequence, not necessarily
the same later dice exposure: different actions can change which player consumes subsequent draws.
We use Gn dk for zero-based decision k in game n.
Agents see their own and opponents’ cash and holdings, buildings, mortgage flags, bank stock,
a twenty-entry public timeline and up to ten own private notes. Model names are visible. Current
trade terms and thread history accompany trade responses. Opponents’ private notes and future
random outcomes are hidden. Purchase prompts supply the landed property’s price, group, building
cost and rent schedule; other phases supply less static board information. The system prompt does
not enumerate the house rules. Agents can therefore draw on board knowledge as well as supplied
information, and remembered values need not match every implementation convention.
The task explicitly permits public bluffing, deception, intimidation and cooperation. It asks private
notes to reflect honest reasoning. The recovery prompt described below retains permission to deceive
while removing the private-note request. We study strategic communication under this norm. An
authorized bluff is not a violation of the task, and the observed tactics do not establish unprompted
emergence. The exact two prompts appear in Appendix D.
2.3
Conditions affecting interpretation
An invalid response normally receives one corrective request before a deterministic fallback. A first-
attempt provider refusal instead consumes that second attempt with an action-only request. Opus
has 1,604 such first-attempt refusals; a further refusal occurs after an invalid first response. All 1,605
refusals contain the same provider message about reverse engineering or duplicating model outputs.
None of the 1,604 action-only recovery responses is refused. The standard prompt requests private
reasoning, whereas recovery removes that request and retains public deception. This evidence points
to a reasoning-request incompatibility, not refusal to bluff. Current provider documentation describes
a reasoning-extraction refusal category [1]; the saved responses do not include that category field, so
the exact upstream trigger is not established.
The refusal rate reaches 100% of first attempts in G5–G20, with the transition already occurring
by wall clock within G1–G4. Opus consequently has fewer authored private notes and no further

note in 1,394 decisions. It still receives public history and current state. Grok has a different condition:
in 139 selected responses, the action named in assistant content disagrees with the received tool call.
Sixty-four of its 136 opening proposals and seven of its twenty-eight acceptances have this disagreement.

Past-trade summaries also misattribute some terms and authors in 2,054 initial prompts. Current

We distinguish transaction measurement from interpretation of delivered history. Excluding the
affected participant would not remove its influence on the other players. These conditions define
the studied systems and prevent a clean ranking of underlying models. Detailed timing, usage and
delivery records are retained in the appendix rather than repeated with each result.
2.4
Measures, comparisons and uncertainty
An offer is one set of terms awaiting a response; its author can differ from the thread initiator. We recon-
struct each party’s post-transfer cash, charges, ownership and newly completed groups. Completion
includes mortgaged deeds and does not imply immediate construction. A completion episode ends
when its owner loses the group or the game ends. A first management window comprises that owner’s
management decisions in the first management turn after completion. Construction availability is
specific to the completed group.
Our new liquidation census begins whenever a debt requires an explicit liquidation menu. It follows
consecutive actions against the same obligation until the event stream records payment or bankruptcy.
Cash-sufficient automatic payments are outside this denominator. For each episode we record the
initial cash shortfall, sales, mortgages and outcome. The generous own-asset bound is initial cash plus
half the building cost per level and the mortgage value of every unmortgaged deed, including collateral
that building sales would unlock. It ignores sale-order and bank-stock restrictions. Falling below
the debt rules out payment by these own assets; exceeding it does not establish feasibility. Historical
legal-sequence checks resolve the four elimination cases above that bound. We do not execute new
branches in this revision.
Asking-price comparisons use acceptance-legal offers exchanging exactly one deed for cash, without
jail cards. We divide the requested cash by the deed’s board purchase price and separate author,
mortgage status and thread position. This normalizes units, not economic value. A same-game,
same-seller, same-deed comparison still changes buyer, time or terms. Rejection comparisons similarly
separate opening from later offers. The appendix gives every cell, including exceptions.
These are dependent, retrospectively studied records. We report exact finite-corpus counts, condi-
tional contrasts, and leave-one-seed-block-out ranges showing how concentrated a contrast is. Those
ranges are sensitivity summaries, not confidence intervals or a significance test. Five seeds and one
observation per seed/rotation do not estimate repeated-run variability at a fixed state or isolate chance
from strategy. We make no population claim that an extra offer supplies an independent trial of
reasoning ability.
Semantic evidence has a separate status. Two Astra research instances coded the seventy-four
outright rejections of legally acceptable, recipient-completing offers; the second had partial exposure
to prior aggregates and one record. We retain agreed codes and disagreements, rather than call them
human ground truth. A single LLM-assisted reader examined a 240-decision temporal sample. Eight
incidents were chosen for distinct mechanisms and contrasts. Exact quotations, source channels and
recipient exposure are checked, but selection is not a prevalence sample. Explanation text is evidence

3
Agents bargain over what the other player can do
3.1
Disagreement changes both participation and price
The agents do not negotiate by simply seeking agreement. Across 526 threads, 920 responses comprise
113 acceptances, 394 counters and 413 rejections. Acceptance is legal for 918 offers. Sol and Opus
counter most often, while Gemini and Grok reject most often (Table 2). Yet each accepts between
twenty-six and thirty offers. Similar settlement counts therefore coexist with very different forms of
disagreement. The low overall acceptance fraction, 113/918, describes selectivity; it does not establish
that accepted terms are fair or optimal.
Offers completing only their author’s group are rejected more often than offers completing only the
recipient’s group: 159/289 versus 38/140. That pooled 27.9-point difference partly reflects negotiation
stage. Author-only offers more often open a thread, where rejection is common. Among openings,
the contrast is 138/187 versus 31/51, or 73.8% versus 60.8%; among later offers it is 21/102 versus
7/89, or 20.6% versus 7.9%. Removing one seed block at a time leaves opening differences of 3.0–25.0
percentage points and later differences of 10.0–15.7 points. The later-offer pattern is less concentrated
across these blocks, although the remaining offers are selected by earlier bargaining.
This is not a uniform configuration effect. Pooled over stage, the rejection ordering holds for Sol,
Gemini and Grok; Opus reverses it and mostly counters both categories. Within stage the ordering
holds in five of six Sol/Gemini/Grok cells. Acceptance orderings are less consistent. Table 4 reports all
completion categories. The result is a descriptive relation between the opportunity being offered and
the response, not a causal effect of handing a rival a monopoly.
Counters reveal a practice shared across opportunities: preserving goods while negotiating cash.
Of 111 counters to author-only completion offers, ninety-two retain all requested deeds; seventy-nine
preserve the entire noncash exchange and improve the counterer’s cash. In a comparison population of
110 non-completing offers that request a deed and receive a counter, those counts are eighty-seven and
eighty-one. Sol and Opus write ninety-two and eighty of these respective populations. Thus repricing
the same exchange is common bargaining behavior, not specific evidence of a completion premium.

3.2
Expressed reasons connect cash to rival development
The recorded explanations identify the economic considerations behind some refusals. Both LLM-
assisted readings find a stated trade-linked consequence for the rival in sixty-one of seventy-four
rejections that would complete the recipient’s group. This includes 27/38 recipient-only completions
and 34/36 offers completing both parties. Gemini and Grok supply fifty-six of the agreed sixty-one;
thirty-four records come from G1 and G15. The text concerns rival building budgets, blockers and
survival, including trades that would give the rival no new complete group. This is concentrated
evidence of strategic attention, not a validated rate of correct opponent modeling.

Transcript 1. Own completion is refused because the rival could develop sooner
At G15 turn 63, Sol has $730 and Grok $333. Sol offers two greens for Ventnor plus $100, completing
both players’ groups. Grok responds:
Grok · applied private note · G15 d169 · attempt 0 · excerpt
Trade gives p4 Atlantic+Ventnor+Marvin and ~$830 plus mortgage capacity to drop 5+ yellow
houses ($150) immediately, then they roll again on doubles. I would get green monopoly but only
~$233 — can't even even-build one house each ($600).
The post-trade balances, $830 and $233, agree with the offer ledger. A full first layer of green houses
costs $600, although one legal house costs $200. Sol removes the cash request and adds Kentucky, then
sweetens again. Grok’s final public response is:
Grok · public message · G15 d175 · attempt 0 · full field
No. $250 doesn't fix it. You'd still mortgage and drop houses on yellows before you roll again.
Ventnor stays mine.
What happened. All three offers fail. Eighteen turns later Grok is eliminated and its blocking deeds
pass to Gemini, which wins; Sol never completes either group.
What this shows. Grok considers its own new monopoly together with the rival’s ability to build. Sol
revises terms in response to that objection. The observed reasoning is relational and liquidity-sensitive.
The later loss does not tell us whether accepting any of the rejected offers would have been better.
G15 d168–175, standard responses; quoted public messages are present in the next counterparty requests.
Transcript 2. A seller prices the rival’s development budget
At G2 turn 45, Opus holds two yellows and bids $325 for Sol’s Marvin Gardens. Sol counters:
Sol · public message · G2 d124 · attempt 0 · full field
Marvin completes your yellows. $650 buys it; $325 doesn't compensate for handing you that
monopoly.
One turn earlier Sol asked Grok, which owned no yellow, for $450 for that deed. It now asks Opus
for $650, then $600. Its note explicitly discusses Opus’s post-purchase building funds. The note’s $520
cash remainder is correct; its claim that this buys nine yellow houses is not, because each costs $150.
Opus advertises a final $450 while retaining a private $475 contingency; Sol refuses.
What happened. At turn 69 the roles reverse. Opus has $17 and sells its two yellows to Sol for two
railroads plus $100. Three turns later those railroads pass to Gemini in Opus’s bankruptcy. Sol builds
on yellow, later sells those buildings, collects $44 undeveloped rent and no developed yellow rent, and
eventually transfers yellow to Gemini through bankruptcy.
What this shows. The same deed draws different asks depending on the buyer, and the expressed
valuation includes what that buyer could build. Subsequent financial positions reverse who buys the
group. The trajectory illustrates why a completed exchange and its final owner must be distinguished.
G2 d119–128 and d197–198; the undeveloped rent is event 1566. The private ceiling is a recorded contingency, not an
estimated reservation value.

Language can bargain, conceal or correct
Public communication accompanies structured terms. Its strategic role cannot be read from settlement
alone: a recipient may accept the price while rejecting the explanation. The next two incidents contrast
an agreed bargaining bluff with explicit factual correction.

Financial reasoning becomes a sequence of actions
4.1
Meeting cash shortfalls
A debt can require several actions even when the debtor owns enough assets. Our census finds 188
episodes entering liquidation, not just the sixty that end the player’s game. In 128 episodes the agent
raises funds and pays, executing 180 mortgages and eighty-nine building-sale actions. These payments
total $51,997. Sixty-seven take one action, while sixty-one require two to ten. All four systems execute
payments: Sol thirty, Gemini thirty-seven, Opus forty-one and Grok twenty (Table 3). The counts
describe the obligations each encountered and are not comparable difficulty-adjusted success rates.
The payable obligations provide direct evidence of financial execution. In G20 d327–336, Sol starts
with $49 against $1,400 and combines $700 building salvage with $665 mortgages, paying with $14 left.
More broadly, all 128 payments have sufficient initial own-asset bounds, and the actual event sequence
demonstrates the financing used. These are solved local problems with checkable endpoints. They do
not establish that the earlier investments that created the shortfall were wise.
Most terminal debts are different: fifty-six exceed even the generous own-asset bound. Of the other
four, historical checks find three legal payment sequences. The remaining case, G7 Opus, owes $750
with nominal resources of $1,310 but can reach only $440 through its offered menu. Its hotel cannot be
downgraded with zero bank houses, and the menu offers no building sale. Appendix C explains the
distinction between this menu and broader bundled-sale validation. Thus the full census separates
128 executed payments, fifty-six insufficient-asset endpoints, one menu restriction and three payable
declarations. This is more informative about local competence than a count of bankruptcies alone.

4.2
Financing investment after agreement
Trade completes sixty-three color groups. All reach a first management window, and forty-three receive
construction in that window: Sol 16/17, Gemini 17/18, Opus 7/15 and Grok 3/13. The remaining
twenty comprise nine groups with no construction option for that group on any recorded menu in
the window, four defaults, four content/call conflicts, and three other valid ends. Availability can
itself depend on financing decisions. Seven of the nine receive a mortgaged completing deed; in two
cases cash alone can cover redemption and one house, and in five more a static budget including other
mortgageable deeds suffices. Those budgets are arithmetic descriptions, not newly executed plans.
Grok’s four no-option windows contain stated decisions to defer redemption or development and
retain liquidity. Its other six unbuilt windows contain four conflicting calls and two stated holds.
Opus’s eight comprise five no-option windows under recovery and three defaults. Thus the observed
variation combines acquisition terms, financing priorities and execution. A low build fraction cannot
simply be read as a preference for cash, nor can every unbuilt group be assigned to the response
system.
Historical checks of sixty-one Opus construction defaults preserve the submitted purchase multiset
and execute it after splitting count-valued entries and interleaving purchases evenly. The original
plans remain invalid. Sixty follow a refused first attempt and invalid recovery; one follows invalid
construction and then refusal. This identifies how a feasible investment target can fail at serialization
and exhaust its opportunity for correction. It does not establish how a fresh retry would have answered.
Other systems do correct many invalid plans: among uneven-build rejections ending in a build, the
initial multiset is retained in 20/25 Sol, 6/18 Gemini and 5/5 Grok cases. Changed allocations can also
be successful repairs.
The financial scale of subsequent development is substantial, but its interpretation requires exposure.
Trade-completed groups receive $57,378 before loss or game end, of which $53,810 goes to the forty-
three built in their first window. A new descriptive exposure check finds similar median remaining
durations, sixty-two versus 63.5 global turns, and forty-two versus forty-seven opponent movements,
for first-window-built and other groups. Median receipts are $590 and $56. Simple duration alone
therefore does not describe the separation, but exact landing opportunities, group type, development
levels and selection still differ. Eleven of the twenty other groups build later. These are realized receipts,
not incremental returns or an estimated benefit of accelerating construction.

4.3
Targets across turns
The corpus also contains extended targets. In G9, Sol’s turn-29 note proposes three houses on each
dark-blue deed as funds arrive; financing and construction reach that target on turn 60. G14 d145–148
realizes an initial eight-house target over several management decisions despite changing the first
attempted allocation. These cases demonstrate expressed goals with later action, not a measured causal
benefit of private memory.
A separate 240-decision, single-reader sample contains fifty-nine prospective own-action statements
and thirty-six opponent-oriented statements, with overlap. Eight name a property to buy if landed on;
six never encounter that opportunity, one is carried out and one ends in a conflicting purchase/auction
response. Fifty-three of sixty Opus sample records lack private notes. We use these records to identify
forms of contingent planning, rather than estimate a general completion rate from opportunities that
mostly never arise. Likewise, repeated construction errors occur under unequal feedback conditions.
They show repair and recurrence, but cannot establish learning or adaptation to unfamiliar opponents.

Bargaining anticipates who inherits
5.1
Third parties matter to bilateral trades
With intact creditor inheritance, keeping another player solvent can preserve a blocker against a third
player. Conversely, giving a near-bankrupt opponent cash can postpone an estate the buyer expects to
receive. These are different strategic reasons for a similar transaction. The following incidents show
agents expressing both, with later actions that clarify what they actually do.

5.2
The economic continuation
Estates are a substantial route of asset transfer in the recorded games. Trade moves 207 deeds; nonter-
minal bankruptcy moves 227, followed by 170 at the final eliminations. Nonterminal estates complete
fifty-two color groups, compared with sixty-three through trade. Sixty-nine further completions occur
at game end and have no subsequent earning opportunity. Fourteen of the fifty-two nonterminal
estate-completed groups receive rent. This separation prevents terminal bookkeeping from being
mistaken for productive acquisition.
The eventual winner receives the first estate in eighteen games, thirty-five of forty nonterminal
estates and 202 of their 227 deeds. These outcome-conditioned counts describe a concentration path.
They do not show that inheritance independently caused victory. A new check of the delivered state
immediately before the first elimination finds its creditor already strictly ahead in building levels in
twelve games, cash in eight and deed count in six. Neither “the strongest player simply inherited” nor
“inheritance created the strongest player” is established by these summaries. The initial position and
ensuing transfer coexist in the observed trajectory.
The connection to development is direct: thirty-eight of forty nonterminal eliminations follow
rent on the creditor’s built color property, as do fifty-seven of all sixty eliminations. Such rent can
produce cash, an estate, or both residual cash and an estate. Consequently, a receipts-only return
calculation misses an economically consequential endpoint. Conversely, an estate with no later earning
opportunity should not be counted as realized rent-generating success.
G15 illustrates this sequence without trade by the eventual winner. Gemini completes orange by
buying Tennessee at turn 47, builds immediately, and later receives $3,250 developed orange rent.
Orange triggers the first two eliminations; inherited brown triggers the last. Green completes only
in the terminal estate. Sol likewise wins G14 without settling a trade. These trajectories show why
negotiation volume is not a necessary path to victory in this ruleset, and why the identity of the
eventual owner can differ from the player who assembled or first developed a group.
6
What the records teach about LLM agency
The clearest behavioral finding is strategic attention to consequences for other players. Agents discuss more
than the immediate exchange: what the recipient could build, which deed blocks a rival, who gains
cash, and who might inherit. This attention appears in explicit reasons, offers revised after objections,
differing asks, and refusals of one’s own completion. It also appears in the opposite direction, when
Opus continues bargaining for a group after withdrawing an earlier price. These are concrete forms of
state-sensitive social reasoning. They do not require us to infer a calibrated model of another agent’s
hidden beliefs.
Strategic attention is useful evidence, but strategic effectiveness is a separate construct. Our strongest
single-asset pricing comparisons are mixed; statement coding is concentrated; rejected bargains have
14

no observed counterfactual payoff. The paper therefore does not label every refusal as skillful or
every acceptance as gullible. G19 makes the distinction especially clear: the recipient recognizes a
false explanation and accepts terms it separately evaluates as favorable. Research on LLM bargaining
benefits from measuring both the structured agreement and the language used to justify it, rather than
treating either as a sufficient account of the other.
A second finding concerns executed multi-step reasoning. The 128 resolved cash shortfalls, including
sixty-one multi-action payments, provide positive evidence with a concrete success criterion. The
agent has to identify resources, choose legal actions and pay a known obligation. This is narrower
than winning but more directly interpretable. Longer investment targets and retained construction
allocations show related forms of planning. The exceptions help locate the boundary: a model can
express a useful target while producing an invalid serialization, or derive a payment route while
a conflicting call declares bankruptcy. In agent systems, reasoning and action selection are jointly
consequential. Neither fluent explanations nor billing-reported reasoning tokens can substitute for
observing completed actions.
A third lesson is that bilateral bargains have persistent, multi-party consequences. A buyer’s investment
can generate rent against the seller; a financing transfer can preserve a third player’s blocker; a failed
debtor can hand its buildings to an opponent. The transcripts expose how agents describe those
dependencies, and the event records show which paths actually occur. This gives the economic
trajectories a role beyond illustrative anecdotes. It also limits their interpretation: the selected cases
cannot establish that distress systematically increases deception, that bluffing improves prices, or that
a financial-denial appeal forms a durable coalition.
The broader questions motivating this work concern strategic rationality, opponent modeling,
negotiation and deception, long-horizon planning, adaptation, economic risk, collective behavior and
evaluation validity. Appendix B maps each to the evidence and what remains unmeasured. We can
answer the concrete question of whether an agent ever refuses its own monopoly because of a rival’s
stated advantage: yes, repeatedly in the observed records. We can identify local payments, contingent
targets and authorized bluffs. We cannot infer a general capability relationship, learning effect or
transfer to new opponents from this cohort. An explicit unanswered row prevents absence of a suitable
design from being reported as absence of a capability.
6.1
Scope and limits
Twenty games with five seeds, one cyclic opponent order and no repeated seed/rotation cells do not
support a model ranking. Wins are 8/7/3/2 for Sol/Gemini/Opus/Grok and 7/7/3/3 for seats one
through four. Sol wins one block from every seat; a different block is won from seat two by all four
systems, each acquiring dark blue early. These patterns demonstrate why wins alone do not separate
skill, seating, random exposure and opponent behavior. No fixed-policy, human or value-oracle
comparator was evaluated in this cohort.
The information and response conditions in Section 2 affect the entire interacting population,
including opponents of the altered participant. Our findings concern those configured systems in
this ruleset. Public deception is invited rather than prohibited, and hidden internal reasoning is not
observed. The retrospective LLM-assisted coding and purposive incidents have no independent human
validation, and six of eight incidents involve Grok, whose text channels are particularly observable.
All new tabulations are exploratory. We preserve exact definitions, alternative cuts and source records
to make the conclusions inspectable, without treating internal agreement as external validation.

Related work
Negotiation as action and language.
Earlier neural negotiation studies distinguish agreement, utility
and linguistic behavior. Lewis et al. [9] compare likelihood training, reinforcement learning and
dialogue rollouts using explicit utilities, including interactions with humans. He et al. [4] separate
strategy from generation through coarse dialogue acts and evaluate both task success and conversational
behavior. NegotiationArena studies controlled tactic changes in bargaining settings [3]. These studies
motivate our separation of proposed terms, expressed reasons and outcomes. Their utility references
or interventions provide conclusions about quality or tactic effects that our observational game record
does not supply.
The fact that a sale changes other players’ opportunities is an established economic idea. Work on
auctions with identity-dependent externalities formalizes how a buyer’s acquisition affects non-buyers
and their outside options [6]. We use this perspective to describe the stakes that appear in agents’
messages, not to claim a new bargaining equilibrium or estimate the optimal price of a Monopoly deed.
The value of withholding, financing or preserving a blocker is a question about future interaction as
well as immediate cash.
Long-horizon economic agents.
Vending-Bench studies sustained business operation [2]. The Arena
communication study links emails to simulator state, checks subsequent commitments, validates parts
of its annotation pipeline and repeats classification with other judge families [10]. Its corpus-level
communication estimates are methodologically different from our selected incidents. We adopt the
useful presentation principle of showing a proposition, its context and later action, while keeping the
conduct norm and evidential scope explicit.
Cattle Trade combines auctions, hidden offers and resource allocation with code-agent comparisons;
its analysis associates coherent spending with outcomes rather than equating expenditure volume with
skill [11]. AgentMonopoly studies model-family and persona variation in Monopoly behavior [13].
FinBoardBench includes counteroffers and financial management across board games [5]. Thus neither
LLM Monopoly play nor contextual bargaining is novel by itself. Our contribution is the joined
observation of offer terms, delivered context, financing sequences and creditor transfers over complete
games, including a census of successfully resolved cash shortfalls. We do not claim that comparison
systems lack these capabilities merely because their papers emphasize different outputs.
Inference from agent traces.
Explanation-faithfulness research warns against identifying returned
rationale with internal computation [8, 12]. Work on complete agent evaluation emphasizes the
configuration beyond the base model [7, 14]. Our use of response attempts and legal menus follows
that principle: it supports attribution within this cohort without turning the study into a general-
purpose auditing-method claim. The scientific object remains the agents’ bargaining and economic
action.
8
Conclusion
In twenty complete MonopolyBench games, LLM agents explicitly consider rival development, financ-
ing and prospective inheritance while bargaining. They counter and refuse in different ways, vary
asking prices with buyer circumstances, and sometimes reject their own monopoly to withhold an
opponent’s opportunity. They also execute substantial local financial reasoning: 128 cash-shortfall
episodes end in payment, sixty-one through multiple actions. Eight contextual transcripts connect
16

bluffing, correction, selective disclosure and revised assurances to these economic stakes. The result-
ing picture is one of observable strategic attention and often successful local execution, without an
identified measure of optimal strategy or a ranking of models. Linking statements to opportunities
and completed actions makes those positive findings both concrete and appropriately scoped.

Implementation and historical checks
The frozen rules use 32 bank houses and twelve hotels. Construction is checked entry by entry, so
ordering matters even for an even final allocation. Mortgages are unavailable on deeds in a developed
group until its buildings are removed. The liquidation menu identifies highest-built deeds, and hotel
downgrade requires four houses in the bank. In G7, that menu restriction leaves no sale option;
reading the separate validator shows that it can accept a broader bundled group sale if such a sale is
offered. The historical classification concerns the available menu, not a universal impossibility under
all implementations of Monopoly.
Mortgage redemption and transferred-deed interest use floating-point ceiling expressions. For five
board mortgage principals, redemption exceeds the exact ten-percent charge by one dollar; transferred-
deed interest is exact across the board principals. Thus an agent quoting the exact ten-percent re-
demption figure need not have made an arithmetic mistake. No management prompt directly supplies
a redemption price. Buildings pass intact to a player-creditor, inherited mortgaged deeds incur no
interest at transfer, developed groups cannot trade, and assets returned to the bank are not immediately

auctioned.
The earlier executable audit covers all sixty eliminations: fifty-six insufficient generous bounds,
three positive payment sequences and one restricted menu search. It also covers sixty-six construction
defaults, including sixty-one Opus allocations that become executable after legal reordering without
changing purchases. Those results are inherited here; the revision runs no engine, replay, baseline,
model evaluation or synthetic scenario. Historical replay establishes reproduction under the frozen
implementation and canonicalization, not correctness against an independent rules specification.
Stored canonicalization removes observational identifiers and timing rather than economic action

Research assistance and reproducibility
The research workflow uses Astra, Sol, Opus and Fable 5.2 LLM assistants to write complete indepen-
dent drafts and review the full evidence. This draft is Astra’s independent revision before reading
the other new manuscripts. Sol and Opus research assistants share model families or identifiers with
evaluated participants but operate under different research tasks and effort settings. Semantic coding
was performed by LLM instances as described in the main text, not by a human annotation panel.
Human authorship remains to be assigned before submission; the assistants are not authors.
The new tools/analyze_records.py reads original decision and event streams plus the retained offer
and completion ledgers. It closes every liquidation episode against a recorded cash event, reconciles
episode rents, preserves order, and records input hashes in data/source_hashes.json. The generated
tables and figures derive from these outputs. Exact quotation manifests identify original paths, hashes,
fields and character spans. Existing historical witness results are distinguished from new record
tabulations in the evidence map. The previous manuscript, outline, literature library and canonical
games remain unchanged. No new evaluation is part of this revision.
