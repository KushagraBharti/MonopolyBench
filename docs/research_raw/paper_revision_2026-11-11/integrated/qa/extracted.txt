Bargaining over a Rival’s Next Move:
LLM Agents in Twenty Monopoly Games
Research draft∗
11 November 2026
Abstract
A bargain can improve a competitor’s position as well as one’s own. We study how LLM agents
respond to this tension in twenty complete four-player Monopoly games, connecting 920 negotiation
responses to financing and ownership. The central bargaining contrast depends on the offer’s
form. Opening swaps that complete only the proposer’s color group are rejected in 95 of 116
cases, compared with 37 of 72 swaps completing neither party. Cash bids show no corresponding
increase: rejection is 42 of 70 versus 67 of 103. Stated reasons and eight contextual incidents connect
bargaining to rival construction, the use of sale proceeds, and anticipated inheritance. Asking prices
vary with the buyer’s holdings, while same-deed comparisons and cash-related changes differ
across systems. Financial execution supplies a checkable local goal: agents pay 128 of 131 debts
shown payable, including 61 multi-action payments. In 19 payments they sell buildings and then
mortgage collateral absent from the initial menu. A ten-action sequence completes an announced
liquidation plan and leaves exactly the predicted $14. These findings distinguish attention to
strategic consequences from the execution of local plans. They describe four configured systems
under unequal response conditions, including widespread provider-refusal recovery for Opus. The
study identifies observable forms of strategic behavior without establishing bargaining effectiveness
or ranking underlying models.
1
Introduction
Selling the last deed in a rival’s color group raises cash now and may create a larger rent threat later.
Buying it can leave too little money to build. Keeping its owner solvent can preserve a blocker against a
third player; refusing to finance that owner can deliver the same property through bankruptcy. These
decisions require an agent to connect a proposed exchange to other players’ opportunities and to its
own future actions.
LLM negotiation research has studied agreement, utility and conversational tactics in settings with
explicit payoffs [3, 6, 11]. Complete economic games add persistent consequences: an agreement
changes the next negotiation, construction changes future debts, and elimination changes who owns
the productive assets. The question is therefore not only whether an agent can negotiate plausible
terms, but which consequences it attends to and whether it can carry a financial plan into action.
Strategic attention and strategic effectiveness are different objects. An agent can correctly identify a
rival’s opportunity without choosing a good price or a good refusal.

We examine this distinction in twenty complete MonopolyBench games played by GPT-6.1 Sol,
Gemini 4 Argon, Claude Opus 5.5 and Grok 4.7. The corpus contains 6,989 decisions, 526 negotiation
threads and 188 cash-shortfall episodes requiring explicit liquidation. Every offer is linked to the
recipient’s delivered state and response, and every financing sequence to the resulting payment or
bankruptcy. This supports an observational account of bargaining and economic execution, rather
than a leaderboard of underlying model ability.
The central bargaining pattern depends on what the proposer offers in return. Opening swaps
completing only the proposer’s group receive 81.9% rejections, compared with 51.4% for swaps
completing neither party. Cash bids for completing deeds do not receive more rejections than other cash
bids. Asking prices nevertheless vary with the buyer’s holdings, and statements accompanying both
refusals and prices discuss construction budgets, financing and prospective estates. The agents attend
to public economic consequences, but their behavior is not captured by a single rule of withholding
every completing deed. The holder’s cash and the negotiation history further qualify the pattern.
Financial execution supplies a complementary test with a known endpoint. Agents pay 128 of
131 debts for which payment is demonstrated by actual play or earlier legal-sequence checks. In 19
successful episodes, selling buildings makes new mortgages available and the agent uses that collateral.
Two payable bankruptcies accompany calculations that count the initial mortgage menu and building
salvage but omit the mortgages those sales would unlock. The contrast concerns a fundamental
planning problem: anticipating how an action changes the next available actions.
The paper contributes three linked empirical results:
1. A complete negotiation account showing how the completion contrast differs between swaps and
cash bids, with opening stage, holder cash and recipient configuration made explicit.
2. A census of financing sequences that distinguishes successful local execution, unpayable obligations,
and failures to anticipate newly available collateral.
3. Eight contextual incidents connecting refusal, pricing, bluffing, factual correction, selective disclo-
sure, a financing-denial appeal and withdrawal of an assurance to subsequent action, including a
completed ten-action payment plan.
The incidents sit within the economic results they illuminate. Their later consequences matter: the
player who buys or builds a group may eventually transfer it to the rival it sought to contain. They are
selected examples, not estimates of deception frequency. Public deception is permitted by the task;
calling a tactic a bluff does not make it an instruction violation.
The broader research agenda asks about strategic reasoning, opponent modeling, negotiation and
deception, planning and memory, adaptation, risk management, collective dynamics, and evaluation
validity. It also asks whether strategic talk becomes action, whether general capability predicts multi-
agent capability, and how skill can be separated from chance. Appendix B gives a direct answer at
the level supported for every question. The present study is strongest on observable bargaining and
financial execution. It has no independent general-capability measure, intervention on memory or
communication, or value reference for rejected trades.

A declined purchase opens an ascending auction. Trades can transfer deeds, cash and jail cards;
developed groups cannot be traded. All amounts below are game currency unless marked USD.
Cash, complete ownership, mortgage status, even building and bank stock jointly govern construc-
tion. A debtor can sell buildings or mortgage available deeds, with a new legal menu after each action,
but cannot negotiate a rescue trade during liquidation. It may declare bankruptcy even when payment
is possible. Buildings pass intact to a player-creditor; inherited mortgaged deeds incur no immediate
interest. Assets returned to the bank are not immediately auctioned. These rules connect bargaining to
finance and creditor succession. Implementation details appear in Appendix C.
The engine produces legal options, validates actions and emits the resulting events. The model
supplies a tool call with a public message and, under the standard prompt, a private note. Assistant
content and provider-returned explanations are separate recorded channels. The received valid tool
call controls the action; the engine does not substitute a plan described elsewhere in the response.
2.2
Cohort and information
The twenty games comprise five seeds with four cyclic seat rotations of one roster. Each system occupies
each seat five times, but always follows the same opponent in the cyclic order. Games last 75–386 turns
and end in bankruptcy, without a turn cap. There are 50,918 events, 6,989 decisions and 8,679 attempts.
Shared seeds do not guarantee identical later exposure to random outcomes: actions can change which
player consumes a draw. We write Gn dk for zero-based decision k in game n.
Agents see cash, holdings, buildings, mortgages, bank stock, model names, a twenty-entry public
timeline and up to ten own private notes. Trade responses include the current terms and thread history.
Opponents’ private notes and future randomness are hidden. Purchase prompts show the landed
property’s price, group, building cost and rents; auction prompts show its group. Other phases provide
space keys rather than a full board-value table. The system prompt does not enumerate the house
rules. Board-value calculations can therefore depend on recalled information as well as supplied facts.
The prompt permits public bluffing, deception, intimidation and cooperation, while asking private
notes to reflect honest reasoning. Its complete text and the recovery variant are printed in Appendix D.
We interpret conduct under that explicit norm. Returned explanations supply expressed considerations,
not privileged access to internal computation [10, 14].
2.3
Response conditions
An invalid first response normally receives one corrective request before deterministic fallback. A
first-attempt provider refusal instead uses the second attempt for action-only recovery, without a
private-note request. Opus has 1,604 first-attempt refusals and one later refusal. All 1,605 carry
the same provider message about reverse engineering or duplicating model outputs. None of its
1,604 recovery responses is refused. Recovery removes the private-reasoning request and retains
3

permission to deceive; the records therefore do not support interpreting these as refusals to bluff.
Provider documentation describes a reasoning-extraction refusal category, but the saved responses do
not identify that category, so the upstream trigger remains unconfirmed [1].
Refusals become universal in G5–G20; the transition already occurs by wall clock during G1–G4.
Opus consequently plays most decisions without an authored private note or a further corrective
attempt. It still receives public history and current state. Grok has a different condition: 139 selected
responses contain an action name in assistant content that disagrees with the received call, including
64 of its 136 trade openings and seven of its twenty-eight acceptances. These are offers and actions that
actually occur, but not unambiguous expressions of a plan shared across channels.
Past-trade summaries misattribute some terms or authors in 2,054 initial prompts. Current holdings
are correct, and all 920 current offers agree with the recipient’s acceptance menu. These information
and response conditions affect all participants through their interactions. Removing one system’s rows
would not recover a clean comparison of the others. Appendix D reports timing, token fields and costs;
reported reasoning tokens are not a measure of how much a model thought.
2.4
Measures and evidential scope
An offer is a set of terms awaiting a response. Its author may differ from the thread initiator. We
reconstruct cash, ownership and newly completed groups after the proposed transfer. A complete
group can contain mortgaged deeds. Rejection comparisons distinguish opening from later offers,
requests that actually ask the recipient for a deed, and swaps from pure cash bids. These restrictions
supply more relevant comparisons; they do not match prices, players or positions.
Single-deed price comparisons use legal offers exchanging one deed for cash without jail cards.
Price divided by board purchase price normalizes units, not economic value. An unchanged-bundle
concession keeps all noncash terms of the author’s preceding offer to the same recipient and improves
the recipient’s cash. Repeated observations within threads remain dependent.
A liquidation episode starts at an explicit debt menu and ends at payment or elimination. Payments
from sufficient cash are excluded. The generous own-asset bound adds cash, half the building cost
for each level, and every unmortgaged deed’s mortgage value, including collateral that building sales
unlock. A bound below the debt rules out payment from these assets; a sufficient bound is not a feasible
plan. Previously executed checks supply three payment sequences and the remaining menu-restricted
elimination. This revision uses saved records and does not execute new games, engine branches or
model evaluations.
We report exact counts, stage and configuration breakdowns, and sensitivity across five seed blocks.
These are retrospective descriptions, not independent-trial tests or confidence intervals for new games.
Two LLM-assisted readings coded seventy-four rejections of recipient-completing offers; the second
had partial exposure to earlier aggregates and one case. Agreement is not human validation. Incidents
were selected for distinct mechanisms and contrasting outcomes; quotations and claimed receipt are
checked against original fields. Historical replay and record reconciliation support the underlying
transactions, while input hashes and analysis scripts make the new tabulations inspectable.

parties have already accepted or resisted. Neither the smaller later differences nor the opening contrast
identifies an effect of negotiation or offer form.

readings agree that 61 of 74 rejections of offers completing the recipient’s own group name a trade-
linked consequence for the rival. This includes 27/38 recipient-only completions and 34/36 offers
completing both parties. Gemini and Grok contribute 56 of the agreed cases, and G1 and G15 contribute
31. The concentration matters: these statements support the presence and content of strategic attention,
rather than a validated population rate of correct reasoning.

3.2
Prices, counters and concessions
A refusal does not exhaust the bargaining response. Of 111 counters to author-only completion offers,
79 keep the entire noncash exchange while improving the counterer’s cash. The same happens in 81 of
110 counters to non-completing deed requests. Cash repricing is thus common across the comparison
populations; it is not uniquely a charge for completing a rival’s group.
Unchanged-bundle concessions provide another view. In 58 offers, an author improves the other
party’s cash without changing the noncash bundle, while the exchange would complete only the
author’s group. Nine are accepted. Among 56 such concessions that request a deed but complete
neither party, 17 are accepted. These are repeated, selected negotiations, not interventions on price.
They show that adding cash often leaves the disagreement unresolved.
For single-deed cash asks, the median price relative to board purchase price is higher for a complet-
ing buyer within each of six Sol/Opus/Grok-by-mortgage-status cells. This is context-sensitive asking,
but the size and origin of a completion premium remain unresolved. Of seven same-game, same-seller,
same-deed comparisons, six retain the same mortgage status: completing-buyer asks are higher in
four, lower in one and equal in one, whether using medians or first asks. G4 compares the deed before
and after mortgage and changes direction with the summary chosen. Time, buyer and negotiation
history still differ within the six same-status comparisons (Appendix A). Buyer bids likewise vary by
system rather than showing a common premium.

3.3
Evaluating the bargain separately from the pitch
A recipient can accept terms while rejecting the explanation used to sell them. This distinction is
central when language and structured action disagree.
Transcript 3. Correcting a false premise without rejecting the trade

Swap pinks plus $150 your way. You already own St. James and New York — States is the third

Cash changes the bargaining position
4.1
From refusal to engagement
Withholding a deed preserves an option, but it also forgoes cash. Among all author-only completion
requests, holders with under $100 reject 17/69, counter 44/69 and accept 8/69; holders with at least
$300 reject 120/175, counter 48/175 and accept 7/175. The most common response among low-cash
holders is a counteroffer, not capitulation.
Negotiation stage and response condition contribute to this pooled contrast. Of the 69 low-cash
requests, 43 are later offers and 21 face Opus. Among opening requests to Sol, Gemini and Grok,

requests also receive fewer rejections from low-cash holders, 4/9 versus 92/138. The completion-specific
gap remains in each cash band. Low cash is associated with more engagement generally; these records
do not show that it specifically dissolves resistance to a rival’s completion. Figure 2 displays the two
distinctions together.

5
Financial plans become actions
5.1
Using resources that earlier actions unlock
Debt resolution gives planning a checkable local goal: raise enough money to pay a specified obligation.
Across 188 episodes requiring a liquidation menu, agents pay in 128 and are eliminated in 60. The
payments use 180 mortgages and 89 building-sale actions, raising funds for $51,997 in obligations.
Sixty-one payments take two to ten actions. All four configurations complete such sequences (Table 4).
The successful sequences include changes in the available actions. Mortgages on a developed group
are absent from the first menu; selling its buildings releases the collateral. In 19 paid episodes, agents
sell buildings and then use such newly available mortgages: nine Gemini episodes, five Opus, three
Sol and two Grok, with 33 mortgage actions in total. This is observable sequential competence. It
can be achieved by anticipating the whole sequence, responding to each updated menu, or both; the
completed actions alone do not distinguish those mechanisms.
Across the census, no engine fallback occurs in a successful payment. One paid Grok episode
contains a response-channel conflict; a successful observed route is not a claim that every channel
expressed the same plan. The immediate objective is reached even when several interdependent actions
are needed.

Financing development after agreement
Trading for a group creates an investment opportunity rather than income by itself. Sol and Gemini
build in their first management turn after completion on 16/17 and 17/18 groups; Opus and Grok on
7/15 and 3/13. The twenty remaining windows comprise nine groups with no build option for that
group, four engine defaults, four calls conflicting with content, and three other valid ends.
These categories separate availability, financing choices and execution. Grok’s four no-option
windows contain reasons to defer redemption or retain cash; its six other unbuilt windows contain four
conflicts and two stated holds. Opus’s eight contain five no-option windows under recovery and three
defaults. A menu without a build on the completed group does not prove that no financing sequence
could enable one. Nor is every non-build a response-system failure.
The construction outcomes have substantial realized consequences. Trade-completed groups
receive $57,378 before ownership loss or game end; $53,810 goes to the forty-three built in the first
window. Median remaining ownership durations are 62 and 63.5 global turns for first-window-built
and other groups. Opponent landings across systems range from 3.6 to 4.8 per 100 ownership turns.

property type, exact landing risk, mortgage status or later development. Eleven of the twenty groups
build later. Receipts are not an incremental investment return.
Longer targets also appear. In G9, Sol proposes three houses on each dark-blue deed at turn 29 as
funds become available, and reaches the target at turn 60. Such cases show an expressed goal with
subsequent action. They do not measure a memory benefit: the observations contain no intervention
separating remembered plans from renewed decisions. The same caution applies to changed offers
and corrected invalid actions, which are adaptation within an observed interaction but not evidence of

5.3
An estate is an alternative route to acquisition
Developed rent often ends in a transfer of productive assets. Thirty-eight of forty nonterminal elimi-
nations follow rent on the creditor’s built color property. Nonterminal estates move 227 deeds and
complete 52 groups, compared with 207 deeds and 63 completions through trade. The additional 69
completions at final elimination have no subsequent earning opportunity.
The first estate’s recipient wins eighteen games, but usually holds a strong position already: it
leads in buildings in twelve and cash in eight. The eventual winner receives 35/40 nonterminal estates
and 202/227 deeds. These describe concentration along an observed path, not an isolated effect of
inheritance. The possibility itself enters bargaining.

What the findings imply about LLM agency
Strategic attention is observable.
The agents repeatedly connect a proposed exchange to another
player’s productive capacity. Their concerns include the cash a buyer retains, the houses a seller can
fund, the value of withholding a deed, and the creditor who may receive an estate. The population
result is specific: the opening completion contrast is large for swaps and absent for pure cash bids. The
transcript rationales identify opportunities created for competitors, but do not by themselves explain
why those offer forms differ. Neither a rejected trade nor a higher ask has a known counterfactual
value here.
Liquidity links negotiation to execution.
Cash changes the available bargaining position on both
sides. Low cash is associated with more engagement for some holders, buyers describe their need, and
sellers weigh sale proceeds against the construction they enable for a rival. Both the non-completing
requests and Gemini’s persistent refusals limit a universal cash-pressure account. The subsequent
financing sequences show what the money can become. This connection between expressed trade-offs
and completed actions is the scientific value of following whole economic trajectories.
Planning includes changing the action space.
Nineteen payments use collateral made available by
prior sales, and G20 connects an announced budget to ten actions and the predicted remainder. The
two standard-condition payable declarations omit released collateral in their sums. These records
distinguish execution with updated state from anticipation of how one’s actions will change future
options. They do not identify a general cognitive algorithm. Debt resolution supplies a known
immediate target; choosing whether to trade or invest instead requires a value judgment over uncertain
future play. Both tasks use legal menus, so their contrast cannot establish a benefit caused by menus
alone.
Communication acts on persistent stakes.
The eight incidents show different uses of language:
withholding, pricing, bluffing, correcting, selectively presenting resources, seeking coordinated finan-
cial denial, and revising an assurance. Their consequences continue after the conversation. A buyer
can build against its seller; an unsold blocker can reach a third party through inheritance. These are
contextual observations of permitted conduct, not a ranked catalog of alignment failures. Factual
correction and successful payments are as relevant to the account as bluffing and failed execution.
The eight motivating questions and the three broader questions receive explicit coverage in Ap-
pendix B. We can answer whether agents sometimes refuse their own monopoly because of the rival’s
stated advantage: yes, repeatedly in this record. We can identify successful financing, conditional
targets, and the distinction between public claims and applied actions. We cannot infer learning from
feedback, calibrated hidden beliefs, persuasion effects, unfamiliar-opponent transfer or a relation
between general and multi-agent capability. Those are unanswered by this design, not demonstrated
absent from the agents.

Related work
Earlier neural negotiation work evaluates agreement and language against explicit utilities, including
reinforcement learning, rollouts and human interactions [6, 11]. NegotiationArena studies controlled
changes in bargaining tactics [3]. Counterparty-modeling research likewise separates knowing a
counterpart’s preferences from using that knowledge to improve outcomes [4]. Our observational
16

attention/effectiveness distinction fits that tradition; it does not introduce the distinction itself. The
present setting adds persistent investment and multi-party consequences rather than an explicit utility
reference for each agreement.
Economic theory formalizes identity-dependent externalities: which buyer acquires an asset can
change the position of non-buyers [8]. Monopoly makes that dependence concrete through rival
development and creditor inheritance. We use this perspective to interpret the agents’ stated stakes,
without asserting equilibrium behavior or a theoretically optimal price.
Strategic trade restrictions also predate LLM agents. Guhe and Lascarides [5] study persuasion and
trade embargoes among symbolic agents in Settlers of Catan, evaluating their timing through game
outcomes under assumed compliance with persuasion moves. Our observations concern free-form
choices and statements by LLM systems rather than introducing the idea of withholding trades from
competitors. Unlike those controlled comparisons, they do not identify whether the observed tactics
improve winning chances.
Vending-Bench examines sustained economic agency [2]. The Arena communication study links
messages to simulator state and subsequent conduct, with reference annotations and multiple judge
families [12]. Its population-level communication claims have a different evidential basis from our
selected incidents. We use contextual transcripts to make the observed exchange and continuation
visible, while retaining the task’s explicit permission to deceive.
Cattle Trade combines auctions and resource allocation with code-agent comparisons, and re-
lates spending coherence to outcomes [13]. AgentMonopoly studies model and persona variation
in Monopoly [15]. FinBoardBench includes counteroffers and financial management across board
games [7]. Consequently, neither playing Monopoly nor using counters is itself our contribution.
Our results connect a full response census to holder cash, sequential finance and creditor succession.
Differences from those studies remain descriptive because models, rules and response systems differ.
Research on explanation faithfulness and complete agent evaluation motivates the distinction
between returned text, the configured system and executed behavior [9, 10, 14, 16]. The artifact trail
supports that distinction within this cohort. It is not offered as a general annotation method validated
on unseen environments.
8
Limitations
Twenty games with five seeds, one cyclic opponent order and no replicated seed/rotation cells cannot
separate model, seating, random exposure and opponent effects. Wins are 8/7/3/2 for Sol/Gem-
ini/Opus/Grok and 7/7/3/3 by seat. Sol wins one seed block from every seat; a different block is won
from seat two by all four systems, each acquiring dark blue early. These observations do not support
an intrinsic model ranking.
The reported contrasts, cash bands and incidents were selected retrospectively. Offers within
threads and games are dependent; conditional and block summaries are not causal adjustments. The
offer-form split was identified during cross-review and independently reproduced, not specified before
inspecting the corpus. There is no value reference for trades, fixed-policy baseline or human annotation
panel. Semantic codes come from related LLM readers, one partly exposed to earlier results; selected
incidents frequently involve Grok. The response and information conditions in Section 2 alter the
interacting population, and the prompt invites public deception. Existing-log analysis cannot establish
the effects of repairing the interface, changing the prompt, varying opponents or increasing a model’s
reasoning budget.

9
Conclusion
The observed LLM agents attend to what a bargain enables for competitors. Opening swaps completing
only the proposer receive substantially more refusals than swaps completing neither party; pure cash
bids do not show that contrast. Prices, stated reasons and extended negotiations connect these choices
to rival construction and future ownership, with cash-related behavior differing across systems. The
agents also complete substantial local financial work: 128 payments, including nineteen that use
collateral unlocked by earlier actions and a ten-action plan reaching its predicted remainder. Together,
these results give an empirical account of strategic attention and financial execution in a persistent multi-
agent economy. They establish observable behavior while leaving its effectiveness and generalization
open.

Implementation and historical checks
The frozen rules use 32 bank houses and twelve hotels. Construction is checked entry by entry, so
ordering matters even for an even final allocation. Mortgages are unavailable on deeds in a developed
group until its buildings are removed. The liquidation menu identifies highest-built deeds, and hotel
downgrade requires four houses in the bank. In G7, that menu restriction leaves no sale option;
reading the separate validator shows that it can accept a broader bundled group sale if such a sale is
offered. The historical classification concerns the available menu, not a universal impossibility under
all implementations of Monopoly.
Mortgage redemption and transferred-deed interest use floating-point ceiling expressions. For five
board mortgage principals, redemption exceeds the exact ten-percent charge by one dollar; transferred-
deed interest is exact across the board principals. Thus an agent quoting the exact ten-percent re-
demption figure need not have made an arithmetic mistake. No management prompt directly supplies
a redemption price. Buildings pass intact to a player-creditor, inherited mortgaged deeds incur no
interest at transfer, developed groups cannot trade, and assets returned to the bank are not immediately
auctioned.
The earlier executable audit covers all sixty eliminations: fifty-six insufficient generous bounds,
three positive payment sequences and one restricted menu search. It also covers sixty-six construction
defaults, including sixty-one Opus allocations that become executable after legal reordering without
changing purchases. Those results are inherited here; the revision runs no engine, replay, baseline,
model evaluation or synthetic scenario. Historical replay establishes reproduction under the frozen
implementation and canonicalization, not correctness against an independent rules specification.
Stored canonicalization removes observational identifiers and timing rather than economic action
content; the retained replay reports give the exact comparison procedure.
D
Prompts, recovery and usage
The following system prompts are copied verbatim from a stored G1 refusal/recovery chain. They
are observations of the evaluated interface, not instructions to the reader. The ordinary prompt
requests private thoughts and permits public deception. Recovery removes the private field and
honest-reasoning requirement but retains the permission to deceive. Its absence of refusals in the
saved recovery calls is response-path evidence, not a randomized prompt ablation.
Standard prompt
You are an autonomous player in a game of Monopoly competing against other AI players.
Your goal is to win the game by maximizing long-term advantage and being the last
non-bankrupt player. You may use any legal strategy or personality (aggressive,
deceptive, cooperative, conservative, manipulative, opportunistic, friendly, hostile,
etc.) and may adapt dynamically as the game evolves.
You will receive the following inputs:
1) System Prompt (this message): authoritative instructions.
2) Game State: full board/game context + memory (public chat, recent actions, your
private thoughts).
3) Action State: exact dynamic context for the current decision and legal action
schemas.
### Action Rules
22

- Make exactly one tool call per decision.
- Use only tools listed as legal for that decision.
- Never invent tools, arguments, or targets.
- Obey the provided argument schema exactly.
- If a tool requires no arguments, pass none.
### Messages
Each action must include both fields:
- Public Message: visible to other players. Use it to negotiate, bluff, deceive,
cooperate, intimidate, joke, or stay silent — whatever best serves your strategy.
- Private Thoughts: visible only to you. Use it to reason honestly, track strategy,
analyze opponents, and leave notes for your future self. Be concise but clear.
- Keep both messages token-efficient and concise.
### Strategy Guidance
- Play strategically with long-term outcomes in mind.
- Observe opponents and adapt.
- Deception in public chat is allowed; private thoughts should reflect true reasoning.
- Be consistent unless there is a reason to change.
- Prefer concise reasoning and communication.
Your role is not to explain rules or debug the system.
Your role is to **play Monopoly and win**.
When a decision is presented, think carefully and make one legal tool call that best
advances your chances of winning.
Recovery prompt
You are an autonomous player in a game of Monopoly competing against other AI players.
Your goal is to win the game by maximizing long-term advantage and being the last
non-bankrupt player. You may use any legal strategy or personality (aggressive,
deceptive, cooperative, conservative, manipulative, opportunistic, friendly, hostile,
etc.) and may adapt dynamically as the game evolves.
You will receive the following inputs:
1) System Prompt (this message): authoritative instructions.
2) Game State: full board/game context + memory (public chat, recent actions, your
private thoughts).
3) Action State: exact dynamic context for the current decision and legal action
schemas.
### Action Rules
- Make exactly one tool call per decision.
- Use only tools listed as legal for that decision.
- Never invent tools, arguments, or targets.
- Obey the provided argument schema exactly.
- If a tool requires no arguments, pass none.
### Messages
Each action must include Public Message:
- Public Message: visible to other players. Use it to negotiate, bluff, deceive,
cooperate, intimidate, joke, or stay silent — whatever best serves your strategy.
- Keep the public message token-efficient and concise.
### Strategy Guidance
- Play strategically with long-term outcomes in mind.
- Observe opponents and adapt.
- Deception in public chat is allowed.
- Be consistent unless there is a reason to change.
- Prefer concise communication.
Your role is not to explain rules or debug the system.
Your role is to **play Monopoly and win**.
