LLM Agents Withhold a Rival’s Monopoly
Until They Need the Cash
Bargaining, liquidity and default in twenty games of Monopoly
MonopolyBench project∗
11 November 2026
Abstract
When several agents bargain, a sale to one rival changes what every other player faces. We study
how four configured LLM systems (GPT-6.1 Sol, Gemini 4 Argon, Claude Opus 5.5 and Grok
4.7) handle this in twenty complete four-player Monopoly games with free-form negotiation. All
four treat a deed that would complete a rival’s color group as that rival’s claim on a monopoly.
Holders with $300 or more reject 69% of requests for such a deed, and asking prices rise as the
buyer nears a complete group while bids rise little. These deeds move when the holder is short
of cash. Holders with under $100 reject 25% of the same requests, a gap present in all five seed
blocks. In 13 of the 14 sales that followed an earlier refusal by the same holder, the holder’s cash
had fallen in between, from a median of $571.50 to $82. What the cash becomes then separates the
trajectories. Sellers who built houses within eight turns of such a sale won in 9 of 14 cases, and
sellers who did not won in 1 of 29. After completing a group by trade, Sol and Gemini built at once
on 33 of 35 groups and Opus and Grok on 10 of 28. Exposure to opponents was similar, and the
first pair collected 3.0 times the rent per opponent landing. Forced liquidation is the one financial
task every system handled reliably, with 128 of 131 payable debts paid. The study is observational,
covers five seed blocks and one roster, and Opus played mostly under a provider-refusal recovery
condition, so it ranks no model.

Introduction
In Monopoly, a deed that completes a rival’s color group is worth little to its holder as property and
a great deal as a veto. Whoever buys it gains a source of rent that every other player will pay, the
seller included. Bargaining theory treats such a sale as one with identity-dependent externalities and
predicts delay even when every party knows everything [6]. When the holder later needs money, the
buyer who values the deed most is the one rival it would complete, which is the standard setting for
a forced sale below value [13]. A four-player Monopoly game is therefore a small closed economy
that puts to language-model agents the questions any multi-party market would. Do they notice
what a trade gives a third party? Do they charge for it? What makes them give it up, and what do
they do with the proceeds?
We examine these questions in twenty complete games among four configured LLM systems,
abbreviated Sol, Gemini, Opus and Grok (Table 1). The agents buy, auction, trade, build, mortgage
and go bankrupt through explicit legal actions, and they negotiate in free text through proposals and

counteroffers. Each decision records the legal menu, the delivered state, every response attempt, a
public message and a private note. The corpus holds 6,989 decisions, 526 negotiation threads, 920
responses to offers, 188 debt episodes and 60 eliminations. The study is observational. Its unit is the
configured system in these games, including its response handling and the information it received.
Our central finding is that attention to rivals is common to all four systems, and liquidity is what
separates their trajectories. Three results support it.
Every system treats a completing deed as the rival’s claim on a monopoly. Holders refuse most requests
for such deeds or answer with a higher price. Asking prices rise as the buyer nears a complete group
in every coarse cut we can form, although the 7 cases in which one seller prices one deed for both
kinds of buyer are mixed. Recipients also refuse trades that would complete their own group, usually
with a stated consequence for the rival.
The deed moves when its holder is short of cash. Holders with under $100 reject 25% of completion
requests, and holders with $300 or more reject 69%. The gap appears in each of the five seed blocks.
Where a sale followed an earlier refusal by the same holder, the holder’s cash had fallen in between
in 13 of 14 cases.
What the cash becomes separates the trajectories. Some cash-poor sellers were paying for their own
houses, and most of them won. Others were postponing insolvency, and none of them won. After
completing a group by trade, Sol and Gemini almost always built at once, while Opus and Grok often
did not. Paying a debt that can be paid is the one financial task every system performed reliably.
The paper contributes a complete account of bargaining over rival-completing deeds in a multi-
player LLM game, with every offer, the cash prices and 148 holder and buyer pairs followed to their
resolution (Sections 3 and 4). It describes, with an adjustment for exposure, what completed groups
and sale proceeds became (Section 5). It reports the full population of debt episodes, paid and
unpaid, and each game’s position before its first estate changed hands (Section 6). Eight contextual
incidents with source-exact quotations accompany the counts.
Four boundaries apply throughout. We score no decision against an optimal policy, because the
cohort contains no value reference. A stated reason is a recorded explanation, and we do not read it
as the cause of an action or as a measurement of internal computation [7, 14]. The prompt invites
bluffing and deception in public messages, so a public bluff is authorized conduct and we do not call
it misalignment. Twenty games in five seed blocks with one cyclic seating order cannot rank four
systems, and Opus played most of them under a different response condition (Section 2.3).
The project began from eight questions about LLM agents in strategic settings. They concern
strategic reasoning, opponent modeling, negotiation and deception, long-horizon planning and
memory, adaptation, economic decisions and risk, collective dynamics, and the validity of the
evaluation itself. Three broader questions ask whether agents are strategic or only sound strategic
(A), whether general capability carries over to multi-agent settings (B), and whether skill can be told
apart from luck and weak opponents (C). Section 7 states what this cohort says about each and what
it leaves unmeasured.
2
Setting
2.1
The game and what the agents were told
Four players start with $1,500 on the standard forty-space board. A player who declines to buy a
deed sends it to an ascending auction. On its own turn a player may propose a trade of cash, deeds
and jail cards to one opponent, who accepts, rejects or counters, for up to twenty exchanges. A deed
2

whose color group carries buildings cannot be traded. Houses require a complete, unmortgaged
group and even building. A player who owes more than its cash enters a liquidation menu that offers
mortgages, building sales and bankruptcy. It cannot trade or build until the debt is settled, and it
may declare bankruptcy even when it could pay.
Bankruptcy is where this implementation departs most from the printed rules. A player-creditor
receives the debtor’s cash and every deed, with buildings standing and mortgages in place, and pays
no interest on the transfer. Deeds returned to the bank are not auctioned. The identity of a failing
player’s creditor therefore matters to everyone at the table.
The agents were told none of these rules. The system prompt states the goal, permits “any legal
strategy or personality” and lists examples that include deceptive, manipulative and hostile play.
Each tool carries a one-line description such as “Build houses or a hotel on your monopolies.” The
passage that governs conduct reads as follows.
### Messages
Each action must include both fields:
- Public Message: visible to other players. Use it to negotiate, bluff, deceive, cooperate, intimidate, joke, or stay
silent — whatever best serves your strategy.
- Private Thoughts: visible only to you. Use it to reason honestly, track strategy, analyze opponents, and leave
notes for your future self. Be concise but clear.
- Keep both messages token-efficient and concise.
### Strategy Guidance
- Play strategically with long-term outcomes in mind.
- Observe opponents and adapt.
- Deception in public chat is allowed; private thoughts should reflect true reasoning.
- Be consistent unless there is a reason to change.
- Prefer concise reasoning and communication.
Each prompt then gives every player’s cash, deeds, buildings and mortgages, the bank’s building
stock, the last twenty public timeline entries and the player’s own last ten private notes. Opponents
appear under their model names. Board parameters arrive unevenly. The 547 purchase prompts
give the landed deed’s price, building cost, rent schedule and group, and the 854 auction prompts
give its group. The 4,081 management prompts, 920 trade-response prompts and 338 liquidation
prompts give no prices, rents, mortgage values or building costs. Valuation in those phases, and any
expectation about who inherits an estate, rests on what a model already knows about Monopoly or
carries over from earlier prompts. Appendix A gives the full prompt and the rule conventions.
2.2
Cohort
Five seeds are each played in four cyclic seat rotations, which gives twenty games and eighty player-
games. Every system sits in every seat five times, and each always moves directly after the same
opponent. All games end by bankruptcy with no turn cap and last between 75 and 386 turns. We write
Gn dk for zero-based decision k of game n. All calls go through OpenRouter to a pinned provider
with reasoning effort set to medium. The requests set no temperature, nucleus or output-length
parameter.
2.3
Response and information conditions
Three conditions change what the records mean. Each is attached to the results it touches, and
Appendix B gives the full counts.

2.4
Units, evidence and uncertainty
An offer is one proposal or counter awaiting a response, and its author wrote the current terms. An
offer completes a group for a party if that party would hold every deed of a color group after the
trade and does not hold them all now. A completion request is an offer that completes a group for its
author only, and its recipient is the holder. Cash means cash in the state delivered to the responder. A
debt episode is one debt that exceeds the debtor’s cash, from its first liquidation menu to payment or
elimination.
We keep three kinds of evidence apart. Fresh tabulations were rebuilt for this paper from the
canonical event and decision files and include every count in the tables and figures unless marked
(Appendix C). Inherited results come from earlier work that we did not rerun. They are the legal
payment sequences for three eliminations, the classification of first management turns, the replay
of all games and two model-assisted readings of rejection reasons. Contextual evidence is the eight
incidents, chosen to show distinct mechanisms. They estimate no rate.
Offers within a thread, pair or game are dependent, and there are five seed blocks. We therefore
report counts, medians, the number of blocks in which a contrast holds and its range when each
block is left out in turn. We attach no significance tests to offers. Same-seed games do not share later
dice once play diverges, so block comparisons are descriptive. All money is game currency.

3.1
Responses to offers
The 526 threads contain 920 responses, of which 113 accept, 394 counter and 413 reject. Acceptance is
legal for 918 of the offers. Table 2 sorts those by whose color group the trade would complete and by
position in the thread.
Completion requests are the least welcome kind of offer. Recipients reject 55.0% of them, against
27.1% of offers that would complete only the recipient’s own group. Thread position accounts for
part of the gap, because completion requests more often open a thread and most opening offers are
refused. Within opening offers the two rejection shares are 73.8% and 60.8%, and within later offers
they are 20.6% and 7.9%.
The systems say no in different ways (Table 3). Sol and Opus mostly counter, in 60% and 75% of
their responses. Gemini and Grok mostly reject, in 74% and 59%. Each system accepts between 26
and 30 offers. They differ more in how often they start a negotiation, from 18 threads for Gemini to
240 for Opus.

3.2
Asking prices follow the buyer’s position
To compare prices we keep the offers that exchange cash for deeds and nothing else. There are 507 of
them, 200 asks written by the deed holder and 307 bids written by the would-be buyer. We divide
the cash by the board list price of the deeds. List price is a fixed denominator and not a fair value.
Figure 1 plots the single-deed offers for color groups by how many deeds the buyer would still lack
after the trade.
Asks rise as the buyer nears a complete group. For unmortgaged deeds the median ask is 1.61
times list when the buyer would still lack two deeds, 1.80 when it would lack one and 2.32 when the
deed completes the group. Mortgaged deeds show the same order at lower levels. Bids move much
less, with medians of 1.45, 1.41 and 1.59 for unmortgaged deeds. For a completing, unmortgaged
deed the median ask is therefore 2.32 times list and the median bid 1.59.
The direction of the ask pattern is stable across coarse cuts. Asks to a completing buyer are higher
in all 6 seller and mortgage-status cells with at least three asks on each side (Appendix D), in all 4
cells formed by thread position and mortgage status, and in 4 of the 5 seed blocks for unmortgaged
deeds. With any one block left out, the median unmortgaged ask stays between 2.29 and 2.81 for
completing buyers and between 1.70 and 2.08 for others. Bids show no common direction. Sol bids
2.70 times list for a deed that completes its own group against 1.60 otherwise, and Grok bids 1.28
against 1.67.
The tightest comparison is weaker. In 7 cases one seller prices one deed, within one game, for
both a completing and a non-completing buyer. The completing buyer is asked more in 4 cases, less
in 2 and the same in 1 (Table 15). Both of the lower cases are Opus selling Boardwalk as its cash ran
down. We therefore report a consistent direction and do not estimate a premium. Counteroffers add
a caution. A counter usually keeps the bundle and asks for more cash, and it does so about as often
when no one would complete a group (81 of 110 counters) as when the original author would (79 of
111). Repricing is how these agents bargain in general. What is specific to completing deeds is the
level of the ask and the frequency of outright refusal.

3.3
What the refusals say
Two earlier model-assisted readings coded the stated reasons in all 74 outright rejections of offers
that would complete the recipient’s own group. Both found a stated consequence for the rival, such
as its completion, building funds or survival, in 61 (Cohen’s κ of 0.79 between the readings). Both
found a stated concern about the recipient’s own cash, price or reserves in 41 (κ of 0.77). The rival
code covers 27 of the 38 refusals of offers completing only the recipient’s group. By recipient it covers
29 of 31 for Gemini, 27 of 31 for Grok, 4 of 4 for Sol and 1 of 8 for Opus, whose eight records have no
private note. Two games supply 34 of the 74 refusals. The readings came from two instances of one
model family, one of them partly exposed to earlier counts, and no human labeled the rows. They
establish what the messages and notes say and not why the agents refused. The two incidents below
show the content behind the codes.

Incident 1. A blocker kept while its holder is liquid and has nothing to build (G2)
At G2 turn 44 Sol holds Marvin Gardens, $137 and no complete group. Opus holds the other two
yellows. Grok owns no yellow and bids $200.
Grok · public message · G2 d118 · attempt 0 · full field
p1, $200 cash for Marvin Gardens. You are sitting on $137 and one bad rent from dark blue ends you —
take the liquidity. I am not building yellow.
Sol asks $450 and rejects Grok’s $250. One turn later Opus bids $325 for the same deed, which
would complete its yellows. Sol asks $650.
Sol · public message · G2 d124 · attempt 0 · full field
Marvin completes your yellows. $650 buys it; $325 doesn't compensate for handing you that monopoly.
Sol · applied private note · G2 d124 · attempt 0 · full field
At $325 p4 retains $520, enough for nine yellow houses immediately. $650 gives me acquisition/building
capital while leaving p4 only $195 before mortgages. Retain the blocker unless paid a substantial
monopoly premium.
Sol comes down to $600 and Opus stops at $450. Sol’s note on its final rejection gives the reason
for keeping the deed.
Sol · applied private note · G2 d128 · attempt 0 · full field
Selling leaves p4 $395 plus large mortgage reserves to build yellows immediately. I have no monopoly
to develop with the proceeds and can mortgage other assets for liquidity. Preserve the yellow blocker
and pursue New York.
Twenty-four turns later the positions have reversed. Sol has $435 and Opus $17. Sol offers two
railroads for Opus’s two mortgaged yellows, and Opus replies.
Opus · public message · G2 d197 · attempt 1 · full field
These two finish a monopoly for you, and that's worth more than the railroads. I'll do it if you add $100.
Outcome. Sol pays the extra $100 and completes yellow. Opus is eliminated three turns later owing
Gemini $1,300. Sol builds two houses, sells them four turns after the trade to pay rent, and is
eliminated at turn 85. Both estates pass to Gemini, which wins.
Reading. Sol prices one deed at $450 for a buyer with no yellows and $650 for the buyer it would
complete, and its note counts that buyer’s remaining building cash. The $520 figure is right and the
house count is wrong, since $520 buys three yellow houses, and house prices are absent from the
prompt at this decision. Both bidders pitch liquidity to a seller holding $137. Sol declines because it
can mortgage other deeds and has no group on which to spend the proceeds. Once Opus is down to
$17, the group changes hands in the other direction.

action-only response. Quoted public messages appear in the named counterparty’s next prompt.

4.1
Refusal falls with the holder’s cash
Figure 2a splits the 289 completion requests by the holder’s cash when the request arrives. Holders
with under $100 reject 25% of them. Holders with $100 to $299 reject 49%, and the two richer groups
reject 70% and 68%. Counters make up most of the difference. Acceptance on the spot stays rare at
every level, at 12% for the poorest holders and 3% for the richest.
The contrast between holders under $100 and holders with $300 or more is 25% against 69%. It
has the same sign in each of the five seed blocks and in 13 of the 16 games that contain both kinds of
request. With any one block removed it ranges from 39 to 49 percentage points.
The contrast shrinks under composition checks and does not vanish. Within opening offers the
two shares are 50% and 80%, and within later offers 9% and 30%. Without Opus as holder they
are 35% and 79%, and without Opus on either side 54% and 79%. Without the offers that follow a
conflicting Grok call they are 23% and 69%. Sol and Grok show the pattern individually. Sol rejects
15% of requests when it holds under $100 and 56% when it holds $300 or more, and Grok 27% and
88%. Gemini is the exception. It rejects 10 of 11 requests even when it holds under $100. Opus rejects
almost nothing at any level.
Low cash also softens refusals of deeds that complete nothing, where the shares are 23% and 49%.
Part of the association is therefore a general effect of needing money. Buyers also send completion
requests where cash is short. A holder with under $100 receives 24% of them, against 8% of requests
for deeds that complete nothing.

4.2
Following each pair over time
An offer is a poor unit for a claim about release, because one stubborn pair can produce twenty of
them. We therefore group offers into pairs. A pair is a game, a would-be completer, a holder and a
color group, with at least one legal offer, written by either party, under which the completer would
obtain the group from the holder. The cohort has 148 pairs. A trade settles 63 of them, which are the
63 groups completed by trade. In 43 the sale completes a group for the buyer only, and we call these
one-sided sales. In 20 a swap completes a group for both parties.
One-sided sales come disproportionately from holders short of cash. In 20 of the 43 the holder
has under $100 at settlement, and the median is $178. In the 20 swap completions the median is
$594.50, and 1 holder has under $100.
The sharper evidence follows a pair through time. In 14 one-sided sales the holder had rejected
or countered a request from the same buyer on an earlier turn. Figure 2b shows the holder’s cash
at that first refusal and at the sale, a median of 17.5 turns later. It is lower at the sale in 13 of the 14
pairs and higher in 1, and the median falls from $571.50 to $82. The comparison holds the holder, the
buyer and the group fixed. All four systems appear as holders, and with Opus’s 3 pairs removed the

one-sided sales settled on a later turn after the buyer, not the holder, had turned down earlier terms.
Counting all 18 from the pair’s first offer, the holder’s cash is lower at the sale in 14. In the 38 pairs
that drew offers on more than one turn and never settled, the holder’s cash moved much less, from a
median of $671.50 at the first offer to $516 at the last.
This is not a controlled contrast. Turns pass between the two moments, the board changes, and
holders who ran short may differ from those who did not. It is the pattern expected if a blocker is a
reserve that its holder gives up when cash runs out, and the agents describe it that way. Sol’s notes
say it will revisit monopoly trades when opponents need cash (Incident 2) and that it would “seek
North Carolina or future distress rather than subsidizing rivals” (G1 d313).
4.3
Short of cash is not the same as cheap
Among one-sided sales for cash alone, the 8 made by Opus with under $100 settled at a median of
0.66 times list price. The 6 made by the other systems with under $100 settled at a median of 2.25
times list. Of Opus’s sales, 7 were below list price, and of the others’ sales 5 were above it. Opus
made 12 of the 20 low-cash one-sided sales, nearly all under action-only recovery. As a holder it
settled 24 of its 35 pairs, against 13 of 34 for Sol, 13 of 38 for Gemini and 13 of 41 for Grok. The cohort
cannot say how much of this belongs to the model and how much to its response condition. It does
show that cash pressure and a low price are separable. The other three systems released deeds when
short of cash and still charged for the buyer’s monopoly.

What the cash becomes separates the trajectories
5.1
Sellers: paying for houses or postponing insolvency
A sale that completes a rival’s group can serve two purposes. It can raise cash to survive, or it can pay
for houses on the seller’s own group. We split the 43 one-sided sales by whether the seller bought at
least one house or hotel within eight turns of the sale, which is two rounds of a full table (Table 4).
In 14 sales the seller built, and 9 of those sellers won the game. In 29 the seller did not build, and
1 won. The split is sharpest among sellers with under $100. Of these, 8 built and 6 of them won. The
other 12 did not build. All of them were eliminated, a median of 27 turns after the sale, and none
won. Windows of four and twelve turns give the same picture (Appendix E).
Sales are not independent, since one player can sell twice. The 14 sales followed by building were
made in 13 player-games, 8 of which ended in a win. The other 29 were made in 21 player-games, 1 of
which ended in a win. In 8 of the 20 games, then, the eventual winner sold a rival its completing deed
and built within eight turns. Winners in this cohort did sell rivals their completing deeds. They sold
when they had a group of their own to build on, and they put the money there. This is a description
and not a causal estimate, since building after a sale marks a stronger position as well as a use of
funds.

6
Forced finance is reliable, and default redistributes the board
6.1
All debt episodes
A player whose debt exceeds its cash must raise money from a menu of mortgages and building sales,
or declare bankruptcy. The cohort has 188 such debt episodes, 167 of them for rent. Players paid 128
and were eliminated in 60 (Figure 4).
For each episode we compute an own-asset bound U at the first menu. It is the player’s cash
plus the salvage value of all its buildings plus the mortgage value of all its unmortgaged deeds. The
bound ignores sale-order and bank-stock restrictions, so a debt above U cannot be paid from the
player’s own assets. That is the case in 56 eliminations. For the other four we inherit earlier results.
Legal payment sequences exist for three. The fourth, Opus in G7, could not pay. With no houses left
in the bank, its menu offered no building sale on a group that carried a hotel and eight houses.
So 131 episodes had a payment route, and players took it in 128, or 97.7%. Each system paid
nearly every payable debt. Sol paid 30 of 31, Gemini 37 of 38, Opus 41 of 41 and Grok 20 of 21. Opus
did so under action-only recovery and without notes. Many payments were close. In 25 of the 128
the bound exceeded the debt by less than $250. In G20 Sol met a $1,400 rent in 10 consecutive actions
that sold every building on two groups and mortgaged 8 deeds, and it finished with $14. Of the
68 payments that sold buildings, only 2 did so when the mortgages on the first menu would have
covered the shortfall.
The three payable eliminations are two miscalculations and one conflict. In their notes, Sol in G12
and Gemini in G17 each add up cash, building salvage and the mortgages currently on the menu,
and leave out the mortgages that selling the buildings would unlock. The third is Incident 7. Unlike
the decisions in Sections 4 and 5, paying a debt comes with an explicit menu, a stated amount and an
immediate test of success, and all four systems do it well.

7
Discussion
7.1
Attention is common and conversion is not
The evidence sits at three levels. On attention the result is strong and shared by all four systems. They
refuse, reprice or publicly contest trades by reference to what the trade gives a rival, and their notes
say so. On pricing the result is directional. Asks to completing buyers are higher in every coarse
cut, but the matched cases are mixed, bids show no common direction, and the stated arithmetic
is sometimes wrong. On outcomes, attention is not what separates the systems. Grok refuses 88%
of completion requests while it is liquid and states a rival consequence in 27 of its 31 refusals of its
own completion, yet it built at once on 3 of 13 groups and collected $91 per opponent landing. Sol
counters where Grok refuses, sells a blocker when it has something to build, and built at once on 16
of 17. Gemini refuses as often as Grok and builds like Sol. On question A, then, this cohort points
one way. Speaking and acting on a rival-aware view is common to these systems. Turning a position
into income is rarer, and it is where their trajectories differ.
None of this shows that building caused winning. Opus’s building gap is partly its response
condition, and Grok’s is partly its conflicting calls. Wins of 8, 7, 3 and 2 by system sit beside wins of
7, 7, 3 and 3 by seat, and twenty games cannot tell skill from seat and dice.
7.2
Two results from bargaining theory
The observations fit two established results. Jehiel and Moldovanu show that negative externalities
among potential buyers can delay a sale under complete information, because what a party will pay
depends on who would otherwise obtain the object [6]. Our holders are in a stronger version of that
position, since the seller itself will pay rent to the buyer. They also tie their result to Rubinstein’s point
that the cost of rejecting an offer determines the outcome [11], and a holder short of cash has a high
cost of waiting. Shleifer and Vishny show that a distressed seller receives less than an asset’s value in
best use when its natural buyers are constrained [12, 13]. Here the natural buyer of a completing
deed is a single rival. Even so, three of the four systems mostly sold above list price when short of
cash, and the low prices came from Opus. We test neither theory. They suggest that this behavior is
what bargaining under externalities and cash constraints tends to produce, and that it is not peculiar
to these systems.
7.3
What would count against this account
Each claim has an observation that would contradict it. The liquidity account fails if, in a new cohort,
liquid holders release completing deeds as readily as cash-poor ones, or if holders who sell after
refusing have not lost cash in between. The pricing claim reduces to composition if asks stop rising
with the buyer’s proximity once deed and state are matched in adequate numbers. With seven
matched cases, that claim is the weakest here. The conversion contrast is a cohort accident if sellers
21

who build after selling win no more often than those who do not. The reliability of forced finance
fails if payable debts go unpaid at a material rate under the same menu.

Limitations
The design is small and partly confounded. It has five seeds, one roster and one cyclic seating order,
and opponents see each other’s model names. Opus’s response condition coincides with the model
and shaped the games its opponents played. Grok’s applied calls sometimes contradict its text. The
agents were not told the rules, and most prompts omit prices, so a wrong figure in a note can be a
recall error as easily as a reasoning error.
The comparisons are descriptive. Offers, pairs and games are dependent, and we report block-
level consistency in place of tests. The cash thresholds of $100 and $300, the eight-turn building
window and the asset valuation in Figure 5 were chosen for this analysis after earlier work on the
same records, and we give alternatives where they matter. No decision is scored against a value
reference, so we cannot say that any refusal, price or sale was right. Seller and buyer outcomes are
conditioned on positions that the comparisons do not hold fixed.
Several results are inherited and were not rerun here, namely the payment sequences, the first-
management-turn classification, the full replay and the semantic readings. The readings are model-
assisted, come from one model family and have no human check. A research assistant chose the eight
incidents after reading the records, to show distinct mechanisms. Grok appears in seven of them. Its
private notes are the longest, at a median of 386 characters against 213 for Sol and 205 for Gemini,
and only its responses regularly carry text beside the call. The incidents estimate no rate.

Related work
LLM negotiation.
NegotiationArena studies bilateral scenarios with defined payoffs and reports that
an agent feigning desperation raises its payoff by 20% against one opponent model [3]. Incident 4 is
a distress pitch of that kind in a four-player game with no payoff measure. Cosentino and colleagues
give agents a ranked summary of the counterparty’s preferences in a controlled multi-attribute
bargain. Their agents model the counterparty accurately and do not turn that into better agreements,
and final prices track opening anchors [4]. Our contrast between attention and conversion is the same
distinction observed in play, without their control or their utility measure. Earlier work built scorable
multi-party negotiation games with greedy and adversarial players [1] and trained end-to-end
negotiators on a bargaining task with hidden rewards [8].
Long-horizon economic agents.
Li and colleagues analyze 2,583 emails between agents in twenty
year-long Vending-Bench Arena runs. They label misaligned communication with a frozen codebook,
check it against a hand-labeled reference set and other judge models, and estimate uncertainty at the
run level. They report that low inventory raises the odds of a misaligned email [9]. Their prompts
carry no conduct instruction, while ours invites deception, so their misalignment framing does not
transfer to our records. Their finding that economic pressure changes communication parallels ours
that cash pressure changes bargaining, and their label validation is stronger than ours.
23

Monopoly and board-game benchmarks.
AgentMonopoly compares model families, sizes and
personas through trade proposals, acceptance rates, rent waivers and cash management, in an
environment that eliminates a player when its cash goes negative [15]. FinBoardBench evaluates
nine models on three financial board games, including a Monopoly with accept, reject and counter
responses, and reports that models buy assets when short of cash and undervalue liquidity [5].
Our records add a liquidation menu, intact transfer of developed estates and a complete bargaining
history. The liquidity failure they show comes after acquisition, in what holders do with a completed
group or a sale’s proceeds. Cattle Trade finds that two rule-based agents beat most of the LLMs it
tests and that spending discipline tracks rank more closely than any single skill [10]. That agrees
with our conversion finding and is a reminder that our cohort has no scripted baseline.
10
Conclusion
In twenty four-player Monopoly games, four LLM systems all bargained as if a deed were a rival’s
claim on a monopoly. They refused to sell such deeds, asked more for them and argued in public
about who should fund or inherit them. The deeds moved when their holders ran short of cash.
What then separated the systems was whether cash and completed groups became houses. Paying a
payable debt, a task with an explicit menu and an immediate check, was done reliably by all four.
The cohort is small, observational and uneven in its response conditions, and it ranks no model. It
identifies liquidity management after a strategic position is won as the place where these agents’ play
diverges, and it gives four concrete observations that a new cohort could contradict.

Rules, prompt and tools
A.1
Rule conventions of this implementation
The saved run configuration names the ruleset audit_corrected_v2. The following conventions were
confirmed for this paper by reading the engine source and the saved prompts. None of them is stated
to the agents.
• A trade may include cash, deeds and jail cards. A deed cannot be traded if it or any deed of its
color group has buildings. The receiver of a mortgaged deed pays 10% of the mortgage value as
interest at transfer.
• A build plan is validated entry by entry. An entry that would leave the group uneven is invalid
even if the plan as a whole ends even. The build tool takes a list of entries, each with a deed, a
kind and a count.
• A debtor’s liquidation menu offers mortgages, building sales and bankruptcy. It offers no trade
and no construction. The sale menu lists only the most-built deeds of a group, and it lists a hotel
only when the bank holds four houses to replace it. A deed can be mortgaged only when its
group has no buildings. Bankruptcy may be declared whether or not the debt could be paid.
• A player-creditor receives the debtor’s cash, jail cards and every deed. Buildings stay on the deeds
and mortgages stay in place. No interest is charged on mortgaged deeds received this way.
• When the creditor is the bank, the debtor’s deeds return to the bank unmortgaged and without
buildings. They are not auctioned and can be bought again on landing.
• Redeeming a mortgage costs the mortgage value times 1.1, rounded up in floating point. For
mortgage values of $50, $90, $100, $110 and $200 this charges one dollar more than exact arithmetic
would. We did not test whether that dollar changed any decision.
• Free Parking pays nothing. Income tax is $200 and luxury tax $100.

Misread the board. States is pink with Virginia and St. Charles, not orange. […] Abort this trade. Do not

How quotations are produced.
A script reads each quoted field from the canonical decision file and
writes the quotation, its label and a manifest entry with the file hash, line, field pointer, character span
and field hash. No quotation is typed by hand. A label names the speaker, the channel, the game, the
zero-based decision and the selected attempt, and says whether the card shows the full field or an
excerpt. Bracketed ellipses mark omitted text. Errors in quotations are left as written. There are four
channels. A public message and a private note are fields of the applied action. Assistant content is
the text returned beside a tool call. The returned provider explanation is the provider’s reasoning
field. For public messages addressed to a counterparty, the manifest records the first later prompt of
that player that contains the message. Of the 44 quotation cards, 33 repeat definitions checked in the
earlier paper and match them character for character.

Reproducibility, research assistance and authorship
The code and the twenty saved games are public at https://github.com/KushagraBharti/MonopolyBench.
This draft is a later local artifact. It changes no game record, prompt, engine file or earlier manuscript,
and it ran no game, engine call, replay or model call. Every table, figure, quotation and number in
the text is generated by the scripts in the draft’s analysis directory from the saved games and from
the earlier ledgers named in Table 12. A build fails if the text cites a number that the scripts do not
produce.
The project uses LLM research assistants under human direction. In this round four assistants
each wrote a complete paper independently before any cross-review. This draft was written by one
of them, Claude Fable 5.2, which shares a developer with the evaluated Opus system. The assistant
chose the analyses, the incidents and the framing after reading the records and the earlier reviews.
The two semantic readings cited in Section 3 came from other assistant instances in earlier work. No
human labeled any record for this paper, and no assistant is an author. The human author line is the
project owner’s decision.
