Guarding the Last Deed:
Defensive Bargaining by LLM Agents in Twenty Games of
Monopoly
MonopolyBench project
Independent draft by the Opus research assistant; author list to be set by the project owner
11 November 2026
Abstract
When four language-model agents trade property in Monopoly, the value of a deed depends on
who would own the rest of its colour group. We study how agents bargain over such deeds in a
fixed cohort of twenty complete four-player games (6,989 decisions; 920 responses to trade offers)
played by four configured systems through one gateway. Every offer is rebuilt from the request its
recipient received. The most consistent pattern is defensive. Opening offers that would complete
only the proposer’s group are rejected more often than offers that complete nobody (74% against
64%); the gap is positive in all five seed blocks and for each of the three systems responding under
the standard condition. Cash concessions on an unchanged bundle are accepted less often when
the bundle would complete the proposer’s group (9 of 58 against 24 of 86). When agents refuse
offers that would complete their own group, two LLM-assisted readings find a stated consequence
for the counterparty in 61 of 74 refusals. Asking prices are higher for buyers whom the deed would
complete, but this varies across seed blocks and same-deed comparisons, and buyers do not bid
consistently more. Responses also do not change with the proposer’s post-trade building cash, the
quantity agents cite most. Completing deeds change hands mostly when the holder is short of
cash (14 of 22 such cash sales). The completed groups then earn rent once built, and each game’s
first bankruptcy estate usually goes to a player who already leads. One system played almost all
decisions under a provider-refusal recovery without private notes; its bargaining is not defensive
and is reported separately. The study is observational. It describes what these agents attended to
and did, not whether their bargaining was good play.
1
Introduction
In a four-player property game, a trade between two players also changes the position of the two
who are not at the table, and it changes what the counterparty can do next. Monopoly makes this
concrete. A street deed earns little rent on its own, but the player who owns every deed of a colour
group may build houses, and houses multiply the rent. The last missing deed of a group is therefore
worth far more to the player who holds the other deeds than its board price suggests. It is also worth
more to everyone else, as something to withhold. Economists describe goods of this kind as carrying
identity-dependent externalities: the holder cares who acquires the object, because the acquirer’s later
behaviour affects the holder [6].
This paper asks how large language model (LLM) agents bargain over such goods when they play
complete games against one another. Recent work shows that LLM negotiators can model what a
counterparty wants without turning that model into better agreements [4], and that agents’ messages in
1

long multi-agent simulations can be checked against the simulator’s state [9]. A multi-player property
game adds a different demand. A competent player must reason about what an exchange enables for a
rival over later turns, and must weigh that against what the exchange does for itself. We study whether
the agents attend to that consequence, and what form their attention takes in offers, responses and
prices.
The evidence is a fixed cohort of twenty complete four-player games played by four configured
systems, which we call Sol, Gemini, Opus and Grok (Section 2). The cohort contains 6,989 decisions,
526 negotiation threads and 920 responses to offers. Every action was chosen from a legal menu
generated by a deterministic engine, and every prompt, response and event was saved. We rebuilt
every offer from the request that its recipient actually received, so each response can be related to the
holdings, cash and terms the responding agent saw.
Our central finding is that the agents bargain defensively over the deed that would complete a
rival’s group. Opening offers that would complete only the proposer’s group are rejected in 138 of
187 cases (74%), against 152 of 238 (64%) for opening offers that complete no group. The difference
is positive in each of the five seed blocks and for each of the three systems that respond under the
standard response condition. When a proposer repeats the same deeds with more cash, the concession
is accepted in 9 of 58 cases if the deal would complete the proposer’s group, against 24 of 86 if it
would complete nobody; recipients counter instead. In most refusals of offers that would complete the
recipient’s own group, the refusing agent’s text names what the counterparty would gain (61 of 74 by
two LLM-assisted readings).
The pattern has clear limits. Asking prices are higher when the deed would complete the buyer’s
group (median 1.63 against 1.08 times the board price), but the ordering holds in only three of five
seed blocks, and comparisons of one seller pricing one deed to different buyers are mixed. Buyers
do not bid consistently more for deeds that would complete their own groups. Responses also do
not change with the proposer’s post-trade building cash, which is the quantity the agents most often
cite when they refuse. The agents’ attention to rival completion is therefore visible as a category that
changes their responses, not as a graded valuation of the amounts they discuss.
Completing deeds still change hands, mostly when the holder needs cash. In 14 of 22 settled cash
sales of a deed that completed the buyer’s group, the seller held less than $100 before the sale, compared
with 7 of 31 other cash sales. The groups assembled this way then pass through development, rent
and bankruptcy. Developed groups collect almost all post-completion rent. The first bankruptcy estate
of a game goes to the existing asset leader in 15 of 20 games, and the first creditor goes on to win 18 of
the 20.
One configuration needs separate treatment. Opus played almost all decisions under a recovery
path that followed a provider refusal of its first request. That path removed its private notes and its
corrective retry, while keeping the permission to bluff. Opus’s bargaining departs from the defensive
pattern: as a recipient it rejects proposer-completing offers less often than other opening offers, and as
a seller it prices completing deeds close to list. We report Opus separately throughout. Because Opus
is also every other agent’s trading partner, its condition is part of their environment, and removing it
would not produce a clean comparison.
The paper makes three contributions.
• A population analysis of multi-player bargaining over a completion externality, built from every
offer as its recipient saw it, with each contrast reported by seed block, by configuration and by
response condition (Section 4).
• Evidence that the agents’ strategic attention to rivals is mainly defensive and categorical. It appears
in rejections, unanswered concessions, asking prices and stated reasons, but it does not track the

2
Setting, cohort and conditions
2.1
The game
Four players start with $1,500 on the standard forty-space United States board. The bank holds 32
houses and 12 hotels. A player who declines to buy an unowned property starts an ascending auction.
On its own turn a player may open a bilateral trade thread, in which the two parties alternate offers
and counteroffers for up to twenty exchanges until one accepts or rejects. An offer may combine cash,
deeds and get-out-of-jail cards on both sides. A player who owns every deed of a colour group may
build evenly on it if no deed in the group is mortgaged. A player who cannot pay a debt enters a
liquidation menu offering mortgages, building sales and bankruptcy, and may declare bankruptcy
even when payment is possible.
The implementation departs from common house practice in ways that matter for this paper
(Table 1). The most important is that a bankrupt player’s deeds pass to a player creditor with their
buildings and mortgages intact, so an estate can deliver a developed group at once. No rule text, price
list or rent table appears in the agents’ instructions. Purchase prompts state the landed deed’s price,
house cost, rent schedule and group, and auction prompts state its group. Management, trade, jail
and liquidation prompts name deeds by key without those values. Agents therefore value deeds in
negotiation from prior knowledge of the game, from earlier prompts, or from messages.

2.2
The cohort
The cohort is the complete set of twenty research games run in October 2026. Five engine seeds are
each used for four cyclic seat rotations of the same four configured systems, so each system sits in
each seat five times. The cyclic order is fixed, so each system always moves directly after the same
opponent; the design covers four of the twenty-four seat orders. Games last 75 to 386 turns and all end
by bankruptcy without a turn cap. Sol wins eight games, Gemini seven, Opus three and Grok two; by
seat the counts are seven, seven, three and three. These outcomes are too few and too dependent to
rank the systems, and we do not use them to do so. The same seed does not fix the later dice a player
sees, because different actions consume the random sequence differently.
Table 2 lists the systems. All four were called through OpenRouter with the recorded reasoning
setting “medium” and no temperature or sampling parameter. Opponents’ model names were visible
in every prompt. Grok was called with required tool choice. Each player saw its cash, the full holdings
and buildings of every player, bank stock, the last twenty public timeline entries and its own last ten
private notes. Trade responses also showed the current terms and the thread history. Appendix A
gives the system prompt in full and an outline of the request.
2.3
Response conditions
The prompt asks every agent to attach a public message and a private note to each action, and states,
“Deception in public chat is allowed; private thoughts should reflect true reasoning.” An invalid action
receives one corrective retry and then a deterministic engine default.
Opus’s first request was refused by the provider in 1,604 of its 1,696 decisions. Every one of the 1,605
refused responses (one is a second attempt) carries the same text, beginning “This request was blocked
as it seems to violate Anthropic’s Terms of Service restrictions on reverse engineering or duplicating
model outputs.” A refusal triggered an action-only request. That request removes the private-note
field, the instruction that private thoughts reflect true reasoning and the request for concise reasoning,
and adds “Return only a legal tool call and its public_message. Do not include private_thought or a
reasoning transcript.” It keeps “Deception in public chat is allowed” and the invitation to “negotiate,
bluff, deceive, cooperate, intimidate”. None of the 1,604 action-only requests was refused. An invalid
action-only answer received no corrective retry, and 60 such answers became engine defaults.
The refusals changed over time rather than at a code change. In games 1 to 4, which ran together
on 25 October, 64 of 84, 40 of 60, 30 of 53 and 80 of 109 Opus first attempts were refused. The last
accepted first attempts were sent between 16:14 and 16:38 UTC, and every first attempt sent after 16:40
UTC in those games, and in games 5 to 20, was refused. Anthropic’s current documentation for this
model says that requests pushing it to reproduce its internal reasoning in the response text “might
be declined with the reasoning_extraction category” [1]. That is a plausible explanation for refusing a
4

request for honest private reasoning. The saved responses, which came through OpenRouter, do not
contain the refusal category, so the trigger is not confirmed.
The consequences are specific. In 1,394 of Opus’s 1,696 prompts the private-note window held
no note written by the model (1,108 were empty and 286 held only fallback text). Opus still saw the
full game state, the public timeline and the trade history. Its public messages and actions are its own.
Its corrective retry was gone: 60 of its 61 construction defaults follow an invalid action-only build
request that could not be corrected. We report Opus’s bargaining separately because it may reflect
this condition, the model, or both. Opus also authored 381 of the 920 offers and opened 240 of the 526
threads, so its condition shaped the market the other three agents faced.
For Grok, 139 of 1,663 selected responses carry a received tool call that differs from the action
named in the assistant content. The engine applied the call. Sixty-four threads open with such a call,
and 17 of them settle. We keep these offers, because they are offers that other agents actually received,
and we report the main contrasts without them.
Two information conditions affect all agents. A formatter misattributed some past trades in the
history text of 2,054 prompts, although the structured holdings and current-offer fields were correct in
every trade response. And 149 applied public messages never entered the public timeline. Incident
claims about what a recipient saw are checked against that recipient’s actual request.
3
Data and measures
Offers.
An offer is one proposal or counteroffer awaiting a response. Its author wrote the current
terms; its recipient responds by accepting, countering or rejecting. An opening offer is the first offer of a
thread; later offers are counters. We rebuilt all 920 offers from the recipient’s delivered request, which
states both players’ holdings, cash and the current terms, and from the applied response. Completion
means that the exchange would give a party every deed of a colour group it did not fully own before,
counting mortgaged deeds. Each offer falls in one of four cells: it completes a group for the author only,
the recipient only, both, or neither. The rebuilt ledger agrees with the retained ledger of the earlier
analysis in every row and every compared field (responses, parties, stage, completion and cash after
acceptance). Two offers could not legally be accepted; the response analyses use the other 918.
Concessions and prices.
A concession is an offer whose author keeps the same non-cash bundle as its
previous offer in the same thread and moves cash toward the recipient. A cash-for-deed offer transfers
only cash one way and only deeds the other way. It is an ask when the deed holder writes it and a bid
when the cash payer writes it. Its price ratio divides the cash by the deeds’ board list prices. Board
price is an arithmetic denominator, not a fair value.
Groups and exposure.
We replay the event stream with a simple tracker of cash, ownership, mort-
gages and buildings, including estate transfers. Its state matches all 3,246 saved turn-start snapshots
exactly. A completion episode runs from the moment a player first owns a whole group until it no longer
does, is eliminated, or the game ends. We record the route that completed it, the owner’s first build,
every arrival of an opponent’s token on the group (an opponent landing), whether the group carried
any building at that moment, and the rent paid to the owner on it. Landings are observed counts, not
expected exposure.
Debts and estates.
A debt episode starts when the engine presents a liquidation menu because a
required payment exceeds cash, and ends with payment or elimination. For eliminations we join the

Stated reasons.
Two earlier LLM-assisted readings, by separate Astra research-assistant instances,

Uncertainty.
Offers share threads, players and boards, and the twenty games form five seed blocks.
We therefore do not attach offer-level significance tests. For each main contrast we report the pooled
counts, the contrast in each seed block, the range when each block is left out, the split by configuration,
and cuts without Opus or without Grok’s conflicting proposals. A contrast that keeps its sign across
blocks is less likely to depend on one seed’s events; it is still a description of these games, not an
estimate for other opponents, prompts or seat orders.

Bargaining over a rival’s completion

bargaining is unsuccessful at the first offer. Only 32 of 524 acceptance-legal opening offers are accepted,
and 81 settlements follow at least one counter. Table 3 divides the offers by whose group the exchange
would complete and by thread position.

4.2
Concessions rarely buy a completing deed
Price concessions show the same asymmetry within threads. There are 199 offers in which an author
keeps the same deeds as its previous offer and moves cash toward the recipient (Table 4). When
the bundle would complete only the author’s group, the recipient accepts 9 of 58 such concessions
(16%) and counters 39. When it completes nobody, the recipient accepts 24 of 86 (28%). The median
concession is $60 and $50 respectively. Without Opus as recipient the acceptance counts are 4 of 42
and 16 of 62. The ordering holds in four of the five seed blocks, but the blocks contain as few as three
author-completing concessions, so we treat this as consistent with the opening contrast rather than as
separate confirmation.
Counters themselves are not specific to completion. Among counters to offers that request at least
one deed, 79 of 111 to author-completing offers and 81 of 110 to non-completing offers return the same
deeds and ask for more cash. Repricing an unchanged bundle is the agents’ ordinary way to disagree.
What differs is how often a better price ends the disagreement.
4.3
Asking prices rise, bids do not
If holders treat a completing deed as worth more to its buyer, their asks should rise when the buyer
would complete a group. Pooled, they do. The median ask for a deed that would complete the buyer’s
group is 1.63 times list (87 asks), against 1.08 (113 asks) otherwise (Figure 1b). Among the three
standard-condition systems the medians are 2.29 and 1.39. Sol asks 2.32 against 1.80, and Grok 2.00
against 1.05. The ordering survives a split by whether the deed is mortgaged for Sol, Grok and Opus
alike.
The pattern is weaker than the response contrast. It holds in only three of the five seed blocks;
in blocks 1 and 5 asks to completing buyers are lower. The cleanest test is one seller asking for the
same deed from two kinds of buyer in the same game, and there are only seven such single-deed
9

comparisons. The first ask to the completing buyer is higher in five, lower in one and equal in one. In
only three of the seven (G2, G16 and G20) is every ask to the completing buyer above every ask to the
other buyer, and the asks are up to 106 turns apart. Opus’s asks barely move with completion (0.93
against 0.88).
Bids show no matching premium. Standard-condition bidders offer a median of 1.13 times list for
deeds that would complete their own group and 1.50 for others. Sol bids more for completion (1.88
against 1.25), Grok much less (0.50 against 1.54), and Opus slightly more (1.50 against 1.36). Holders
thus price a rival’s completion more consistently than buyers price their own. The asymmetry leaves
a gap that neither side’s offers close, which fits the low acceptance of completing deals. It is not an
estimate of a premium, because deeds, stages and states differ across cells.
4.4
What refusals cite, and what moves responses
The agents’ explanations name the rival’s gain. In 74 outright rejections of offers that would complete
the recipient’s own group, both LLM-assisted readers found a stated consequence for the rival in 61
records (κ = 0.79; 4 disagreements). The rate is 34 of 36 when both parties would complete and 27
of 38 when only the recipient would. Both readers also found a stated concern with the recipient’s
own price, cash or liquidity in 41 records (κ = 0.78). The coded refusals come mostly from Gemini
(31) and Grok (31). Sol writes only four outright rejections of this kind, and Opus eight, all without a
private note. Thirty-one of the 61 agreed rival codes come from two games, G1 and G15.
The most common rival consequence is that the counterparty could build at once. We can test
whether responses follow that quantity. For opening offers that would complete only the author’s
group, rejection is 75% (100 of 134) when the author would keep enough cash for a full first layer of
houses, 76% (32 of 42) when it could afford at least one house but not a layer, and 55% (6 of 11) when
it could not afford one house. Later offers show the same flat profile, at 20%, 21% and one of three. The
recipients do not reject more as the rival’s building cash grows. Their response follows the category, a
rival completing a group, rather than the amount they cite. The amount may still matter in ways these
cells cannot show, since prices and deeds also vary across them.

4.6
The Opus exception
Opus departs from the defensive pattern at each step. As a recipient it rejects proposer-completing
openings less often than other openings, it accepts 5 of 16 completing concessions, and its asks hardly
change with completion. It also opens 240 of the 526 threads and bids above list for completing deeds.
Opus played most decisions without a private note and without a corrective retry. These records
cannot separate the model from that condition, and we do not attribute its behaviour to either. What
they do show is that one of the four players was a ready trading partner, and that 8 of the 14 distressed
completing sales came from it. The other agents’ defensive bargaining took place in a market that
contained this partner.
5
From completed groups to income, debts and estates
The defensive pattern concerns what holders try to prevent. This section follows what happens when
a group is completed anyway: whether it is built on, how much opponent traffic it receives, how debts
are met, and who receives the first estate.
5.1
Development and rent exposure
Trade completes 63 groups. Their owners build on 54 of them, 44 no later than the owner’s next
turn (Figure 3). Opponents land on these groups 213 times while the completing owner holds them,
150 times after the owner has built. Rent on the groups totals $57,378, of which $56,126 is collected
while they carry buildings. A landing on a developed trade-completed group pays the owner $374 on
average; a landing on an undeveloped one pays $20. These figures come from different deeds, stages
and building levels, so the ratio is not the return to building. They show why a completed group
matters to everyone else at the table: its value comes from the houses that can follow.
The owners differ in how quickly ownership becomes development. Sol builds on all 17 of its
trade-completed groups, 16 of them no later than its next turn, and only 3 of the 60 opponent landings
on them find the group without buildings. Gemini builds on 17 of 18. Opus builds on 12 of 15, and 13
of 35 landings find its groups without buildings. Grok builds on 8 of 13, and 33 of the 50 landings on
its groups find them without buildings; those groups collect $4,560 in total. The nine groups that are
never built receive 33 landings and $538 of rent.
Mortgages go with the groups that stay unbuilt. Eight of the nine never-built groups were completed
with at least one mortgaged deed, against 10 of the 54 groups that were built. A mortgaged deed must
be redeemed at 110% of its mortgage value before any house can go on the group. The earlier analysis
of each owner’s first management turn after completion found that Grok’s notes on four of its groups
explicitly defer redemption or construction to keep cash, and that three Opus groups went unbuilt in
that turn because an invalid action-only build request became an engine default. Holders, as Section 4
showed, ask little for mortgaged deeds (Figure 1b); the buyer who completes a group this way still
has to pay to make it productive.

7
Discussion
7.1
What the agents attend to
The completing deed is a textbook identity-dependent externality. Its holder is worse off if a particular
rival gets it, so the holder’s reservation price depends on who buys [6]. The agents behave as if they
know this. They state it in so many words (“$450 is the price for the player who completes yellow”),
and three kinds of behaviour follow it: recipients refuse proposer-completing openings more often
than neutral ones, holders’ asks rise for completing buyers in the pooled data, and cash concessions
rarely buy such a deed. This is opponent attention of a particular kind. The agents reason about what
a rival will be able to do with an asset, using holdings and cash that the prompt shows exactly. The
19

records contain many such deductions about public capacity. They contain no comparable evidence
about rivals’ hidden beliefs or intentions, which the design does not measure.
The attention is mainly defensive. Holders price a rival’s completion more consistently than
buyers price their own. Recipients often refuse offers that would complete their own group when the
counterparty also gains, and the refusals cite the rival’s gain more often than any other consideration.
In a game that only one player survives, relative position is what counts, so a refusal of a mutually
completing swap is not irrational by itself. G15 shows the logic: Grok declines its own monopoly
because Sol could build first. The records cannot tell us whether such refusals helped. A refusal
followed by elimination, as in G15, is one path, not a counterfactual comparison.
7.2
Attention without calibration
The quantities the agents cite do not organise their responses. Rejection of proposer-completing
openings is about 75% whether the proposer would keep enough cash for a full first layer of houses
or for one house only. Asking prices for completing deeds vary widely across seed blocks, and
comparisons of one seller pricing one deed to two buyers are mixed. Counters reprice the same bundle
whether or not completion is at stake. Several of the numbers inside the explanations are also wrong:
$520 does not buy nine yellow houses, a nine-square roll is not 5 in 36, and one house per green is not
the minimum build.
A coherent reading is that the agents apply a categorical rule, roughly “do not hand a rival a
monopoly cheaply”, and then bargain over price with a generic routine. That reading matches the
controlled two-party finding that LLM negotiators can model a counterparty accurately while their final
prices follow opening anchors and local adjustment [4]. Our setting is uncontrolled and multi-player,
so it cannot test that mechanism. It does show the same separation between knowing what matters to
the other side and pricing it consistently, in complete games with real consequences. We describe this
as strategic attention. Strategic effectiveness would require a value reference for each decision, which
this cohort lacks.
7.3
A market that clears under distress
If holders guard completing deeds and buyers do not pay more for them, few such deeds should trade
except when a holder’s circumstances change. That is what the settled sales show. Most completing
deeds that sold for cash came from sellers holding less than $100, and the six standard-condition sellers
in that position still received a median of 2.25 times list. Buyers who complete a group this way often
receive a mortgaged deed and must redeem it before building, which is where many of the unbuilt
groups stall.
The collective consequence is that negotiation does not stop consolidation. Groups are completed
by trade, purchase and inheritance; developed groups collect almost all rent; rent drives the debts
that end players; and the first estate usually goes to a player who already leads. The agents see this
chain and bargain over its last link. Grok sells “insurance” against Sol inheriting its yellows (G13),
Opus withdraws a standing offer once the seller’s estate is close (G18), and Gemini asks the table to
stop funding a rival’s houses (G20). The creditor rule makes a third player’s bankruptcy a matter for
everyone, and the agents’ messages treat it that way.
20

7.4
Conditions are part of the environment
Opus is the one system whose bargaining does not look defensive, and it is also the system that
played almost every decision under action-only recovery. We cannot separate the model from the
condition. What matters for the other agents is that a willing trading partner existed. Opus opened
240 threads, sold half of the completing deeds that changed hands for cash, and sold 8 of the 14
distressed ones, seven of them below list. Grok’s conflicting calls opened another 64 threads. Each
participant’s response condition therefore changed the offers that the others received. Analyses of
multi-agent behaviour need to report such conditions as properties of the population, because deleting
one participant’s rows does not recover a clean experiment for the rest.
7.5
The motivating questions
The study began from eight capability questions and three broader ones (Appendix E). The cohort
answers the example in the first question most directly: agents do reject seemingly attractive trades
because of what the trade gives an opponent, and their rejections and prices follow that consideration
at the level of categories. The records also describe opponent attention to public capacity, bargaining
tactics in context, liquidity management at every debt, and the concentration of assets through creditor
succession. They do not measure calibrated beliefs about hidden information, negotiation efficiency,
learning caused by feedback, the expected value of a price, transfer to unfamiliar opponents, or the
relation between general and multi-agent capability. Those are unmeasured here, which is different
from a finding that the agents lack them.
8
Related work
Negotiation by language models.
NegotiationArena studies LLM bargaining in controlled two-party
scenarios with tactic manipulations [3]. Cosentino et al. give one side ranked information about the
other’s preferences in a multi-issue purchase negotiation and find that accurate counterparty modelling
does not reliably improve the informed side’s outcome, that final prices track opening anchors more
than utility weights, and that sellers are more accommodating than buyers [4]. Those designs have
explicit utilities and can score agreements. Ours has none, but its bargaining happens inside a complete
multi-player game in which the counterparty’s gain affects third players and later turns.
Economic and board-game agents.
AgentMonopoly studies open-weight model families, sizes and
personas in four-player Monopoly games with a 150-step limit and immediate elimination at negative
cash, and argues that persona following should be judged by actions rather than language [11].
FinBoardBench evaluates financial decisions across board games, including Monopoly counteroffers,
and its Monopoly bankruptcy treatment sells buildings to the bank [5]. DECE evaluates complete
model-and-execution configurations across ten Eurogames with matched comparisons of execution
frameworks [12]. Vending-Bench studies long-horizon operation of a simulated business [2]. Its
multi-agent communication study analyses 2,583 emails over twenty one-year simulations, checks
factual claims against the simulator’s event log, measures promise follow-through, and replicates its
judging with other model families [9]. That study is the closest model for joining messages to state;
our incidents are selected illustrations, not a population rate. The present paper adds a population
analysis of bargaining over an identity-dependent externality, rebuilt from what each recipient saw,
and follows the completed groups into development, debt and inheritance.
21

Externalities in exchange.
Jehiel, Moldovanu and Stacchetti model sales in which a non-acquirer
suffers an externality that depends on who acquires the object, so that buyers’ outside options are
endogenous [6]. We use the concept descriptively. Our agents are not mechanism designers, and
Monopoly sellers are themselves exposed to the externality they price.
Reading agents’ explanations.
Model explanations can be unfaithful to the computation that pro-
duced an answer [8, 10]. We therefore treat private notes, public messages and provider-returned text
as recorded channels, and draw behavioural conclusions from applied actions. Evaluations of complete
agent systems also motivate reporting configuration and execution conditions with results [7].
9
Limitations
The cohort is small and structured. Five seeds, one fixed roster, a single cyclic seat order and visible
model names limit what the patterns say about other opponents, prompts or seatings. Contrasts that
hold in every seed block are still descriptions of these games.
The response conditions differ by system. Opus played most decisions without private notes or a
corrective retry after a provider refusal, Grok’s applied calls sometimes contradict its content, and all
agents received some misattributed trade history. We report the main contrasts with and without the
affected rows, but the conditions also shaped every other agent’s opportunities, so no subset is a clean
control.
The agents were not told the rules, and prices appear only in some prompts. Their valuations
therefore mix strategic judgment with recall of the standard game, which the implementation sometimes
departs from.
The comparisons are observational. Offer cells differ in deeds, prices, stages and states; the distress
result conditions on settlement; asset value is a crude index. No value reference exists to say whether a
refusal, a price or a bluff was good. The stated-reason codes come from two readers of one model family,
one partly unblinded, and agreement between them is not validation. The eight incidents were selected
to show mechanisms, and seven involve Grok, whose separate content and call channels make conflicts
visible; they estimate no rate. The engine replay and the event tracker establish correspondence with
the implemented rules, not with any other rule set.
10
Conclusion
In twenty complete games, four configured LLM agents bargained over the deed that would complete
a rival’s group as if its value depended on who bought it. They refused proposer-completing offers
more often, rarely accepted cash concessions for such deeds, asked more from completing buyers in
the pooled data, and said why. Their responses followed that category rather than the cash amounts
they cited, and the deeds changed hands mainly when holders needed money. The groups then turned
into income through building, and into control through creditor succession that usually rewarded
an existing leader. The records establish what the agents attended to and what they did. Whether
that defensive bargaining was good play is a question for a design with a value reference and varied
opponents.

The prompt and the request
Every decision sends one system message and one user message. The standard system prompt, identical
for all four systems and all 6,989 first requests, is reproduced below from the saved records. Markdown
asterisks are part of the original text.
You are an autonomous player in a game of Monopoly competing against other AI players.
Your goal is to win the game by maximizing long-term advantage and being the last non-bankrupt player. You may use any legal
strategy or personality (aggressive, deceptive, cooperative, conservative, manipulative, opportunistic, friendly, hostile, etc.)
and may adapt dynamically as the game evolves.
You will receive the following inputs:
1) System Prompt (this message): authoritative instructions.
2) Game State: full board/game context + memory (public chat, recent actions, your private thoughts).
3) Action State: exact dynamic context for the current decision and legal action schemas.
### Action Rules
- Make exactly one tool call per decision.
- Use only tools listed as legal for that decision.
- Never invent tools, arguments, or targets.
- Obey the provided argument schema exactly.
- If a tool requires no arguments, pass none.
### Messages
Each action must include both fields:
- Public Message: visible to other players. Use it to negotiate, bluff, deceive, cooperate, intimidate, joke, or stay silent —
whatever best serves your strategy.
- Private Thoughts: visible only to you. Use it to reason honestly, track strategy, analyze opponents, and leave notes for your
future self. Be concise but clear.
- Keep both messages token-efficient and concise.
### Strategy Guidance
- Play strategically with long-term outcomes in mind.
- Observe opponents and adapt.
- Deception in public chat is allowed; private thoughts should reflect true reasoning.
- Be consistent unless there is a reason to change.
- Prefer concise reasoning and communication.
Your role is not to explain rules or debug the system.
Your role is to **play Monopoly and win**.
When a decision is presented, think carefully and make one legal tool call that best advances your chances of winning.

Fresh arithmetic.
The following were computed for this draft by scripts in tools/ that read the saved
records (events, decision streams, snapshots and board data) and import neither the engine nor a
model client. corpus.py loads the twenty games and records SHA-256 hashes of every source file.
offers.py rebuilds the 920 offers from delivered requests and compares them with the retained ledger
(no field differs). bargaining.py, pricing.py, extras.py and sensitivity.py produce every bargaining
number. track.py replays events into board state and matches all 3,246 turn-start snapshots. groups.py,
debts.py and estates.py produce the episode, debt and estate results. conditions.py produces the
refusal, prompt-difference, token and cost results. build_figures.py and build_tables.py format the
outputs.

Inherited evidence.
The classification of the 60 eliminations, the payment witnesses, the 61 executable
Opus construction targets, the Grok channel-conflict census, the history-formatter and message-delivery
counts, and the first-management-window findings come from earlier engine-executed or record-
reading audits and were not rerun. The stated-reason codes are earlier LLM-assisted labels, joined
here without recoding. The quoted fields in the transcript cards come from earlier checked quote
definitions; this draft adds four new definitions from G2 and re-verifies every displayed quote against
its original field.
