LLM bargaining over competitors’ future options:
Strategic attention and its limits in twenty Monopoly games
Independent Sol draft, 11 November 2026
Abstract
An agent can acquire a useful asset while making its competitive position worse. We examine
how LLM players address this tension in twenty complete four-player Monopoly games. The records
contain 920 negotiation responses, legal choices, delivered messages, and subsequent economic
histories. The central observation is strategic attention to competitors’ future options: agents
discuss construction funds, intermediary resale, and who would inherit a distressed rival’s estate,
sometimes withholding a deed that would complete their own group. This attention coexists with
generic cash repricing and mixed valuation evidence. Higher seller asks for completing deeds persist
in most seller, mortgage, and stage strata, but seven same-game/seller/deed comparisons contain
four increases, two decreases, and one tie. Eight contextual incidents distinguish rival-sensitive
bargaining from factual correction, public bluffing, and conflicting execution. Exposure-aware
development histories and all 188 debt-menu episodes place those negotiations in the economy they
affect. First creditors already lead or tie in prior paid rent in fifteen games, restricting an inheritance-
driven account of victory. These are observations of strategic conduct under heterogeneous response
conditions, not evidence of optimal bargaining, causal persuasion, or a ranking of underlying
models.
1
Introduction
In a competitive economy, a transaction changes more than the holdings of its two participants. Cash
paid for a spare property can fund a rival’s next investment. A deed can become an intermediary’s
bargaining instrument. Keeping a distressed opponent alive can prevent another player from inher-
iting a valuable estate. An agent evaluating only its own immediate acquisition would miss these
consequences. An agent that talks about them has demonstrated a different, narrower achievement
than one that chooses the best response to them.
This paper studies that distinction in complete games of Monopoly played by LLM agents. The
scientific question is whether the agents’ bargaining addresses the future options of competitors,
and what the surrounding records establish about the use of that attention. We use strategic attention
for an explicit, state-linked consideration of another player’s future holdings, finance, actions, or
estate. It is an observable property of recorded conduct and explanations. We do not use it as a
latent cognitive score. Strategic effectiveness would require a criterion showing that the consideration
improves a decision or outcome. That criterion is absent for most trades in this corpus.
The distinction matters because conversational sophistication can be read too generously or too
dismissively. A seller who discusses a buyer’s construction budget may be pricing a competitive threat,
applying an ordinary markup, or rationalizing a demand after selecting it. A buyer who rejects a
completing deed may be preserving liquidity, avoiding a rival’s stronger completion, or making an
1

error. Prices, stated reasons, and later results answer different parts of this problem. The purpose
of following a complete game is to identify the stakes and the realized sequence without converting
every winning trajectory into a good strategy.
Our central finding is that competitive consequences enter bargaining at several distances from
the immediate exchange. Agents negotiate over a counterparty’s building funds, a third party’s resale
opportunity, and a prospective creditor’s acquisition. Some refuse their own group completion even
after a proposer adjusts terms to address a cash objection. This is more specific than observing frequent
trading or fluent strategic vocabulary. The same records also resist a stronger claim about coherent
strategic valuation. Most counters to the examined deed exchanges reprice an unchanged bundle
whether or not it completes a group. Aggregate higher asks for completing deeds become mixed in
the small set of same-deed comparisons. In one focal negotiation, the correct concern about a rival’s
remaining cash accompanies an incorrect house-count calculation.
The twenty games contain 6,989 decisions and 526 negotiation threads between four fixed config-
urations. We examine the entire response population, then use eight contextual incidents to show
what numerical categories omit. The incidents include a refused final-price claim and a settled one,
refusal of one’s own completion, correction followed by acceptance, a construction-financing denial
appeal, selective disclosure during distress, withdrawal of a public assurance, and a payable debt
resolved through a contradictory action. They were chosen retrospectively to expose different rela-
tionships between a statement and its consequence. They are neither a balanced model sample nor a
conduct-frequency estimate.
The economic histories supply substantive checks on the interpretation. We add movement expo-
sure and ownership timing to all sixty-three trade-completed groups. We reconstruct every episode
reaching a debt menu, including 128 observed payments rather than studying only eliminations. We
examine the positions immediately before the first estate, when the creditor already leads or ties in
paid rent in fifteen games. These analyses make construction finance and creditor identity concrete
objects of bargaining while constraining an explanation that assigns victory to inheritance alone.
The paper makes three contributions. First, it documents competitive-option bargaining in com-
plete LLM game trajectories, with exact statements, current economic conditions, recipient responses,
and later outcomes. Second, it tests simple aggregate interpretations against available within-record
comparisons, including jointly stratified responses, same-deed asking prices, movement exposure,
successful debt payments, and pre-estate positions. Third, it separates strategic attention, executable
action, and realized consequence in a single observational account. That separation is useful because
these configured participants do not operate under interchangeable response conditions.
This is a bounded study of existing play. Five seeds, cyclic rotations, one roster, uneven information,
and response-specific recovery prevent causal attribution to a model or tactic. The broader research
agenda concerns strategic reasoning, opponent modeling, negotiation and conduct, planning and
memory, adaptation, economics and risk, collective dynamics, and validity. It also asks about strategic
talk versus action, general versus multi-agent capability, and skill versus chance or weak opponents.
Appendix A maps all eleven questions to their measured fragments. Unmeasured capabilities remain
unanswered questions.

3
Measures and analytical scope
An offer is one proposal or counter awaiting a response. The current author may differ from the thread
initiator. We calculate ownership completion atomically and use the 918 offers whose menus permit
acceptance, keeping the two ineligible offers in the overall response census. Opening offers and later
offers are separated because continuation depends on earlier responses. Joint recipient-by-stage counts
make the conditioning explicit. We do not treat offers in a thread or turns in a game as independent
trials.
For pricing, we restrict the ledger to cash exchanged for deeds, excluding jail cards and counter-
vailing assets or cash. An ask comes from the deed seller; a bid comes from the cash buyer. The ratio
divides cash by the sum of deeds’ board list prices. That denominator is a reproducible scale, not
a fair or strategic value. We stratify by author, mortgage status, and thread position, then inspect
single-deed offers within a game and seller. The latter comparisons hold fewer variables uncontrolled
but do not fix timing, buyer identity, cash, outside options, or prior dialogue. Sparse overlap limits
the claim rather than licensing a fitted completion coefficient.
A completion episode begins when an acquisition creates a complete group and ends at ownership
loss or game end. For all sixty-three trade episodes, the first management window is the owner’s
first actual management turn after settlement, including its successive decisions. We retain prior
classifications of construction access and execution. Fresh arithmetic adds acquisition and build turns,
ownership duration, paid rent, and opponent movement events during the episode. Each opponent
move is counted once per exposed group, including card-driven moves. A group landing is a recorded
move ending on any deed in the group, even if mortgaged or nonpaying. This is realized exposure,
not a probability model or an exogenous opportunity treatment.
Debt episodes join consecutive liquidation decisions for one player, turn, creditor, reason, and owed
amount, ending at a non-liquidation decision or elimination. Each of the 128 successful episodes has
a matching payment event in the final action’s emitted range. The sixty elimination endpoints join the
preserved elimination ledger. Existing legal payment sequences establish three payable eliminations.
Fifty-six others have debt above the generous bound of cash plus all building salvage plus every
unmortgaged deed’s mortgage principal, including collateral unlocked by removing buildings. One
has sufficient nominal assets but a menu-level hotel lock. No engine search or witness execution is
added here.
First-estate positions use the simultaneous delivered state before the first bankruptcy action in
each game. We compare cash, deeds, building house-equivalents, and paid rent previously received.
A hotel counts as five construction levels for this accounting display, not five available bank houses.
Rank one includes ties. These are transparent descriptions of prior strength, without a constructed
utility score. They do not estimate the independent predictive value or causal effect of inheritance.
Semantic evidence remains inherited. Two Astra readers labeled the seventy-four outright rejections
of acceptance-legal recipient-completing offers. Both assigned a rival-consequence code in sixty-one

Competitive options enter bargaining, but do not identify a price pre-
mium
4.1
Completion and rejection depend on who responds and when
Across 526 threads, the agents accept 113 offers, counter 394, and reject 413. No trade response is a
deterministic fallback. The four configurations accept similar absolute numbers, twenty-six to thirty
each, while their alternatives to agreement differ. Sol counters 140 of 235 received offers and Opus 141
of 189. Gemini rejects 180 of 242 and Grok 149 of 254. Opus’s response path is action-only recovery in
162 responses. Similar acceptance totals therefore coexist with very different interaction histories.
The pooled rejection rate is 55.0% when only the author completes and 27.1% when only the
recipient completes. Separating thread position reduces the difference from 27.9 percentage points
to 13.0 for openings and 12.7 for later offers. Author-only offers disproportionately open threads,
where rejection is common. Joint recipient-by-stage cells further qualify the pattern. Its rejection
ordering holds in five of six Sol/Gemini/Grok cells; Opus reverses the pooled comparison, rejecting
3/52 author-only and 6/42 recipient-only offers. Appendix B preserves all cells, including small
denominators. These distributions establish varied responses to heterogeneous transactions, rather
than a controlled preference for denying completion.
The reasons are more specific than the response categories. Among thirty-eight outright refusals of
recipient-only completion, both inherited readings identify a stated rival consequence in twenty-seven.
The corresponding count is thirty-four of thirty-six when both sides complete. The recipient-only cases
matter because no rival group is newly completed by that transaction. Cash, retained blockers, and
other holdings can still improve a rival’s competitive position. However, thirty-four common-positive

labels come from G1 and G15, and all eight Opus records lack private notes. This is concentrated
evidence of explicit considerations, not a comparison of underlying reasoning ability.
4.2
Generic repricing and mixed within-deed evidence
Counters often demand more cash without changing the property exchange. Of 111 counters to
author-only completion offers, ninety-two preserve all requested deeds, and seventy-nine preserve
the entire noncash bundle while improving the counterer’s cash. In the comparison population of 110
non-completing offers requesting a deed, eighty-seven preserve all requested deeds and eighty-one
preserve the noncash bundle while improving cash. Sol and Opus supply most counters in both
populations. Repricing is therefore a broad bargaining practice. Its occurrence alone cannot establish
that an agent priced a rival’s development opportunity.
Seller asks show more consistent aggregate ordering than buyer bids. For unmortgaged deeds,
median asks divided by list price are 2.81 versus 2.00 for Sol, 2.00 versus 1.50 for Opus, and 4.14 versus
1.82 for Grok, comparing recipient completion with neither-party completion. Mortgaged asks have
the same ordering for these three sellers. Jointly separating seller, mortgage status, and opening versus
later position gives eleven higher completing asks and one reversal among their twelve populated
comparison cells. The reversal is Opus’s unmortgaged later offers, with medians 1.86 versus 2.10.
Gemini has too few asks for a useful profile. Full cells and exact sample sizes appear in the appendix.
These aggregate comparisons still combine different deeds. Only seven game/seller/single-deed
groups contain asks to both completing and non-completing buyers. Four have higher median asks to
the completing buyer, two lower, and one equal, as shown in Figure 1. Requiring the same mortgage
status and thread position leaves five comparison cells: three higher, one lower, and one equal. The
strongest available restrictions thus preserve counterexamples and sharply reduce overlap. We do
not label the unmatched ordering a seller premium, an endowment effect, or an efficient response to
externalities. Buyer bids also change ordering across configurations and cuts, preventing a general
claim that only sellers recognize completion value.

demand and attached note link the price to the rival’s development budget:

Sol · applied private note · G2 d124 · attempt 0 · full field

4.3
Public claims, corrections, and agreement
The complete response census does not reveal whether accepting a deal means endorsing its explana-
tion. Two cases show why that distinction matters. The first contains an explicit public limit below a
private contingency. The second contains a false board premise corrected by the recipient while the
exchange executes. They place communication in its bargaining role without measuring a persuasion
effect.
Incident 3. A public final price below a private contingency settles
Sol asks $625 for North Carolina. Grok replies with a lower public limit while retaining a higher
private contingency:
Grok · public message · G7 d42 · attempt 0 · excerpt
$600, final. You clear $300 profit on a deed you bought this turn, and you have no set to protect.
Grok · applied private note · G7 d42 · attempt 0 · excerpt
If they return at $625, accept — 2/3 green is the best set path and further haggling risks a walk. Do not go above
$625.
Sol · public message · G7 d43 · attempt 0 · full field
Deal. North Carolina is yours for $600.
Sol’s note favors reinvestment over waiting for another $25. The contradiction between the ad-
vertised final price and the same-response contingency supports a bargaining-bluff interpretation
under a prompt that permits it. Agreement establishes the settled price, not that the bluff caused
Sol’s concession. Grok later completes green through an auction at turn 95, never builds it, and loses
its estate to Sol at turn 110. Sol develops the inherited group two turns later. The same deed can
thus pass through purchase, negotiated resale, and creditor acquisition without its initial negotiation
determining the eventual productive owner.

4.4
Who supplies construction finance?
Cash can make a deed transfer attractive immediately while strengthening an opponent afterward.
Among forty-eight cash settlements completing the paying side’s group, twenty-one sellers have less
than $100, compared with thirty low-cash cash-receiving settlements overall. Opus supplies thirteen
of those twenty-one. Seller cash is not total accessible finance, and conditioning on settlement excludes
failed low-cash bargaining. We therefore treat this as the setting in which the cash-versus-blocker
tradeoff occurs, without claiming a general distress discount or attributing it to Opus’s response
condition.
Incident 5. An appeal to deny construction funds receives mixed participation
Opus sells Illinois to Grok for $500, completing reds and leaving Grok $222. A red house costs
$150. Gemini addresses the table:
Gemini · public message · G20 d96 · attempt 0 · full field
Ending my turn. Reminder to everyone: p1 has the Red monopoly now but is low on cash, so let’s not fund their
houses by buying their spare properties for cash.
Grok’s next selected note recognizes the appeal as a cash boycott. Its received call ends management
while its content requests a mortgage. Later, Grok offers Vermont to Gemini for $150. Gemini refuses:
Gemini · public message · G20 d101 · attempt 0 · full field
No thanks, p1. Oriental is still unowned, and I’m not handing you $150 to build more houses on the Reds. Go
ahead and mortgage it for $50 if you need the cash.
Grok mortgages Vermont for $50 instead. Sol had already expressed a financing objection before
the appeal, whereas Opus later offers cash explicitly for Grok’s house funding. Neither Opus offer
settles. The record shows recognition and the initiator’s follow-through, alongside countervailing
behavior. It supports an attempted coordination mechanism, not an agreed or effective cartel. Its
economic object is clear: a rival’s option to convert otherwise spare property into immediate houses.

What the negotiated positions become
5.1
Development has a financing path and an exposure history
The agents’ concerns about building funds correspond to a real transition between completing a group
and raising its rent schedule. All sixty-three trade-completed groups reach a management window.
Forty-three receive construction in that first window: sixteen of seventeen for Sol, seventeen of eighteen
for Gemini, seven of fifteen for Opus, and three of thirteen for Grok. The remaining twenty divide
into nine without an offered build on the target group, four defaults, four received-call disagreements,
and three other valid ends. Completion counts by themselves conceal these different opportunities
and outcomes.
Eight of the nine groups without a build option contain a mortgage. Two owners have enough
cash at window entry to cover the implemented redemption price and one house; five more have
sufficient budgets if currently offered outside-group mortgages are included. These are static budgets,
not new executed financing routes. Grok’s four notes explicitly defer redemption or construction for
liquidity. Such a deferral can preserve cash while sacrificing immediate rent, and mortgaging other
collateral may disable a different group. The record does not supply a common objective that ranks
those choices.
Response handling contributes a separate path. The historical checks preserve all sixty-one de-
faulted Opus construction purchase multisets after legally splitting and ordering the requested pur-
chases. Their submitted encodings remain invalid. Sixty chains contain refusal followed by invalid
recovery, while G2 d129 contains invalid construction followed by refusal. Exhausting the response
budget ends management. This is evidence about requested allocations and execution, not evidence
that those allocations were good investments or that a corrected interface would change a winner.
Details remain in the appendix rather than becoming a defect taxonomy in the main argument.
The first-window groups collect 93.8% of paid rent after trade completion, a magnitude previously
easy to confuse with a return to early development. Table 3 now shows the associated timing and
exposure. Median acquisition dates and ownership durations are similar, and the non-first-window
group actually has the larger median movement exposure. At the aggregate level, the first-window
groups account for 71.0% of opponent movements and 70.4% of group landings. Their 93.8% rent
share is therefore not solely a consequence of having nearly all the observed landings.

A prior 240-decision textual sample contains many contingent statements and few later matched
opportunities. We retain the concrete positive and conflicting trajectories rather than turning that
selected sample into a planning rate. Neither target realization nor recurrence alone identifies the
contribution of private-note memory.
5.2
Successful financing changes the debt denominator
The full debt-menu population contains 188 episodes. In 128, the final action emits payment of the
recorded amount. The other sixty match the elimination ledger. Its inherited classifications identify
three legal payment routes, fifty-six debts beyond the generous own-asset bound, and one menu-level
hotel lock. Table 4 puts successful and unsuccessful outcomes together. Among the 131 demonstrably
payable episodes, three end in elimination, or 2.3%. This is an exact corpus description using observed
payments and existing witnesses. It is not an estimate of general financial competence, and it does not
include ordinary bills paid immediately without reaching a debt menu.
Sol’s G20 payment illustrates the scale of successful finance. Starting with $49, it raises $700 in
building salvage and $665 through mortgages, pays $1,400, and retains $14. Gemini’s G16 note instead
correctly concludes that $288 cash, $375 salvage, and $730 mortgages total only $1,393 against a $2,000
debt. These are different kinds of competence evidence: an executed multistep payment and a correct
recognition of insolvency. The population denominator prevents a few payable eliminations from
standing for every encounter with financial distress.

5.3
Creditor positions precede the estate
Before the final elimination, estates transfer 227 deeds, 202 to eventual winners. The first creditor
wins eighteen games. Those figures motivate asking what position the creditor already held. Figure 3
shows ranks immediately before the first bankruptcy action. First creditors lead or tie in prior paid rent
in fifteen games and in building house-equivalents in twelve, but lead or tie in cash in only eight. They
are not uniformly the cash-richest or most asset-rich players. They often already hold a rent-producing
position before the estate arrives.
The incoming assets still matter to the continuing game. Nonterminal estates complete fifty-two
groups, forty-five for eventual winners, and transfer buildings intact under this ruleset. Yet only
fourteen of those fifty-two groups receive any subsequent rent, compared with fifty-one of sixty-
three trade-completed groups. Late acquisition and existing dominance restrict further opportunities.
Counting terminal estate completions as productive achievements would be especially misleading
because sixty-nine occur at the final elimination and have no later management window. These histories
make prospective creditor identity a plausible bargaining concern while leaving its independent
contribution to victory unmeasured.

6
Discussion
6.1
What the records teach about strategic behavior
The observed agents sometimes treat a transaction as a change in competitors’ future action sets. Sol
prices Marvin Gardens through construction funds and possible intermediary resale. Grok refuses its
own completion while discussing the proposer’s ability to build before rolling. Gemini asks others to
withhold cash that would fund red houses. Grok and Opus negotiate over whether another player
would inherit a distressed estate. These are different expressions of one substantive pattern: the
competitive consequences named in bargaining extend beyond the nominal item and its current
owner.
This pattern is not dictated action by action by the Monopoly rules. The rules permit refusal,
markup, accommodation, and silence; they do not require the particular resale account, cash-denial
appeal, or rescue pitch. Nor does permission to bluff prescribe the $600 final-price claim or the omitted
mortgage. The prompt does shape which conduct is licensed. We therefore describe the behavior as
authorized strategic interaction, without presenting deception as an emergent violation of an honesty
15

instruction.
The most useful negative result concerns the strength of the valuation interpretation. Strategic
attention has observable examples, but neither cash repricing nor pooled asking-price medians estab-
lish strategic valuation. The unchanged-bundle comparison is almost as frequent without completion,
and same-deed comparisons are mixed. A mechanical haggling routine can coexist with a tailored
explanation. Conversely, the generic pattern does not show that every tailored explanation is empty.
The correct conclusion preserves both scales: a broad bargaining practice and local evidence of state-
specific competitive reasoning. Distinguishing them is more informative than selecting the favorable
price cut and naming it a premium.
Another distinction concerns public capacity versus hidden mental states. Much of the observed
opponent attention concerns cash, holdings, collateral, and turn order, all visible facts. Sol revises
G15 terms after an explicit objection, rather than predicting an unobserved preference and testing its
calibration. G13’s buyer discusses the estate but does not affirm the seller’s asserted cash minimum.
These interactions demonstrate attention to public competitive constraints. They do not establish
theory of mind about hidden beliefs or beliefs changed by persuasion.
6.2
Strategic effectiveness needs its own criterion
The economic data show why the cited constraints are consequential, while also showing why later
victory is an inadequate score for each decision. A completion must pass through financing and valid
construction to raise its rents. Cash receipts depend on actual opponent exposure, mortgages, and the
payer’s solvency. Inheritance can make an earlier sale temporary, as North Carolina returns to Sol, or
move a blocked group to a third player, as in G2. Evaluating a trade by its eventual owner’s winning
status would attribute all intervening chance and opponent action to the original bargain.
The all-debt denominator also changes the balance of evidence. Most menu-reaching debts with
demonstrated payment capacity are paid. A small set of payable eliminations is meaningful, but it is
not representative of every financing decision. The successful $1,400 payment and the correct $2,000
insolvency account deserve the same explanatory attention as the contradictions. Local debt feasibility
has a defined endpoint: paying an existing obligation using permitted own assets. Bargaining quality
instead depends on future responses and risks. A deterministic replay verifies a recorded history and
can support a local payment sequence; it does not supply an optimal continuation policy.
First-estate concentration has a similar boundary. The first creditor often already has the largest
prior rent receipts, and twelve creditors lead or tie in buildings. That supports a pathway in which
productive positions precede estates. It neither makes every estate irrelevant nor identifies estate
acquisition as the cause of winning. The future estate is strategically relevant to G13 and G18 because
ownership can change through failure, not because the observed concentration proves a general
snowball mechanism independent of prior strength.
The evidence would contradict a stronger central claim if rival-sensitive examples disappeared
under exact state checks, if recipients had not received the cited statements, or if the proposed rival
consequences were unrelated to the actual exchange. The corpus contains both confirming and limiting
records. G2’s development calculation is partly wrong, G19’s recipient explicitly rejects a false premise,
and G20’s appeal does not attract uniform participation. Those qualifications belong to the substantive
account of what the agents accomplish. They are not a separate catalog of mistakes.

7
Relation to prior work
Early neural negotiation work studies language and agreement under explicit item utilities [7]. He et
al. [4] separate coarse strategic acts from language generation. That distinction informs our use of
structured offers alongside public messages: their relationship is an empirical object, not an assumed
identity. NegotiationArena supplies controlled tactic comparisons across bargaining scenarios [2].
Our selected public bluffs have no comparable intervention, so we do not transfer its causal tactic
interpretations to these exchanges.
Cosentino et al. [3] directly distinguish counterparty modeling from strategic use in controlled
multi-attribute bargaining, measuring outcomes against known utilities. Our distinction between
attention and effectiveness is consistent with that question, while the multiplayer setting introduces
construction finance and future creditor ownership. We lack its explicit utility reference. Classical
work on sales with externalities formalizes how the identity of an acquirer can affect non-acquirers [6].
That provides an economic interpretation for blockers and resale concerns; we do not claim that the
observed agents implement its optimal mechanism.
AgentMonopoly already studies Monopoly actions, language, model families, opponents, and
persona instructions [10]. FinBoardBench already studies financial board games, negotiation, and
liquidity, including Monopoly counteroffers [5]. Our contribution is the bounded behavioral account
enabled by complete offer histories, financing opportunities, and estate trajectories, with more compa-
rable existing-record cuts. Monopoly play, counteroffers, and language-action contrasts themselves
are not new contributions.
The Vending-Bench Arena communication study joins emails to simulator state, analyzes follow-
through, and repeats judging with other families [8]. It provides a useful precedent for contextual
state-grounded analysis. Our eight incidents are a purposive set rather than a replicated population
communication classifier. We retain the distinction between factual assertion, delivered exposure,
explicit correction, and later action, while applying a different conduct norm that permits public
bluffing. None of these related results validates our semantic labels or confers novelty on source
bookkeeping alone.
8
Limitations
The twenty games have five seeds, a single cyclic opponent order, one fixed roster, visible names,
and provider-default sampling. Response conditions differ by configuration and affect the entire
table. Removing Opus or conflicting Grok records would not recover the unaffected game that would
otherwise have occurred. We do not rank models, infer equal compute from effort settings, or infer
missing reasoning from zero reported tokens.
Trade states, prices, and continuations are selected by the players. Movement exposure, ownership
duration, and first-creditor status are endogenous. Board prices are scales, not utilities. The debt
analysis covers episodes reaching a liquidation menu and uses inherited local feasibility results; it is
not a complete policy-value analysis. No new control condition tests corrected prompts, unfamiliar
opponents, or altered rules.
Semantic codes and incident selection are retrospective and LLM-assisted. Six incidents foreground
Grok, whose visible response channels create unusual observability. Two same-family readers with
partial unblinding do not supply independent human validation. Explanations are not privileged cog-
nition measurements. A final publication requires accountable human authorship; this independent
draft invents no human byline. These limits bound the claims rather than implying that unmeasured
17

capability is absent.
9
Conclusion
The strongest supported discovery is competitive-option attention: LLM agents sometimes bargain
over the finance, development, resale, and inheritance opportunities their transactions create for
rivals. That attention appears in refusals of own completion, revised terms, a funding-denial appeal,
and negotiations over distressed estates. It coexists with generic repricing, mixed same-deed asks,
arithmetic errors, and conflicting execution. Following the surrounding economy makes the stakes
visible without declaring the strategy effective. The resulting account is a study of situated strategic
conduct, with optimal valuation, causal persuasion, learning, and model rankings left open.
