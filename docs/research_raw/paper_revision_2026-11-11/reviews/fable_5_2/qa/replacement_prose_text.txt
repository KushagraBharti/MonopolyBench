Bargaining over a rival’s next move
Strategic attention, liquidity and execution in twenty LLM Monopoly games
MonopolyBench project∗
11 November 2026
Abstract
In a competitive economy, a trade between two agents changes what each of them can then do
to everyone else. We study how four configured LLM systems handle this in twenty complete
four-player Monopoly games with free-form negotiation (6,989 decisions, 920 responses to trade
offers, 188 debt episodes). The agents bargain over what a trade would let a rival do next, and
they use different instruments for different offers. At the opening of a negotiation they reject
82% of swaps that would complete only the proposer’s color group (95 of 116), against 51% of
swaps that complete nobody (37 of 72), and the gap is present for each of the four recipients.
They refuse a cash bid for a completing deed no more often than any other cash bid (60% against
65%). They answer it with a price, and their asks to a buyer the deed would complete are higher
in eleven of twelve seller, mortgage and stage cells. In 61 of 74 refusals of an offer that would
complete the refuser’s own group, two model-assisted readings find a stated consequence for the
rival. This attention is not a calibrated valuation. Same-deed asks are mixed and several cited
quantities are wrong. It also weakens as the holder’s cash runs down, clearly for one system and
on few cases for another. Where the game supplies an explicit menu and an immediate check,
execution is reliable. Agents paid 128 of 131 payable debts, 61 of them through several actions.
The study is observational, covers five seeds and one roster, and one system played mostly under a
provider-refusal recovery condition. It ranks no model and scores no decision against an optimal
policy.

Introduction
Replaces the introduction of each draft.
A deed that completes a color group is the unit of competition in Monopoly. Its owner may
build houses, and houses multiply the rent that every other player pays. A trade that hands over
such a deed changes what the buyer can do to the seller and to the two players who are not at the
table. Economists call this an identity-dependent externality. What a sale costs the holder depends
on who the buyer is [5], and such externalities can delay agreement even when every party is fully
informed [4]. The same structure appears wherever agents trade inputs to one another’s production.
An agent that weighs only its own side of an exchange will miss it.
We ask whether LLM agents bargain over this consequence when they play complete games
against one another, which instruments they use, and what limits the behavior. The evidence is

Four results organize the paper.
The agents bargain over what a trade lets a rival do next, and the instrument depends on the offer.
A swap that would complete only the proposer’s group is refused at the opening of a negotiation far
more often than a swap that completes nobody. A cash bid for a completing deed is refused no more
often than any other cash bid. The holder answers it with a price, and holders’ asks rise when the
deed would complete the buyer. When agents refuse an offer that would complete their own group,
their messages and notes usually name what the other party would gain.
The attention is to a category. It is not a calibrated valuation. The few cases in which one seller
prices one deed for both kinds of buyer are mixed, buyers do not bid in a common direction, and
several of the quantities the agents cite are wrong. We find no graded response to the rival’s building
cash on the one cut we can form, and we report that as a null.
The guard weakens with the holder’s cash. Holders short of cash refuse fewer requests of every
kind, bring blocking deeds to market more often, and supply a disproportionate share of the sales
that complete a rival’s group. Three of the four systems still charge well above list price when they
sell this way.
Execution is reliable where the game supplies a menu and a check. Agents paid 128 of the 131 debts
that could be paid, 61 of them through two to ten consecutive actions. Open-ended follow-through,
such as building on a group after completing it, is less uniform and depends in part on response
conditions.
The paper contributes a population account of multi-player bargaining over a completion exter-
nality, split by the form of the offer and reported by seed block, recipient and response condition.
It adds the liquidity conditions under which blocking deeds are released, with every such sale and
its history. It reports all 188 debt episodes, closed on the engine’s payment and bankruptcy events.
Eight incidents with source-exact text show the mechanisms in context, including one of successful
multi-step planning.
Five boundaries apply throughout. No decision is scored against an optimal policy, because the
cohort has no value reference. A stated reason is a recorded explanation and not a measurement
of internal computation [7, 6]. The prompt invites bluffing and deception in public messages, so a
public bluff is authorized conduct. Twenty games in five seed blocks with one cyclic seating order
cannot rank four systems, and Opus played most decisions under a different response condition. The
comparisons were chosen after earlier analyses of the same games and are exploratory. The project
began from eight questions about strategic reasoning, opponent modeling, negotiation and deception,
planning, adaptation, economic risk, collective dynamics and evaluation validity, and three broader
ones. The discussion states what this cohort says about each and what it leaves unmeasured.

2.1
Refusal depends on the form of the offer
The 526 threads contain 920 responses, of which 113 accept, 394 counter and 413 reject. Acceptance
is legal for 918 offers. An opening offer is the first offer of a thread, so opening offers are one per
thread and 524 of them can legally be accepted. Recipients reject 138 of the 187 openings that would
complete only the proposer’s color group (74%) and 152 of the 238 that complete nobody (64%). The
difference is positive in each of the five seed blocks.
That comparison pools different kinds of proposal. Every proposer-completing offer asks the
recipient for a deed. The other population also holds 62 proposals that ask for none, and recipients
refuse 76% of those. Table 1 separates the openings by what the proposer puts on the table.
Swaps carry the contrast. Recipients reject 95 of the 116 swaps that would complete only the
proposer’s group and accept one. They reject 37 of the 72 swaps that complete nobody and accept
seventeen. A swap that would complete both parties’ groups falls between, at 34 of 48. The gap
between the first two rows is present for each recipient. Sol rejects 12 of 21 against 1 of 16, Gemini
45 of 49 against 25 of 33, Grok 35 of 37 against 9 of 12, and Opus 3 of 9 against 2 of 11. It is present
in each seed block.
Its size depends on one response condition. Of the 72 non-completing swaps, 29 were opened by a
Grok call whose own text named a different action, and those include 13 of the seventeen that were
accepted. Without the 64 threads opened that way, recipients reject 93 of 112 one-sided swaps (83%)
and 26 of 43 non-completing swaps (60%). The gap stays positive for each recipient and in four of the
five blocks, and the fifth has five non-completing swaps left. Counting only the first time a proposer
offers a given bundle to a given recipient in a game gives 77 of 93 and 33 of 67. Leaving out every
swap that Grok wrote gives 90 of 108 and 24 of 36. The gap is between seventeen and thirty-four
points across these treatments, and we do not fix its magnitude.
Cash bids show no such gap. Recipients reject 42 of the 70 opening cash bids for a deed that
would complete the bidder’s group and 67 of the 103 others. The share is lower for completing bids
in four of the five blocks. A holder who receives a cash bid for a completing deed usually keeps the

The contrast belongs to how negotiations start.
2.2
For cash, the response is a price
To compare prices we keep the offers that exchange cash for deeds and nothing else, and divide the
cash by the board list price of the deeds. An ask is written by the deed holder and a bid by the buyer.
List price is a fixed denominator and not a fair value.
Three systems write enough asks to compare. Across the twelve cells formed by seller, mortgage
status and thread position, the median ask to a buyer the deed would complete is higher in eleven.
The exception is Opus’s later asks for unmortgaged deeds, at 1.86 against 2.10 times list. For a single
unmortgaged deed the median ask is 1.61 times list when the buyer would still lack two deeds of the
group (3 asks), 1.80 when it would lack one (45) and 2.32 when the deed completes the group (37).
This is a direction and not a premium. Pooled over sellers, the ordering reverses in two of the
five seed blocks. In seven cases one seller asks cash for one deed, within one game, from both a
completing and a non-completing buyer. Mortgage status matches in six of them. Among those six
the completing buyer is asked more in four, less in one and the same in one, whether each sequence
of asks is summarized by its first ask or by its median. In the seventh, Opus asks $280 for an
unmortgaged Boardwalk at turn 74 and, from 78 turns later, asks between $450 and $100 for the
same deed mortgaged. That case is higher by first ask and lower by median, which is why earlier
summaries of the seven read five, one, one and four, two, one. Both cases with lower asks are Opus
selling Boardwalk while holding $210 or less. Bids move in no common direction. Sol bids more for a
deed that completes its own group and Grok bids less.
A better price also buys a completing deed less often. In 199 offers the author repeats the non-cash
bundle of its previous offer and moves cash toward the recipient. Recipients accept 9 of the 58
such concessions when the bundle would complete only the author’s group and 24 of the 86 when it
completes nobody. For concessions on offers in which the author gives no deed the counts are 3 of 34
and 13 of 43. Countering as such is not specific to completion. Among counters to offers that request
a deed, 79 of 111 to proposer-completing offers and 81 of 110 to others return the same deeds and ask
for more cash. Repricing is how these agents disagree in general. What differs for a completing deed
is that a swap is turned down at the start and that a higher cash offer less often ends the negotiation.
2.3
What the refusals say
Two earlier model-assisted readings coded the stated reasons in all 74 outright rejections of offers
that would complete the recipient’s own group. Both found a stated consequence for the other party,
such as its completion, building funds or survival, in 61 (Cohen’s κ of 0.79 between the two readings).
The count is 34 of 36 when both parties would complete and 27 of 38 when only the recipient would.
Both readings found a stated concern with the recipient’s own price or cash in 41. Gemini and Grok
write 62 of the 74 refusals, the eight from Opus carry no private note, and 31 of the 61 agreed codes
come from two games, G1 and G15. The readings come from two instances of one model family, one
partly exposed to earlier counts, and no human labeled the rows. They establish what the messages
and notes say. They do not establish why the agents refused.

2.4
Attention without calibration
We looked for a graded response to the quantity the agents cite most, the proposer’s cash for building
after the trade, and did not find one. Recipients reject 100 of 134 proposer-completing openings when
the proposer would keep the cost of a full first layer of houses, 32 of 42 when it would keep the cost
of one house, and 6 of 11 when it would keep less. The cut is weak. Gemini and Grok refuse about
nine in ten such openings at every level, the lowest cell is small, and a proposer who offers more cash
keeps less, so the price moves with the grouping. We report the absence of a visible gradient and do
not infer a decision rule from it. The explanations themselves contain errors. In G2 a note says that
$520 buys nine yellow houses when it buys three. In G15 a note treats one house on each green as
the minimum build. In G13 a pitch gives a nine-square landing as 5 in 36 on one roll when it is 4 in
36. None of these prompts states a house price, so some of the errors may be errors of recall.

The guard weakens with the holder’s cash
Replaces Fable Section 4 and 5.1, Opus 4.5 and the last paragraph of Sol 4.3. The incidents G7, G13 and G18 follow
this section.
We call an opening or later offer that would complete only its author’s group a completion request
and its recipient the holder. There are 289.
Refusal.
Among the three systems that respond under the standard condition, holders refuse 13 of
20 opening requests when they hold under $100, 17 of 24 when they hold $100 to $299 and 105 of 125
when they hold $300 or more. Later in a thread the counts are 4 of 28, 5 of 16 and 12 of 24. Cutoffs
of $50 to $200 on the low side and $300 or $500 on the high side give the same picture. The pattern
is Sol’s, with 4 of 26 requests refused under $100 against 25 of 45 at $300 or more, and on few cases
Grok’s, with 3 of 11 against 46 of 52. Nineteen of Sol’s 26 low-cash requests come from three games.
Gemini refuses 10 of 11 and 46 of 52. Opus refuses 3 of the 52 requests it receives at any level and
counters the rest.
Low cash softens refusal of other requests as well. Standard-condition holders refuse 4 of 9 opening
requests for a deed that completes nothing when they hold under $100 and 92 of 138 when they hold
$300 or more. At the opening the gap between completion requests and other requests is seventeen to
twenty-one points in each cash band. In these records the two regularities add. The low-cash cells are
too small to say whether the guard shrinks when money is short.
Who brings the deed to market.
The holder had opened the thread in 21 of the 69 requests
that reach a holder with under $100 and in 15 of the 175 that reach a holder with $300 or more.
Sales.
Forty-three settlements complete a group for one party only. In 20 of them the party
that gives up the completing deed, which we call the seller, holds under $100. Among the 57 cash
settlements that complete nobody, the party receiving cash holds under $100 in 9. Twelve of the 20
are sales by Opus, which played under action-only recovery. Without Opus as the seller the counts
are 8 of 23 against 6 of 41. Restricted to sales of deeds for cash alone they are 14 of 22 against 7 of 31,
and 6 of 11 against 4 of 22 without Opus. Cash-poor sellers are over-represented among completing
sales under each definition. They are not the whole market, since 17 of the 43 sellers held $300 or
more. All of these populations condition on a settlement having occurred.

Price.
Low cash is not a low price. The six cash-only completing sales made by Sol, Gemini or
Grok with under $100 settled at a median of 2.25 times list, and five were above list. Opus’s eight
settled at a median of 0.66 times list, and seven were below it. The cohort cannot separate Opus’s
model from its response condition.
Histories.
In 14 one-sided sales the same holder had refused the same buyer on an earlier turn.
The holder’s cash was lower at the sale in 13. The terms had also changed. The cash going to the
holder was higher at the sale in 8, equal in 2 and lower in 4, and the deeds exchanged differed in 9,
including five refused swaps that later sold for cash alone. In the 38 pairs that drew offers on more
than one turn and never settled, the holder’s cash also fell in 25. The histories are consistent with
cash pressure and do not separate it from a better offer.
What followed.
Neither side of these sales won more often than the other. The seller won the
game in 10 of the 43 and the buyer in 11. Position at the sale matters more. In 20 player-games the
seller held a complete group of its own when it sold. In 12 of those it built within eight turns, and 8
won. In 8 it did not build, and none won. Sellers without a group won 1 of 18 player-games. Building
after a sale marks a stronger position as well as a use of the money, so this describes trajectories and
estimates no effect.

Execution under an explicit menu
Replaces Astra 4.1, Sol 5.2, Opus 5.2 and Fable 6.1. The G12 incident follows the new incident below.
A player whose debt exceeds its cash receives a liquidation menu that offers mortgages, building
sales and bankruptcy, and nothing else. This happens 188 times, 167 of them for rent. Every episode
closes on an engine event. In 128 the debtor pays the stated amount, and in 60 it is eliminated. No
paid episode contains an engine default.
Of the 128 payments, 67 take one action, with a median shortfall of $35, and 61 take two to ten.
The paid episodes contain 180 mortgage actions and 89 building-sale actions, and 68 of them sell
buildings. Where the first menu offered both a mortgage and a building sale, the debtor mortgaged
first in 40 of 51 episodes. Only 2 of the 68 episodes that sold buildings did so when the mortgages on
the first menu would have covered the shortfall. The agents kept their buildings when they could.
For the 60 eliminations we compute a generous bound on what the debtor could raise, namely its
cash, the resale value of every building and the mortgage value of every unmortgaged deed. The debt
exceeds that bound in 56. Earlier engine-executed checks, which we inherit and did not rerun, find a
legal payment sequence for three of the other four. In the fourth the menu offered no building sale,
because the bank had no houses with which to break a hotel. So 131 episodes had a payment route
and the debtor took it in 128, namely 30 of 31 for Sol, 37 of 38 for Gemini, 41 of 41 for Opus and 20
of 21 for Grok. These counts describe the debts each system met and are not difficulty-adjusted rates.
Payment usually postponed elimination. In 102 of the 128 paid episodes the debtor was eliminated
later in the same game. One paid Grok episode includes an action whose accompanying text named a
different action from the applied call.

What happened. Sol sells the nine houses and both hotels in two actions for $700 and mortgages
its eight unmortgaged deeds in eight more for $665. The engine records the $1,400 rent as paid, and
Sol’s next prompt shows $14. Seven of Sol’s ten notes state the remaining shortfall, and each of those

amount and an immediate check. The liquidation prompts list the deeds that can be mortgaged and
the buildings that can be sold without their values, so both systems supplied those figures themselves.
The payment did not change who won. It is evidence of planning and execution on a defined problem,
and it says nothing about the earlier decisions that produced the debt.

Discussion
Replaces the first discussion subsection of each draft.
What the agents attend to.
The agents treat a trade as a change in what a rival will be able
to do. They say so when they refuse, and the pattern of their responses agrees with what they say.
A swap that would complete only the proposer’s group is almost never accepted at the start of a
negotiation. A cash bid for the same kind of deed is met with a price, and the price is higher for the
buyer it would complete. This is attention to public capacity. The holdings and cash that the agents
reason about are stated exactly in every prompt. The records hold no comparable evidence about
hidden beliefs or intentions, which this design does not measure.
What the attention is not.
It is not a valuation that can be read off the agents’ numbers. One
seller’s asks for one deed are mixed across buyers, bids move in no common direction, the stated
arithmetic is sometimes wrong, and we see no gradient with the rival’s building cash. Controlled
two-party work finds the same separation between modeling a counterparty and turning the model
into better agreements [1]. Our records show it in complete multi-player games, without that study’s
utility measure and without its control. Whether any refusal, price or sale was good play is a question
this cohort cannot answer.
What moves a blocking deed.
Liquidity is the largest single correlate of whether a holder refuses,
and cash-poor sellers supply a disproportionate share of completing sales. The evidence is uneven
across systems. It is clear for Sol, thin for Grok and absent for Gemini, and Opus hardly refuses
at any level. A holder short of cash still charges for the monopoly it is selling in three of the four
systems. We therefore describe liquidity as a condition under which the guard weakens. We do not
describe it as the reason blocking deeds move.
Where execution is reliable.
Paying a debt comes with a menu, a stated amount and an
immediate test, and all four systems do it well, including through long sequences that need arithmetic
the prompt does not supply. Building on a completed group, pricing a deed and timing a sale come
with none of these, and behavior there is less uniform and harder to judge. The contrast is between
kinds of task and should not be read as a contrast between systems.

Related work
One paragraph to add. No draft cites this line of work.
Refusing to trade with a rival who is close to winning is an established heuristic for agents in
multi-player trading games. Guhe and Lascarides study symbolic agents in The Settlers of Catan [2].
The JSettlers agents they build on already stop trading with any player who has eight or more victory
points, a behavior the authors switch off for their experiments. They add explicit moves that embargo
a player or block one resource, with examples such as “Don’t give him ore, because then he’ll build
a city and we’re all doomed”, impose compliance by the other players, and measure win rates over
10,000 simulated games per condition. An embargo helps most when the leader reaches seven points
and is too late at eight. Denial of a rival’s opportunity is therefore not new to game-playing agents.
Our agents were given no such move and no rule text. They state the consideration in free language,
apply it to swaps and prices, and comply with one another’s appeals only in part. The contribution
of these records is the unprompted appearance of the behavior in LLM play, the instruments through
which it is expressed, and its dependence on liquidity. On liquidity, FinBoardBench reports that
models favor acquiring assets over keeping cash [3]. Our records concern the next step, which is what
a holder does with a blocking deed once cash is short.

Limitations
One paragraph to add to the consolidated limitations.
The split of opening offers by form, the cash bands and the pair histories were chosen after earlier
analyses of the same twenty games, and no games were held out. They describe this cohort. The
swap contrast is sensitive to how offers opened by a conflicting Grok call are treated, the low-cash
cells hold between 9 and 28 offers, and the liquidity pattern rests largely on one system. In 13 of the
21 pairs of game and recipient that hold both kinds of opening swap the two rejection shares are
equal, so the pooled contrast draws mostly on differences between games and recipients.
