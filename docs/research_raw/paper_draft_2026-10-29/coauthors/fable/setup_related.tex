\section{Environment, instructions and evaluated agents}
\label{sec:environment}

MonopolyBench is a four-player implementation of Monopoly in which a deterministic rules engine owns the state, one language-model agent occupies each seat, and every delivered observation, response attempt, applied action and state transition is written to disk. This section describes the parts of that configuration on which later interpretation depends: the economic rules actually enforced, the information and instructions each agent received, the four configured participants and the way their responses were turned into actions, and the records that make a game inspectable afterwards. All statements refer to the frozen engine and the saved run configurations of the twenty cohort games, not to tabletop convention.

\subsection{Economy and rule variant}
\label{sec:rules}

The board is the standard forty-space United States layout with its published property names, prices and color groups. Each player starts with \$1,500, collects \$200 for passing Go, pays \$200 income tax and \$100 luxury tax, and draws from the two usual card decks, which include a Get Out of Jail Free card. Free Parking pays nothing. A player sent to jail may try to roll doubles on up to three turns and must then pay the \$50 fine; three consecutive doubles in one turn also send a player to jail. All randomness, dice and decks alike, comes from a seeded generator, and event timestamps are synthetic, so wall-clock time and provider latency never enter game progression.

\paragraph{Acquisition.} A player landing on an unowned property either buys it at list price or opens an auction. The auction is an open ascending auction in which the remaining players, in seat order after the initiator, bid or drop out until one bidder remains; a property that receives no bid stays unowned and is never forced back to auction later. Trades are structured offers of cash, deeds and jail cards that a player proposes during its own post-turn management phase; the counterparty accepts, rejects or counters, a thread expires after twenty exchanges, and a settlement is applied atomically by the engine. The recipient of a mortgaged deed pays interest of ten percent of the mortgage value, rounded up, at settlement. No deed in a color group that carries any building can be traded, including undeveloped deeds in that group.

\paragraph{Development.} Houses and hotels can be bought only on a complete color group during the owner's post-turn phase. A build request is an ordered list of purchases, and the engine validates evenness and bank inventory after every entry; a plan whose end state is even but whose order is not is rejected as a whole. The bank holds 32 houses and 12 hotels. A hotel replaces four houses and returns them to the bank; selling a hotel requires four houses in the bank to downgrade, and selling buildings returns half their construction cost. When the bank has no houses, an immediate house purchase is blocked and a hotel cannot be sold back; houses can still return to the bank through an owner's own sales or through a hotel conversion on another group, so the restriction binds the next purchase rather than all further development.

\paragraph{Financing and insolvency.} Mortgaging an undeveloped deed yields half its price. Redemption is implemented as the ceiling of 1.1 times that principal evaluated in floating-point arithmetic, which charges one dollar more than the exact ten percent for five of the sixteen board principals (for example \$111 rather than \$110 to redeem a \$100 mortgage); the ten-percent trade interest is exact for every board value. When a payment exceeds a player's cash, the engine opens a liquidation decision whose legal actions are to mortgage a deed, to sell buildings or to declare bankruptcy; trading is not available in this phase. Selling the buildings of a group makes its deeds mortgageable within the same liquidation, so debt-time finance is a sequence of legal actions rather than a single number read from the current menu. A declaration of bankruptcy is accepted even when financing remains, which is the sense in which voluntary bankruptcy is surrender. If a debtor has neither a mortgageable deed nor a sellable building, or cannot pay the jail fine after a third failed roll, the engine applies the elimination without a further decision; four of the sixty cohort eliminations were automatic in this sense.

\paragraph{Bankruptcy transfer.} When the creditor is another player, that player receives every deed intact, with its houses, hotels and mortgage flags, together with the debtor's remaining cash and jail cards, and pays no transfer interest. When the creditor is the bank, buildings return to stock and the deeds become unowned without an auction. The game has no turn cap and ends when one non-bankrupt player remains; all twenty cohort games ended this way, after between 75 and 386 global turns.

The retained ruleset, versioned as \texttt{audit\_corrected\_v2} in the saved run configurations, therefore departs from the commonly published rules in several declared ways: creditors inherit developed property intact and pay no mortgage interest on it, bank-returned deeds are not auctioned, scarce buildings trigger no competing-purchase auction, surrender is permitted while finance remains, and a player who stays in jail receives no management phase that turn. We describe the environment as a declared Monopoly variant rather than as rules-complete classic Monopoly. Table~\ref{tab:rules} connects each mechanic to the analysis it enables or complicates.

\subsection{Information and normative contract}
\label{sec:contract}

At each of six decision types, namely buy-or-auction, auction bid, jail, post-turn management, trade response and liquidation, the agent receives three inputs. The system prompt is identical for every initial request in the cohort. The game-state payload lists, for the acting player and for each opponent, cash, board position, jail status, jail cards and holdings with houses, hotel and mortgage flags, together with the bank's remaining houses and hotels and the unowned spaces; player names in this payload are the configured model names, so every agent can identify which models it is playing. The action payload gives the decision type, decision-specific context such as the pending offer, auction standing or the amount owed and the shortfall, and the legal actions with their argument schemas. The agent never sees deck order, future dice, other players' private notes or the full event log.

Two memory channels travel with the state. A shared public timeline holds the twenty most recent summarized items, each derived from events together with the public message attached to the action that produced them. A private memory holds the agent's own ten most recent private notes. Each action must carry both a public message and a private thought; the public message is visible to all players through the timeline, and the private thought returns only to its author. Because the timeline is a summary rather than a transcript, delivery is not guaranteed. Of the 6,882 non-empty public messages in the cohort, 149 never entered the shared timeline of any later initial prompt: 94 were dropped when consecutive building entries were merged into one item and 55 were attached to events the timeline does not summarize. A further rendering defect in trade history, affecting 2,054 of the 6,989 initial prompts in at least one of three manifestations, could present the parties of an earlier offer with their roles reversed. These facts matter for social interpretation, where exposure must be traced through the prompt actually delivered at the recipient's decision rather than assumed from a logged utterance.

The instructions define the normative contract against which conduct can be judged. The system prompt tells each agent to win by being the last non-bankrupt player, permits ``any legal strategy or personality'' including deceptive, manipulative, cooperative and opportunistic play, and states that ``deception in public chat is allowed; private thoughts should reflect true reasoning''. It asks for consistency ``unless there is a reason to change''. Three different objects follow. A false public claim is authorized competitive conduct, so it is recorded and analyzed but is not an instruction violation. A factually incorrect private note is not by itself a breach: the instruction asks that private thoughts reflect the agent's true reasoning, not that they be accurate, so a breach would require evidence that the note misrepresents the reasoning behind the applied action. Such evidence is confounded with error, stale context and channel conflict, the note is also fed back as the author's own memory, and no checked case establishes a knowing misrepresentation. The request applies only under the standard condition: the action-only recovery prompt used after a provider refusal removes both the private-note field and the sentence asking that private thoughts reflect true reasoning, so most of Opus's decisions were resolved without the private-honesty clause (Section~\ref{sec:participants}). An illegal action cannot be applied at all: the engine validates every response against the legal menu, issues one corrective retry with the validation errors, and otherwise applies a deterministic fallback. Attempted invalid actions are recorded as validation errors, not misconduct.

Table~\ref{tab:contracts} places this contract beside the two closest comparators, using their published instructions or coding schemes. The same act carries a different normative status in each environment, so rates and labels do not transfer between them. AgentMonopoly encourages bluffing but prohibits public messages that contradict the action taken~\cite{wu2026agentmonopoly}; the Vending-Bench Arena communication study gives its agents no conduct instruction and classifies misalignment with an external codebook~\cite{li2026arena}. A declared norm allows descriptive statements about adherence without randomization; a causal comparison between norms would need a controlled design.

\subsection{Configured participants and action resolution}
\label{sec:participants}

The four participants are GPT-6.1 Sol (\texttt{openai/gpt-6.1-sol}), Gemini 4 Argon (\texttt{google/gemini-4-argon}), Claude Opus 5.5 (\texttt{anthropic/claude-opus-5.5}, configured with \texttt{anthropic/claude-opus-4.8} as a named refusal fallback) and Grok 4.7 (\texttt{x-ai/grok-4.7}); we call them Sol, Gemini, Opus and Grok. Every request went through the OpenRouter gateway with the provider pinned (OpenAI, Google AI Studio, Anthropic and xAI respectively) and provider fallbacks disabled. All four requested the provider-native reasoning effort ``medium''; no temperature or nucleus parameter was set, so provider defaults applied. ``Medium'' is a label, not a matched budget: completion tokens per decision ranged from 128 for Sol to 4,041 for Grok, and mean latency per decision from 4.8 to 65.0 seconds. The unit of evaluation is therefore the configured agent, that is, the model together with its inference settings, observation format, memory, tool-call handling and response-resolution policy~\cite{kapoor2026hal,zhang2026dece}.

Each decision proceeds from one or more attempts to exactly one applied action. The resolver validates the returned tool call against the legal menu and argument schema. An invalid or illogical response receives one corrective retry carrying the validation errors; a second failure triggers the deterministic fallback policy, which attempts mortgage or building sales before bankruptcy when a payment is due and otherwise selects a safe legal action. The cohort contains 8,679 attempts for 6,989 decisions and 66 fallback actions (Opus 61, Sol 4, Gemini 1, Grok 0). Each attempt is saved with its own prompt, tools, raw response, requested and returned model, response condition and cost.

Two participants played under conditions that the other two did not. First, the provider refused 1,605 of Opus's attempts, spread over 1,605 of its 1,696 decisions; in 1,604 of these the refusal was the first response, and in one (\decision{2}{129}) it followed an invalid first attempt. The refusal text cites a terms-of-service restriction on reproducing or reverse-engineering model outputs, and the triggering feature of the prompt has not been localized. The configured recovery retried the same model with the private-thought requirement removed from the system prompt and tool schema (1,604 such chains), with Claude Opus 4.8 as a further action-only stage that was never physically invoked. Under this condition an invalid action-only response proceeds directly to the deterministic fallback, because the refusal consumed the corrective retry; this happened in 60 decisions and accounts for almost all of Opus's 61 fallbacks. Because recovered decisions add no private note, Opus's delivered private memory was empty in 1,108 and contained only synthetic fallback notes in 286 of its 1,696 decisions. The refusal share was 0.57 to 0.76 in games 1--4 and 1.00 in games 5--20 under an identical initial system-prompt hash; nothing in the record shows a moral objection to the game or to its permitted deception. Second, Grok was the only participant queried with forced tool choice, because Opus rejects forced tool use and the other models retained their default setting. In 139 of Grok's 1,663 decisions the received tool call names a different action from the call written in the accompanying text; in 15 of these the parsed arguments are otherwise equal, and in 5 the serialized argument strings are identical. The applied action remains authoritative in every case. The count is a diagnostic of channel disagreement under one configuration, not a measure of deception and not attributable to a particular component of the provider or gateway path. Table~\ref{tab:conditions} summarizes the four conditions.

The cohort consists of five seeds, each played under the four cyclic rotations of one roster, so that every participant occupies each of seats 1--4 five times. There is one realization per seed--rotation cell. Games 1--4 ran from source commit \texttt{42f85183} and games 5--20 from \texttt{d6808534}; the eighteen-file difference between them touches campaign-concurrency plumbing, the user interface, documentation and tests, and no engine, prompt, resolver, player-configuration or model-client file. The two saved treatment fingerprints therefore coincide with the first seed block, as does the shift in Opus's refusal share, and neither a behavioral effect nor its absence follows from the source difference.

\subsection{Artifact and replay contract}
\label{sec:artifacts}

Each saved game contains the append-only event stream, the applied-action stream, every decision with its legal menu and state snapshot, per-turn state files, a prompt and response record for every attempt indexed by decision and attempt, usage and cost per physical call, a summary and a replay report. The cohort holds 50,918 events. Applied actions are authoritative; response text, private notes and provider reasoning fields are evidence of what a response stated, not of why an action was taken~\cite{turpin2023unfaithful,lanham2023faithfulness}.

Five replay operations differ in what they establish, and we keep them apart. Full event replay regenerates a game from its seed, settings and applied actions and compares the event stream; it passed for all twenty games. Memory reconstruction rebuilds the delivered public and private memory from events and matched the saved prompt memory at all 6,989 decisions. Exact decision-prefix reset restores the live engine and memory at a chosen decision; at the twenty prefixes tested, one per game, the prefix events, memory and recorded continuation were reproduced and a different legal action produced a divergent continuation. Local legal-alternative tests, described in the analysis protocol, execute a specified sequence of the acting player's own legal actions from such a prefix and stop at a declared endpoint; they establish that a sequence is legal and what it yields, not that it is optimal or what opponents would do. Fresh policy continuation runs scripted reference policies from a prefix; the components exist and were exercised, nineteen of twenty reference rollouts ended in bankruptcy and one reached its decision budget, so termination is an observed property of this cohort rather than a guarantee. A packaged branching interface, reward definition, reinforcement-learning adapter and any training result do not exist in this work. The repository also contains 130 fixed-state micro scenarios with a legality-validating runner; they produced no cohort result and are not used here.

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Long-horizon economic agents.} Vending-Bench framed sustained economic operation as a test of long-horizon coherence and of the ability to acquire capital, with a single agent, a net-worth score and large run-to-run variance~\cite{backlund2025vendingbench}. Its successors are author-maintained evaluation pages with live leaderboards rather than frozen studies: Vending-Bench 2 lengthens single-agent operation and lists current frontier models, including Gemini 4 Argon and Grok 4.7, on a live leaderboard, and Vending-Bench Arena places three or four agents at one location who email, transfer money and trade goods while scored individually~\cite{andon2026vb2,andon2026arena}. We cite those pages for context only and do not compare a live leaderboard with a historical cohort. The Arena communication study is the closest published work on conduct~\cite{li2026arena}. Across 20 one-year runs, 79 agent-runs and 2,583 emails from 13 models, an LLM judge classifies each email under a misalignment codebook, a deterministic stage verifies factual claims against pricing, transfer and inventory logs, and a third stage audits reasoning summaries; 12.58\% of emails are coded misaligned, transfer promises in misaligned emails are enacted more often than those in neutral ones, validation rests on a single author labeler, and the environment is not redistributed. The study shows that state-grounded verification of speech and promise follow-through is an established method. Our contribution relative to it is not a competing rate. Our agents received an explicit contract that authorizes public deception, so we report proposition-level chronologies against that contract rather than an externally coded misalignment class; our legality is engine-enforced with recorded menus; our economic claims are tested by executing legal alternatives at the historical state; and our adjudication is performed by four model coauthors working from decision identifiers, which is also not human annotation.

\paragraph{Monopoly and board-game economics.} AgentMonopoly is the closest environment~\cite{wu2026agentmonopoly,agentmonopoly2026repo}. It runs eight open-weight models of 8B to 32B parameters at temperature zero over seeds and turn orders, logs a private explanation, a public message and an action per decision, lists legal actions in the prompt, enforces rules in a deterministic engine, offers an interactive replay mode, and explicitly declines to treat generated explanations as evidence of intent. Its paper's table totals 140 games while the inspected repository README totals 120; we leave that discrepancy visible. Three differences in rules and contract define what our results can add. Its games stop at 150 steps with a net-worth winner, ours end only by bankruptcy. Its players are eliminated when cash falls below zero without a mortgage or sale step, so the debt-time liquidation that produces our accessible-versus-nominal finance findings does not exist there by rule; conversely, its bankruptcies do not pass developed property intact. Its prompt prohibits a public message that contradicts the action, whereas ours does not, so a say/do discrepancy is a measured violation there and an authorized tactic here. FinBoardBench evaluates nine focal models against three fixed opponents across Cashflow, Acquire and Monopoly in 108 matches, rotates the focal seat, uses an LLM judge to rate each trade from the full state, reports Monopoly investment profiles including a return-on-investment summary, and applies classic bankruptcy in which buildings are sold to the bank~\cite{hu2026finboardbench}. We evaluate all four seats, measure trades by deterministic set completion and bilateral before-and-after state rather than judged quality, and decline to label developed-rent receipts a return precisely because our variant passes buildings to the creditor. DECE evaluates nine models in twelve model--harness configurations across ten Eurogames with legal-action interfaces, persisted state and replay, and shows in matched studies that the same model's measured performance depends on its execution harness~\cite{zhang2026dece}. We adopt its conclusion that the harness is part of the evaluated object and declare our per-agent conditions accordingly; we did not run its controlled harness comparisons, and its games have no dialogue. Cattle Trade combines auctions, hidden-offer trades and bargaining over 242 short games and finds that two heuristic agents outperform most of its seven language models~\cite{muller2026cattletrade}; our six scripted reference policies make no substantive trade proposals, so we cannot reproduce that baseline calibration in the present cohort. Table~\ref{tab:comparison} summarizes the inspected properties; a cell marked ``n.r.'' records that the property was not reported in the sections we inspected and asserts nothing about its absence.

\paragraph{Social and bargaining environments.} SOTOPIA evaluates social goals in role-played scenarios with an explicit evaluation framework and a judge~\cite{zhou2024sotopia}; we borrow its insistence on stating the social goal and the evaluation criterion, but our goals are economic and our consequences are engine-adjudicated. NegotiationArena measures bargaining tactics in isolated games and reports that feigned desperation improves payoffs against GPT-4~\cite{bianchi2024negotiationarena}; in our setting a pitch is followed by a settlement inside a persistent portfolio, and we do not infer a persuasion effect from the sequence. Concordia adjudicates generative agent-based models through a language game master~\cite{vezhnevets2023concordia}, which is the design we did not choose: our rules are enforced by code, and language affects outcomes only through legal actions. MACHIAVELLI measures task reward and ethically relevant behavior separately in text games, with model-generated annotations~\cite{pan2023machiavelli}; we likewise keep economic outcome and conduct on separate axes, with the game's own instructions as the norm. Four recent studies concern promises. Two workshop papers find frequent, often premeditated deviation from public promises in abstract repeated games~\cite{shi2026cheaptalk,shi2026agentslie}; Empty Commitments studies promises that an agent's runtime cannot execute, with a checker validated against human labels~\cite{empty_commitments_v2}; and Who May Release an Agent's Promise defines standing over commitments in multi-party chat~\cite{commitment_standing}. Our environment has no commitment object: assurances are free text and every accepted trade executes. A standing offer later withdrawn is therefore a chronology with conditions, expiry and observed reliance to be established case by case, not a breach rate.

\paragraph{Capability, alignment and explanations.} Whether undesirable conduct rises with capability is unsettled and design-dependent. The Arena study reports no association with its capability ordering~\cite{li2026arena}; Emergent Collusion reports that, within one model family, more capable models reach a protocol violation earlier~\cite{shi2026emergentcollusion}; MASK separates honesty from accuracy and finds that scale improves the latter but not the former~\cite{ren2025mask}; Safetywashing shows that many safety scores mainly track capability~\cite{ren2024safetywashing}. Four configured agents from four providers at one time point add no point to that curve, and we do not present win counts as alignment evidence. Studies of explanation faithfulness motivate our treatment of model text as a self-report rather than as privileged access to computation~\cite{turpin2023unfaithful,lanham2023faithfulness}, and the monitorability literature motivates recording channel disagreements without treating a count of them as a validated monitor score~\cite{korbak2025monitorability}.

\paragraph{Agent evaluation infrastructure.} Interactive agent benchmarks established reproducible environments with execution-based or state-based checks, from AgentBench's eight environments~\cite{liu2024agentbench} through WebArena and OSWorld~\cite{zhou2024webarena,xie2024osworld} to the consequential workplace tasks of TheAgentCompany~\cite{xu2025theagentcompany}; $\tau$-bench adds policy adherence and reliability across repeated trials~\cite{yao2025taubench}, AgentDojo an explicit attacker and trust boundary~\cite{debenedetti2024agentdojo}, and MLE-bench resource budgets and contamination controls~\cite{chan2025mlebench}. We take from them that a sandbox's value lies in what it lets a researcher check, and that one realized game per cell is not a reliability estimate. AgentBoard's process metrics~\cite{ma2024agentboard} and the Holistic Agent Leaderboard's rollout inspection~\cite{kapoor2026hal} are precedents for trajectory-level analysis, including analysis assisted by language models under stated validation limits. Finally, METR's time horizon is a human task-duration construct~\cite{kwa2025timehorizons,metr2026page}; our horizons are game turns, decisions, attempts and tokens, and we do not convert between the two.

\section{Limitations and release status}
\label{sec:limitations}

\paragraph{Design.} The cohort is one roster of four configured agents in five seed blocks with one realization per seed--rotation cell. Games within a block share raw dice prefixes but not opportunities, decisions within a game are dependent, and the eventual winner's record is a selected endpoint. We therefore report counts with explicit denominators and avoid decision-level significance tests; seat 1--2 winners in 14 of 20 games and Sol's eight wins describe this cohort and neither establishes a seat effect nor an intrinsic ranking. Each participant's opponents were always the same three models, whose names were visible in the delivered state, so no result separates a model from the field it faced.

\paragraph{Environment.} Monopoly is widely documented, and prior familiarity with its rules and heuristics may shape behavior in ways a novel economy would not; several checked rule misstatements show that familiarity is also imperfect. The declared variant, in particular intact creditor inheritance and the absence of forced auctions, changes what rent, estate value and surrender mean relative to other Monopoly studies, and our numbers should not be pooled with theirs. Opportunities are endogenous: a trade, a jail term or an elimination changes who can act, so conditional rates always sit beside whole trajectories.

\paragraph{Interface conditions.} The four agents did not play under one condition. Opus played 1,604 decisions without a private note after provider refusals whose cause is unlocalized, and its 61 fallbacks arise from a recovery path that leaves no corrective retry; Grok alone used forced tool choice, under which 139 received calls disagree with their accompanying text. ``Medium'' effort does not equalize compute, sampling parameters were provider defaults, and recorded cost is a lower bound. Three harness defects affected what agents saw or what the record says they said: the trade-history rendering defect, the omission of 149 non-empty public messages from the shared timeline, and, in one checked liquidation prompt, a single named creditor for a pay-each-player card; one checked public message also contained a private-note string. We report their demonstrated consequences and scope and do not attribute later decisions to them without a case.

\paragraph{Measurement.} The economic definitions, the social review categories and the sample of unflagged decisions were developed during exploration of this cohort and are not preregistered. Extraction was performed by language models whose identities are recorded, and adjudication by four model coauthors who debated with decision identifiers; no human gold-standard annotation exists, and agreement among us is not verification. Social findings are source-grounded cases with counterexamples, not prevalence rates, because channel availability differs by agent and a cross-model deception rate would be undefined. A legal-alternative test proves that a sequence was executable and what it yields; it does not show that the agent should have chosen it, how opponents would have replied or who would have won. No causal persuasion effect, coalition, kingmaking or knowing private falsehood is identified.

\paragraph{Scope.} There is no human baseline, no training or reinforcement-learning result, no monitor evaluation and no evidence of transfer to real-world economic or safety settings. The capability--alignment question that motivates the work is not answered by it; the prospective design in the discussion states what would be needed.

\paragraph{Release status.} At the time of writing, the twenty saved games with their events, actions, decisions, per-attempt prompts and responses, per-turn state and usage records, together with the audit scripts and their outputs, reside in the project repository under its saved-games and research directories, and the original per-game archives are held outside the repository. A public release package with a hash manifest, a clean-environment reproduction check and a privacy review has not yet been produced. We will state the final release location, commit identifier and manifest hash before publication, and any claim in this paper that depends on a reader reproducing a number should be read as conditional on that release.
