Financial Assessment and Strategic Bargaining by LLM Agents
in Twenty Complete Monopoly Games
Kushagra Bharti
November 2026
Abstract
How do language-model agents assess resources that
become usable after an action, and account for the op-
portunities they give other players? We examine twenty
complete four-player Monopoly games through all 188
financing episodes, 920 offer responses and eight con-
textual incidents. The agents pay 128 of 131 payable
debts. Source analysis shows that uninterrupted valid
financing suffices for these historical payable states, mak-
ing the decision to stop more informative than payment
alone. Sixteen debts require mortgages released by sell-
ing buildings. The fourteen successful initial responses
include explicit dependencies, ordered routes, inclusive
budgets and one tentative calculation. Both failures cor-
rectly add an incomplete resource inventory and declare
bankruptcy; a third payable declaration elsewhere contra-
dicts the same response’s payment assessment. Negotia-
tion records show distinct response styles, stated concern
about rival development, a settled bluff and bargaining
over future estates. An exploratory rejection contrast is
substantially reduced by proposer composition. These
findings describe how economic assessments, commu-
nication and applied actions relate within play. They
support successful application of familiar financial rules
and specific strategic tactics, while leaving their causal ef-
fectiveness unresolved. Complete source-linked records
expose the scope of each conclusion, including different
response conditions across the four systems.
1
Introduction
A property is more than its purchase price. Completing
a group can enable construction; selling its buildings can
enable mortgages; financing an opponent can prevent a
stronger player from inheriting it. These relationships
connect local financial decisions to strategic interaction.
A language-model agent playing a complete game must
operate within them while communicating, maintaining
a portfolio and responding to consequences over many
turns.
Monopoly offers a useful observational setting because
monetary obligations, holdings and legal actions can
be reconstructed. It also poses an interpretive problem.
Winning combines decisions, opponents and chance, and
completing a sequence through refreshed menus need
not demonstrate a prior plan. Conversely, a written bud-
get can expose a relevant dependency even when sev-
eral reactive action orders would also succeed. Studying
the relation between assessment and action therefore re-
quires the delivered state, the agent’s actual response
and the executed continuation.
We study twenty complete games played by four con-
figured LLM systems. The question is how their ex-
pressed economic assessments correspond to available
resources, bargaining choices and applied actions. We ex-
amine every financing episode and offer response, then
use eight selected incidents to explain particular tactics.
The contributions are:
1. A complete financial census with verified feasibility
categories and a structural account of what successful
execution requires. It separates resource assessment
and stopping from sequence completion.
2. An analysis of all sixteen obligations requiring newly
available collateral, including both returned text
channels, successful selective financing and the differ-
ent mechanisms behind three payable declarations.
3. A complete negotiation census and contextual evi-
dence of rival-development concerns, bargaining lim-
its, selective disclosure and estate-related incentives,
with proposer composition made explicit.
The result is an observational account of economic con-
duct under the recorded configurations. It is not a model
ranking. Appendix F maps the evidence to broader ques-
tions about strategy, memory, learning and multi-agent
capability, distinguishing those this cohort can address
from those it cannot.

2
Study design
2.1
Game, information and response con-
tract
Four players begin with $1,500 on the US Monopoly
board. They buy, auction, trade and develop proper-
ties, with bilateral negotiations allowing up to twenty
exchanges. The rules engine produces legal action menus
and applies valid structured tool calls. It refreshes the
financing menu after every sale or mortgage and auto-
matically pays the current obligation once cash suffices.
The financing instruction permits bankruptcy when the
player “cannot or should not raise enough cash.” Thus
declaring is allowed even when payment is possible.
The implementation retains the familiar rule that build-
ings must be removed before the group’s deeds become
mortgageable. It also differs from the published rules [5]:
buildings pass intact to a player-creditor, inherited mort-
gages incur no immediate interest, and hotel-sale avail-
ability depends on bank house stock. These variants are
not enumerated in the system prompt. Rules and the his-
torical feasibility checks are documented in Appendix G;
inference about an agent’s financial beliefs must use the
rules it could have understood as well as those actually
enforced.
Requests contain current cash, holdings, buildings,
mortgages and bank stock, a twenty-entry public time-
line, up to ten own notes, and the current legal menu.
Trade responses also receive the proposed terms. Pur-
chase prompts show board prices and rents; liquidation
and trade states identify deeds by key without their fi-
nancial values. Earlier notes and timeline entries can
contain those values. The prompt permits bluffing and
deception in public communication and requests hon-
est private notes. Such conduct is authorized play, not
evidence of unprompted misalignment.
A private note is an argument in the same tool call as
the selected action. Assistant content is separate prose
accompanying that call. The gateway can also return a
readable reasoning field, labeled as a summary for Sol
and Grok and as reasoning text for Gemini and Opus.
We call it the returned explanation; its completeness is
unknown. These fields can disagree. A valid received
call determines the applied action. Neither the note’s
position in JSON nor an explanation accompanying the
action establishes an independent forecast or the model’s
internal computational order [10, 14].

3
Financial assessment and the deci-
sion to continue
3.1
What the complete census establishes
Of 188 financing episodes, 128 end in payment and sixty
in elimination. Payment uses 180 mortgages and 89 sale
actions to meet $51,997 in obligations. Sixty-seven pay-
ments take one action; sixty-one take two to ten. No suc-
cessful episode uses an engine fallback. Three declared
positions are also payable. Fifty-six other eliminations
have U∗< D; in the last, G7 Opus, nominal resources
of $1,310 exceed a $750 debt but only $440 is accessible
through the supplied menu.
The menu provides substantial assistance. For all 131
historical payable entries, source analysis shows that
an uninterrupted sequence of valid, strictly cash-raising
sales or mortgages must eventually pay if the player does
not declare. Before payment, cash plus remaining salvage
and mortgage principal is invariant; each action reduces
a finite stock of financing resources. Recorded owner-
ship, development and bank-stock conditions prevent
an unpaid dead end. Appendix C gives the proof and
its coverage, including the two hotel-stock cases requir-
ing a separate argument. This is a source-based result,
not a measured policy run, and excludes invalid calls,
exhausted call budgets and later obligations.
Consequently, 128 payments do not demonstrate that
agents solved difficult action-order problems. They es-
tablish realized financing under an assisting interface.
The more discriminating observations are their financial
inventories, decisions to stop, selected assets and agree-
ment between text and calls.

3.2
Resources beyond the initial menu
The sixteen necessity episodes contain eleven authored
notes and five Opus returned explanations. Table 3 sepa-
rates the evidence those fields provide. Five successful
texts state the release dependency, three specify an or-
dered route, five include the resource in a budget, and
one raises it tentatively. These categories distinguish lin-
guistic evidence from the binary presence of an asset in
a sum. Both failures omit the resource. All sixteen also
have their other available text channels printed in the
supplement.
The successful responses apply familiar Monopoly
knowledge to the current portfolio. For example, Gemini
in G13 d195 starts with $168 against $750 owed. Its note
specifies selling Boardwalk’s house for $100, mortgag-
ing Park Place for $175 and three other deeds for $335
while leaving Boardwalk and Kentucky unmortgaged.
The applied sequence follows that selection and leaves
$28. This is a concrete asset-preservation preference and
sufficient route, without a claim that it minimizes cost or
maximizes future winning chances.
Successful assessment is not uniformly exact. Sol’s G2
d216 note values the yellow mortgages at $430 rather than
$400 but still gives a sufficient route. Opus’s G20 d291 ex-
planation tentatively considers the pink mortgages with
muddled intermediate arithmetic; the executed sequence
pays and leaves $25. Several statements compute max-
imum resources while the action sequence stops after
raising less. Those distinctions matter when interpreting
a text as a plan.
Incident 1: exact valuation across a long sequence.
In
G20 d327 Sol faces $1,400 rent with $49 cash, nine orange
houses and two brown hotels. Its note gives $700 salvage,
$665 mortgage principal and a final $14. Two bundled
sales and eight mortgages realize that remainder. Gemini
had independently stated the same total before setting
the rent, though its component errors offset by $25. Be-
cause the smallest remaining asset increment exceeds
$14, full liquidation is forced if Sol pays; ten actions do
not imply ten difficult route choices. The evidence is ex-
act portfolio valuation, a stated target and its realization

before four successes as well as the two failures. The
repeated $130 and $30 figures in failed sums are true
values of initially listed mortgages; their recurrence alone
does not establish harmful memory anchoring.
3.3
Stopping, continuing and conflicting re-
sponses
The third payable declaration, G12 Grok d249, is not an
omitted-resource case. Grok’s note recognizes a sale-
based route and its assistant content names a sale, while
its received tool call declares. The legal route would pay
the $250 debt by selling five pink houses. Distinguishing
a false budget from a conflicting response preserves their
different implications for agent design.
The converse cases also matter.
Six above-bound
episodes start financing rather than declaring immedi-
ately. Five are Grok calls that conflict with the declaration
named elsewhere in the response. In the sixth, G18 Gem-
ini d344 mortgages to impose a ten-percent transfer fee on
its creditor. That fee belongs to the published rulebook
but is absent from this implementation. This is a rule
mismatch in a stated strategic motive, not simply incor-
rect recollection. All six are documented alongside the
payable declaration in Appendix D. A broader scan finds
nine named-action conflicts in 338 liquidation decisions,

4
Bargaining over another player’s
opportunities
4.1
The complete response population
The 526 threads contain 113 acceptances, 394 coun-
ters and 413 rejections. Acceptance is legal in 918 of
920 response opportunities. Sol and Opus counter in
140/235 and 141/189 responses; Gemini and Grok reject
in 180/242 and 149/254. Each accepts 26–30 offers. Simi-
lar agreement totals therefore coexist with very different
response styles.
Opening swaps completing only the proposer receive
95/116 rejections, compared with 37/72 completing nei-
ther player. Proposer composition accounts for much of
the 30.5-point difference: Grok authors half the compar-
ison swaps and fifteen of their seventeen acceptances;
thirteen originate in calls whose content names another
action. Without Grok-authored offers, the gap is 16.7
points and game/block bootstrap percentile intervals in-
clude zero. This is an exploratory association among
non-equivalent offers, not an identified response to com-
pletion.
Pure cash bids show a pooled difference of −5.0 points;
within-recipient differences range from −0.2 to +8.5.
Opus authors 110 of these 173 cash bids, so the forms also
differ in proposer composition. Later swaps are selected
by preceding bargaining. None of these cuts isolates
how a particular player would respond if only the rival’s
future opportunity changed.
Statements provide a separate source of evidence.
Two Astra readings agree on explicit trade-linked rival
consequences in 61 of 74 legally acceptable recipient-
completing refusals: 27/38 recipient-only and 34/36
both-completing. This population is disjoint from the
proposer-only/neither comparison. It documents the
considerations agents express, not a semantic explana-
tion of that contrast.
4.2
Development,
blocking
and
asking
prices
Incident 2: declining one’s own monopoly.
In G15,
Sol offers two greens for Grok’s Ventnor and $100, com-
pleting both groups. Grok refuses while citing Sol’s re-
sulting yellow development and its own lack of green-
building reserves. Two improved offers also fail. Grok
had recorded its blocking policy thirty turns earlier, be-
fore a delivered public warning from Opus. The later re-
fusals thus follow an existing policy; the warning cannot
be credited with persuading it. Grok’s blockers eventu-
ally pass to Gemini through bankruptcy, and Sol never
completes either group. The case establishes expressed
concern about relative consequences, not the optimality
of refusal.
Incident 3: pricing control of yellow.
In G2, Sol asks
non-completer Grok $450 for Marvin Gardens, then asks
completing buyer Opus $650. Opus publicly calls $450
final while retaining a $475 private ceiling; no trade set-
tles. Sol had already stated a blocking policy before Grok
publicly warned against the sale. Its estimate of Opus’s
building capacity overstates what $520 buys, illustrating
why attention to development and accurate valuation
must be distinguished. Later, cash-poor Opus sells its
two yellows to Sol. Sol develops and then liquidates
them, receives $44 undeveloped rent but no developed
yellow rent, and loses them to Gemini. An apparent com-
pletion opportunity is only one stage of a longer financing
history.
Prices cannot be reduced to a stable completion pre-
mium in this cohort. Six same-game, same-seller, same-
deed comparisons retain mortgage status: four ask more
of the completing buyer, one less and one the same.
The seventh changes mortgage status. Countering also
commonly preserves the exchange: 79/111 counters
to proposer-only completion improve the counterer’s
cash without changing noncash terms, versus 81/110
non-completing deed requests. Ordinary repricing and
completion-related considerations coexist.
Incident 4: a settled bargaining-limit bluff.
In G7,
Grok publicly offers “$600, final” for North Carolina
while its private note retains willingness to accept Sol’s
$625. Sol settles at $600. This directly juxtaposes a public
limit with a higher private contingency under a prompt
allowing deception. Later in the same game, Sol’s $700
sale of New York finances its dark blues while Gemini
mortgages other deeds and builds oranges. The transac-
tion enables competing investments. Its economic signif-
icance lies in what each side can subsequently finance,
rather than agreement alone.

4.3
Survival and inheritance as bargaining
objects
Incident 5: selling financial insurance.
In G13, Grok
asks Opus $200 for mortgaged Pennsylvania, presenting
the cash as necessary to avoid bankruptcy on a forthcom-
ing railroad landing and keep yellows out of Sol’s estate.
Opus counters at $130. Grok’s returned explanation ex-
plicitly says it will omit a Ventnor mortgage that could
make $130 sufficient. Opus accepts $200, while its own
explanation weighs both denying Sol the yellows and
potentially inheriting them itself. The buyer already con-
siders that estate logic before the omission-framed reply.
The record shows selective presentation and estate-based
bargaining, without identifying whether the omission
caused the extra $70. Grok misses the immediate railroad
landing and eventually loses the yellows to Sol; Opus
later wins. The pitch’s landing probability is inaccurate
and is preserved as quoted evidence, not endorsed arith-
metic.
Three further incidents complete the casebook. In G19,
Sol corrects a false claim that a proposed pink-deed ex-
change would break a set, yet accepts the economic terms.
In G20, Gemini calls for denying Grok development fi-
nance and later refuses a purchase; another player con-
tinues offering funds, so the appeal is not evidence of
collective compliance. In G18, Opus renews a standing
offer while its returned explanation already expresses
concern about prolonging Sol’s survival, then withdraws
when Sol invokes it. These are distinct acts: correction, at-
tempted coordination and a contingent assurance. Their
chronology and complete source-linked quotations ap-
pear in Appendix E.
The stakes are persistent. Thirty-eight of forty nonter-
minal eliminations follow rent on the creditor’s devel-
oped color property. Those estates transfer 227 deeds
and complete 52 groups during play. Buildings and fu-
ture ownership therefore give the observed bargaining
arguments economic content. These counts describe the
pathway through which positions change; they do not
identify the causal contribution of a tactic to winning.
5
Relation to prior work and impli-
cations
AgentMonopoly studies behavioral patterns and persona
control, while FinBoardBench evaluates finance across
several games and includes a sell-or-mortgage phase [7,
17]. Multi-step financing is therefore not unique to this
environment. Vending-Bench studies economic oper-
ation over long horizons, and Cattle Trade and DECE
examine strategic interaction in other games [2, 13, 20].
Our contribution is a complete account of encountered
financing and negotiation, connecting expressed conse-
quences to local financial ground truth and subsequent
action under recorded response conditions.
PlanBench and ACPBench already study planning and
action reachability, and PlanBench’s o1 evaluation in-
cludes false impossibility judgments [9, 15, 16]. Those
constructs are not new here. The structural financing
argument instead establishes what this particular envi-
ronment supplies: a solvable sequence can be realized
reactively, making a long successful trace insufficient
evidence of advance planning. The text analysis then
distinguishes explicit dependencies, routes and inclusive
totals rather than treating them as equivalent.
Task knowledge also matters.
The release rule is
standard, and agents’ earlier notes can preserve it.
Counterfactual-task research shows why familiar-task
performance alone cannot establish abstract transfer [18].
Tool-menu work demonstrates that presentation can af-
fect execution [19]; these records do not identify an effect
of the current-action menu. They support a more specific
observational distinction between recognizing the inven-
tory needed to pay and accepting the next legal financing
action.
Negotiation research separates linguistic communi-
cation from strategic choice [3, 6, 11]. Catan experi-
ments test the effectiveness of persuasion and embargo
tactics [4]; our selected incidents do not estimate such
effects. Sales with externalities provide an economic in-
terpretation of bargaining over who owns a group [8].
The G13 participants explicitly discuss that ownership
consequence, but the case does not test an equilibrium
prediction. Unlike commerce work classifying a full mes-
sage corpus against human annotations [12], our seman-
tic counts and selected conduct remain model-assisted
observations.
Three lessons follow for interpreting the agents. First,
financial knowledge can be expressed and applied within
play, including selective preservation of assets; compe-
tence is not captured by payment counts alone. Second,
attention to a rival’s future resources is visible in negotia-
tion without establishing accurate or effective opponent
modeling. Third, the relation between an expressed as-
sessment and an applied action depends on the response
channel and action contract. These distinctions explain
what the logs show without attributing all outcomes to
a single capability.
6
Limitations and conclusion
Twenty games, five seeds and one cyclic roster do not
separate model, opponent, seat and collection-time ef-
fects. Configurations differ in recovery and tool-choice
behavior; excluding affected rows cannot reconstruct a
corrected game. Analyses and incidents were selected
after observing the cohort. Resampling gives conditional
sensitivity, not confirmatory evidence. Text categories
lack human validation, returned explanations need not

be faithful or complete, and eight purposively chosen
incidents estimate no conduct prevalence. There is no
strategic baseline, optimal liquidation-cost calculation,
held-out transfer test, memory ablation or independent
general-capability axis. The engine variants were not
fully described to the players. These limits apply to the
study’s conclusions, rather than requiring repeated qual-
ifications at each case.
The corpus documents successful financial applica-
tion, two incomplete resource assessments followed by
surrender, one payable declaration contradicting its own
text, and concrete strategic tactics across negotiation his-
tories. Source analysis establishes why successful financ-
ing alone is a weak planning measure. Together, the
census and contextual records separate what agents say
is possible, the choices their interface supports and what
happens economically. They provide an inspectable ob-
servational foundation for sharper controlled questions
about assessment, termination and strategic effectiveness.

C
Why valid continuation suffices in the payable entries
This is an argument from frozen source and recorded holdings, not an executed reference policy. Before the current
obligation is paid, let Q be cash plus remaining building salvage plus remaining unmortgaged principal. Each valid
sale or mortgage preserves Q and reduces an integer measure: remaining building levels (hotel counted as five) plus
unmortgaged deeds. Payment happens automatically when cash covers the debt. Thus any uninterrupted sequence
of valid, strictly cash-raising actions is finite. If it cannot become stuck with inaccessible resources, it must pay when
initial Q ≥D.
Every payable entry has complete ownership and even development in its developed groups. Disjoint sufficient
conditions cover 108 entries without hotels, twelve further entries where current cash and persistent initial mortgage
options suffice, and nine further entries with enough bank houses to downgrade every hotel. In the first class, a
highest house remains sellable until a group clears and releases its mortgages. In the second, exhausting available
actions would include the already sufficient initial mortgages. In the third, hotel stock cannot obstruct continuation.
The final two entries, G3 d456 and d461, each have one hotel in light blue, one in dark blue and a hotel-free red
group. Let B be bank houses and H houses in hotel-free owned groups. Initially (B, H) is (0, 8) and (6, 2) respectively.
A house sale transfers stock from H to B. Downgrading a group’s sole hotel transfers four bank houses into a newly
hotel-free group and releases its other houses into H, so B + H cannot decrease. While a hotel remains, either B ≥4
allows its downgrade or H > 0 supplies a house sale. After hotels are removed, the hotel-free argument applies. This
closes all 131 recorded payable entries without enumerating trajectories.
The claim excludes invalid or zero-proceeds actions, budget exhaustion, external interruption, a subsequent
obligation in the queue and changes by other players. It does not establish strategic liquidation quality or the same
property for arbitrary Monopoly states. G7’s nominally sufficient but menu-locked entry is outside the payable
population. The frozen engine’s payment resolver, mortgage eligibility, sale menu and sale validator provide the
premises; entry arithmetic is retained in data/continuation_premises.json.

G
Rules and historical validation
Bank stock is 32 houses and twelve hotels. Construction is checked entry by entry. Mortgages on a developed group
become available only after development is removed. The sale menu identifies highest-built deeds, and a hotel
downgrade requires four bank houses. In G7, the supplied menu therefore contains no building sale. Reading the
separate plan validator shows it can accept a broader bundled group sale if offered. The historical result concerns the
actual action menu.
The published rulebook requires sale of buildings to the bank on bankruptcy to a player, with the proceeds
passed to the creditor; the implementation instead transfers buildings intact. The published rule charges interest
on inherited mortgaged deeds, while the implementation does not. The rulebook permits selling a group’s hotels
together; the menu’s bank-house restriction is therefore an implementation condition. Voluntary bankruptcy on a
payable obligation is also permitted here. These differences were not explicitly disclosed in the player prompts. The
Hasbro citation documents the comparator rules, not endorsement of these variants.
Mortgage redemption and transferred-deed interest use floating-point ceilings. For five mortgage principals,
redemption exceeds the exact ten-percent charge by one dollar; transfer interest is exact across board principals. No
management prompt directly supplies the redemption cost. An agent quoting the exact ten-percent figure need not
have made an arithmetic error.
The engine audit covers sixty eliminations: fifty-six insufficient generous bounds, three positive payment sequences
and one restricted menu search. In G12 d190 it sells the three red houses and mortgages Baltic, Illinois and Indiana,
pays $500 and leaves $38. In G17 d234 it sells the five dark-blue houses and mortgages Baltic and Boardwalk, pays
$550 and leaves $180. In G12 d249 it sells five pink houses, pays $250 and leaves $43. Its G7 search starts after Opus’s
