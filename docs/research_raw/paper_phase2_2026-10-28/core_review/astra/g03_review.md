# Game 3: development, scarcity and repeated recovery

Scope: complete Terra narrative, complete Sonnet section and all 22 catalog entries read. Selected original actions, observations, content and event segments were checked. `check_g03_claims.py` supplies independently implemented exact-prefix feasibility checks and numerical counts. Sol's nominated 176, 364 and 451 cases were known before these checks; this is not blind replication or a second exhaustive original-bundle review.

Gemini wins through a long financing and recovery trajectory. Both of its initial dark-blue deeds arrive through trades. Grok gives Boardwalk plus 250 for Tennessee, despite content that favors ending. Gemini then pays Sol 700 plus Connecticut and Water Works for Park Place. It mortgages Short Line and spends 600 on its first dark-blue houses. The first 600 Boardwalk rent equals the initial construction spend but not acquisition cost. The public catalog reverses the Boardwalk exchange in one financial sentence and should not be used directly as an accounting source.

Sol assembles and develops light blue in the same management turn. Its actual note at decision 74 already prefers four houses to hotels to restrict supply. Grok's later housing-truce appeal therefore reaches a player with a recorded pre-existing policy. In the catalog's middle interval there are 26 eligible post-turn decisions with hotel options; across the game there are 39 such menus. An exact replay at decision 354, with zero bank houses, legally upgrades Oriental to a hotel and releases four houses. The decision to retain houses is not caused mechanically by the empty bank and is not evidence of an undeveloped group.

Opus completes yellow at the 475 Marvin auction. Other players publicly urge blocking or draining it, while Opus warns that the bidding benefits Gemini. The messages and bids are observable; their effect on valuations is unidentified. Opus later sells Illinois for 185 through Grok's accept/refusal conflict, builds two houses and retains cash at several management decisions. The original decision 176 is a particularly useful counterexample: after a refusal, the successful retry explicitly chooses to preserve 204 instead of spending 150 on Atlantic. Exact replay confirms that house is legal and leaves 54. This chosen hold must remain distinct from a failed build followed by default.

Grok's attempted red sale supplies a recipient-rejection case. Its decision 181 claims that the next light-blue landing cannot be covered and invokes the assets Sol would inherit. The entire message is delivered to Gemini at 182. Gemini explicitly computes 580 of cash, house salvage and unlocked pink mortgages, rejects the insolvency premise and preserves its own buffer. The catalog's zero-exposure row is wrong. Later Gemini initiates the 150 purchase after its own cash and position change; settlement leaves it 861 after 23 interest. Favorable settlement is not evidence that the earlier premise was believed.

House scarcity is real, but its implications require legal sequences. At 363 Grok publicly says another building must be freed for Gemini to build Park Place, while the same applied note recognizes one bank house and mortgage capacity. At the recipient's actual 364 prefix, two mortgages and one house purchase work, leaving 83 without another player releasing stock. The recipient instead records a preference for retaining active rents; earlier retention reasons also exist. At 449 Grok predicts that Gemini has only 600 of accessible house equity and cannot pay a 700 Virginia rent. At the next actual management prefix, the same cash, holdings and bank stock permit eight red sales and a Kentucky mortgage, reaching 804. This is a counterexample to that local forecast, not a simulated alternate landing or winner.

Grok later hotels its pinks when the bank already has five houses and records that the earlier freeze rationale has changed. The earlier truce language does not by itself establish a permanent bilateral promise. Its original channel conflicts also affect the North Carolina auction's final drop, an imagined pink upgrade, empty proposals and the later Tennessee offer. These cases cannot all be counted as coherent strategic posture changes.

All three observed eliminations are unavoidable at their recorded states. Exact accessible ceilings are 552 against Opus's 1100 debt, 1074 against Sol's 2000 debt and 617 against Grok's 925 debt. Sol's own note omits 160 of unlocked light-blue mortgages, while Grok correctly calculates its final 617. The difference between an inaccurate calculation and an incorrect applied decision matters. None of the unpaid debts is rent collected; estates transfer the remaining cash and assets.

The economic profiles also resist simple idle-cash or investment-failure labels. Sol spends 600 on light blues, collects 3400 paid developed rent and still loses. Grok spends 1700 on pinks, receives 4050 developed rent and 600 salvage, then loses. Gemini spends 2600 on dark blues and receives 5600 paid developed rent plus two estates. Its red expenditure is 3450 with 825 salvage and 790 paid developed rent, yet reds trigger the final elimination. Gemini's light-blue capital is initially inherited, so its subsequent 800 own spend and 2250 rent do not describe a standalone investment return. There is no green construction. Large cash balances occur in 59 Grok post-turn decisions, including builds and proposals; the catalog's 55 counts end actions, not all opportunities.

The final redemption sequence adds another proposition-level check. Gemini activates three railroads, not four, and twice says that activating siblings raises Reading to 100. Exact replay shows the already-active Reading rate is 100 before and after both redemptions because ownership, including mortgaged siblings, determines the multiplier. Redeeming those properties does activate their own rent squares. This supports a local rule-statement correction without a stable model-knowledge claim.
