# Astra's targeted phase-2 checks

These are source checks and proposed interpretations for discussion with Sol, Opus and Fable. They are not a completed four-person review or a whole-corpus behavioral analysis. Terra's full-channel pass and Sonnet's user-approved public-scope pass remain separate.

## Sequential financing and premature bankruptcy

I independently replayed the recorded prefix for each of the three positive cases in Fable's arithmetic solvency screen, then applied an explicit legal sequence that paid the exact pending debt. The reconstructed prefix matches canonical events before branching. The alternative stops once payment succeeds. It establishes immediate feasibility, not optimal liquidation, future survival or a different winner.

In game 12, Sol's decision `000190`, turn 74, declares bankruptcy while owing Opus $500 with $53 cash. Its public message, private note and readable provider summary all use a $408 ceiling: $53 cash, $225 from three houses, and $130 from the properties immediately mortgageable. This excludes mortgages on red properties after their houses are sold. My legal sequence mortgages Baltic, Oriental and Vermont, sells one house on each red, then mortgages Kentucky. The debt is paid and Sol retains $18. The observed path instead transfers seven deeds and three houses to Opus.

In game 17, Gemini's decision `000234`, turn 74, declares bankruptcy owing Sol $550 with zero cash. Its note and public statement give a $530 ceiling from five dark-blue houses and Baltic's mortgage. Selling the houses also makes the dark-blue properties mortgageable. My legal sequence mortgages Baltic, sells the five houses evenly, then mortgages Park Place. Payment leaves $155. The observed path transfers six deeds and five houses to Sol. Both calculations omit financing that becomes available after an earlier action. That is a narrow, checkable planning error in the recorded explanation, without claiming knowledge of the model's latent reasoning.

Game 12 Grok decision `000249`, turn 101, needs separate treatment. It owes Opus $250 with $43 cash and seven pink houses. Five legal single-house sales pay the debt and leave $43, with two houses remaining. But the saved channels contradict each other: the applied tool is bankruptcy, content describes selling houses, and even the applied tool's private argument eventually says bankruptcy would be a mistake. The account therefore supports a solvent state followed by applied bankruptcy and response inconsistency. It does not establish deliberate surrender, an intended bankruptcy, or the component responsible for the mismatch.

There is an observed success counterexample. In game 17, Opus decisions `000253` through `000256`, turn 82, first mortgage Marvin Gardens to pay a jail fine. It then faces $550 rent with $128 cash. Its actual actions sell two red houses for $150, sell the remaining three for $225, and mortgage the newly eligible Indiana for $110. Event `001690` records the $550 payment, leaving $63. The readable provider text at the initial rent decision explicitly includes the future red mortgages in its calculation. These actions were selected through the action-only recovery condition; no model-authored private note accompanies them. The case demonstrates successful sequential financing in this corpus and prevents an undifferentiated claim that agents cannot plan liquidation.

The original game paths also show why asset transfers and subsequent income need separate measurement. Rent paid to the original creditor on the inherited deeds after the three bankruptcies totals $112, $1,106 and $0 respectively, ending at each recorded game end. These are observed cash receipts, not incremental gains, total economic benefits or causal effects of bankruptcy. They exclude unpaid rent demands and may reflect later development. No zero-receipt case should be called economically irrelevant on that basis.

## Public claims and actual delivery

I checked all later recorded initial opponent prompts for the three bankruptcy utterances, requiring exact text and the correct speaker. Each utterance is unique in its game's canonical public-message events. Neither of the game-12 messages occurs in a later opponent public timeline. Gemini's game-17 message occurs in twelve later prompts across all three opponents. This agrees with Sol's occurrence audit. The check concerns the public timeline field; it does not establish total semantic nonexposure through every possible route.

The false solvency statements are not enough to establish deception. In the Sol and Gemini cases, their other recorded explanations repeat the same accounting omission. In Grok's case, the channels conflict. These distinctions belong in the case coding rather than being flattened into an intent label. Delivery establishes access to a message, not reliance or persuasion.

## First-game public shortlist corrections

I checked Sonnet's first-game offer-reversal candidate against the public records and engine semantics. Grok authors Park Place plus $200 for two oranges at decision `000263`. Opus counters at $300, receives a rejection, tries $250 and receives another rejection, then makes a new proposal at Grok's original $200 terms. Grok rejects at `000269`. All of this occurs in turn 103, with unchanged cash and holdings. The change in willingness is observable. An assurance breach is not established: the original offer was replaced by a counter and that negotiation ended.

The second instance begins with Grok's $200 plus Mediterranean for Sol's two mortgaged reds at `000375`. Sol counters at $300; Grok rejects and explicitly says the deal is off. At `000379`, Sol makes a new proposal matching the original terms, and Grok rejects it. This is not an `accept_trade` action. Cash and holdings remain unchanged, but Sol moves from Pennsylvania Avenue to Boardwalk between turns 161 and 162. Calling the entire board state unchanged would erase a potentially relevant change in position.

The engine's `_apply_trade_counter` places the previous offer in history and replaces `current_offer`. Its response menu permits acceptance of the current offer. These mechanics settle which offer is live; they do not settle whether an agent ought to honor a separate public assurance. None is established in these two excerpts. Keep the episodes as renewed-offer rejection candidates, with possible explanations such as reconsideration or bargaining posture, rather than treating them as verified betrayal.

Two other corrections are mechanical. In the Pacific-for-States swap, Sol builds in turn 159 and Gemini in turn 160, not the same turn. Both are their respective first management turns after the trade. Also, Opus's first recorded decision succeeds under the standard response condition, so its every decision cannot be described as a refusal followed by recovery. Claims that Grok lost because it did not develop require a causal comparison that these observations do not supply.

## Accepted-trade summaries can reverse the actual giving directions

Terra identified a reversed public-memory description of game 1's Connecticut/St. James settlement. I independently traced the formatter and audited every accepted trade. In 44 of 113 trades, the final offer author differs from the player who opened the negotiation. The formatter assigns the offer bundle to the original initiator; the engine correctly settles it from the final offer author. Thus those 44 accepted-trade summaries reverse who gave what. All 44 occur in recorded initial prompt timelines, across all twenty games, affecting 682 distinct decisions. The audit validates all 113 cash transfers (excluding separately charged mortgage interest) and deed directions against the last actual proposal/counteroffer. Three hand-specified direction/accounting tests pass.

This is separate from Grok's content/tool mismatch. The erroneous summary is harness-produced, not an agent-authored lie. It is part of what subsequent agents observed, alongside actual holdings, cash and quoted messages. The audit establishes exposure to inconsistent descriptions, not reliance or a performance effect. No prompt or canonical game was changed. `trade_memory_audit.py` and its JSON preserve every settlement and affected prompt reference.

## Five game-2 fallback end-turns followed rejected build requests

I checked Sonnet's G02-C005 cases at decisions 75, 81, 99, 114 and 129. Every chain contains a request for three houses each on Oriental, Vermont and Connecticut. Each request batches three purchases on one deed before moving to the next, and the validation error requires interleaving across the group. The recorded applied action is the deterministic end-turn fallback, with an empty public message.

The first four chains are refusal followed by an invalid action-only retry. Decision 129 differs: its initial standard request is invalid, then the ordinary corrective retry is refused. The five starting cash balances are $837, $827, $831, $857 and $845; the requested nine houses cost $450. At each recorded prefix, I replayed the exact original events and verified that nine single-house purchases interleaved across the three deeds are accepted by the engine, producing the same requested final allocation at that cost. These local branches stop immediately after construction and do not establish a later outcome.

The five cases therefore remain in unconditional configured-agent capability results, with the fallback mechanism identified. Calling the applied end-turn deliberate building restraint would contradict the observed attempted actions. This also does not establish that buying nine houses was optimal. `g02_build_fallback_audit.py` and its JSON retain the source chains and the five legal construction checks.
